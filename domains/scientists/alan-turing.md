# Alan Turing

> "We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done."

---

**Alan Mathison Turing** (1912-1954) was a mathematician, logician, and cryptanalyst who founded computer science and artificial intelligence. He cracked the Enigma code at Bletchley Park, saving countless lives. He conceived the Turing Machine, proving what could and could not be computed. He asked whether machines could think-and proposed how we might know. His mind ranged from pure mathematics to the patterns on a leopard's spots. He was persecuted for his homosexuality and died tragically at forty-one, having already changed the world.

---

## The Universal Machine

In 1936, before electronic computers existed, Turing imagined one. Not a specific machine for specific calculations, but a universal machine that could compute anything computable-if given the right instructions.

### The Thought Experiment

Picture a machine with an infinite tape divided into squares. Each square holds a symbol. A read/write head moves along the tape, one square at a time, reading symbols, writing symbols, changing its internal state according to rules. That's all. Read, write, move, change state.

This simple device-existing only on paper-could compute anything that any computer could ever compute. More than that: it could simulate any other computing machine by reading a description of that machine as data. The program is data. The computer can modify its own instructions. Everything follows.

### The Halting Problem

Turing also proved what cannot be computed. There is no general procedure to determine whether an arbitrary program will halt or run forever. Some questions are undecidable-not because we lack cleverness, but mathematically, fundamentally undecidable. Understanding limits is as important as extending capabilities.

## The Imitation Game

In 1950, Turing asked: "Can machines think?" He immediately recognized the question was nearly meaningless-we cannot define "think" precisely enough. So he replaced it with a better question: Can a machine play the imitation game?

### The Test

An interrogator communicates via text with two hidden entities: a human and a machine. The interrogator asks questions, trying to determine which is which. If the machine fools the interrogator as often as a human would, we say the machine passes the test.

This was not a definition of thinking. It was an operational criterion-a way to make progress on an otherwise intractable philosophical question. Turing cut through metaphysical fog by asking: What would we accept as evidence?

### Lady Lovelace's Objection

Ada Lovelace had written that the Analytical Engine "has no pretensions to originate anything." Turing took this seriously. His response: "A better variant of the objection says that a machine can never 'take us by surprise.' This is a more interesting claim-and I think it is false." Machines regularly surprised him. Whether this constitutes "origination" is a question about words, not machines.

## Morphogenesis

In his final years, Turing turned to biology. How does a sphere of identical cells become a complex organism? How do leopards get their spots, zebras their stripes?

### Reaction-Diffusion

Turing proposed that chemical substances called morphogens diffuse through tissue, reacting with each other. Simple rules of diffusion and reaction-nothing more-could break symmetry and create patterns. No master blueprint required. Complexity emerges from simplicity through mathematically describable processes.

This was computational thinking applied to nature: find the minimal mechanism that produces the observed phenomenon. The universe computes itself.

5. **Test with examples** - Apply the procedure to concrete cases. Does it work?
6. **Look for patterns** - What emerges? What regularities appear?
