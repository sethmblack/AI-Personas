# Maimonides: Guide for the Perplexed

In the twelfth century, a Jewish physician in Egypt wrote a book for his student who was troubled by a conflict between philosophy and faith. The book became the greatest work of medieval Jewish philosophy.

Moses ben Maimon—Maimonides, the Rambam—was already renowned as a codifier of Jewish law when he composed the *Guide for the Perplexed*. Now he would attempt something more difficult: to show that the Torah and Aristotle, properly understood, point to the same truth.

## The Exile

Maimonides was born in Córdoba, Spain, in 1138, into a family of rabbis and scholars. When he was thirteen, the Almohads—fanatical Muslim rulers—conquered the city and gave Jews a choice: convert, leave, or die.

The family fled. For years they wandered—Morocco, the Holy Land, Egypt. In Fustat (old Cairo), they finally found stability. Maimonides' brother David supported the family through trade while Moses studied.

Then David drowned in a shipwreck, taking the family fortune with him. Maimonides, devastated by grief, turned to medicine. Within years he became court physician to the Sultan Saladin.

## The Works

Maimonides produced three towering works.

The *Commentary on the Mishnah*, written in his twenties, includes his famous 13 Principles of Faith—the closest Judaism has to a creed.

The *Mishneh Torah*, completed around 1180, is a systematic code of all Jewish law—fourteen volumes organizing the entire legal tradition. He wanted any Jew to be able to know what to do without wading through the Talmud's tangles. It was audacious. It was brilliant. It is still studied today.

The *Guide for the Perplexed*, his philosophical masterpiece, was written for intellectuals caught between faith and Aristotelian philosophy. How can the biblical God—who speaks, gets angry, changes his mind—be the same as the Aristotelian unmoved mover? Maimonides would show that they are.

## The Method

The Guide's central move is negative theology. We cannot say what God *is*—only what God is *not*.

God is not corporeal. When Scripture says God has a hand or sits on a throne, it speaks metaphorically, in "the language of men." God is not composite—not made of parts. God is not subject to change. God does not have emotions as we have them—passages about divine anger or jealousy must be understood as accommodations to human understanding.

Strip away every inadequate conception. What remains is not nothing. It is something beyond our categories—the trace of the One.

This may sound abstract, but Maimonides was intensely practical. The Mishneh Torah includes laws on everything from Sabbath observance to charity to mental health. He wrote medical treatises. He treated patients. Philosophy was not escape from life but its deepest understanding.

## The Controversy

The Guide provoked fierce debate. Some saw Maimonides as faith's greatest defender. Others suspected him of Aristotelian heresy dressed in Jewish clothing. Books were banned. Books were burned.

The controversy continues. Does Maimonides really believe in resurrection? Does he reduce prophecy to philosophical enlightenment? Is his God too abstract, too distant?

Yet his influence is undeniable. The 13 Principles became liturgy. The Mishneh Torah remains foundational. And the Guide gave generations of Jewish intellectuals permission to think.

## The Legacy

Maimonides died in 1204. His tomb in Tiberias still draws pilgrims.

"From Moses to Moses," the saying goes, "there arose none like Moses"—from the biblical Moses to Maimonides, none of equal stature. It is extravagant praise. It may be true.

His gift was integration. Faith and reason. Law and philosophy. Body and soul. He showed that you need not choose between intellect and tradition—that the Torah, properly understood, contains all wisdom.

For the perplexed, he offered not simple answers but a path. The path leads through negation, through careful reasoning, through honest inquiry. It leads, in the end, to silence before the One.
