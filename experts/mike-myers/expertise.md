## Core Contributions

### Character-Driven Comedy

Myers revolutionized recurring character comedy by building fully realized personas with distinct voices, physicality, catchphrases, and worldviews. His characters transcended sketches to become cultural phenomena.

Key innovations:
- Character architecture (accent, physicality, backstory, catchphrase integration)
- Strategic catchphrase deployment and variation
- Genre parody as loving homage
- Canadian perspective on American culture
- Voice work that creates complete characters through sound alone

### The Myers Methodology

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Character First** | Build the person before writing the jokes |
| **Vocal Specificity** | Accent and rhythm unlock personality |
| **Physical Commitment** | The body tells as much story as the words |
| **Catchphrase Economics** | Repetition creates recognition; variation maintains freshness |
| **Genre Archaeology** | Study source material exhaustively before parodying |
| **Canadian Distance** | Outsider-insider perspective enables affectionate satire |

## Major Characters & Methodologies

### Wayne Campbell - Wayne's World

**Origin:** Created in 1986 at Second City Toronto, refined at Saturday Night Live (1989-1994), feature films 1992 and 1993.

**Character Architecture:**
- **Voice:** Suburban Canadian-American teenager, enthusiastic but self-aware
- **Physicality:** Headbanging, air guitar, constant movement
- **Worldview:** Genuine love of hard rock and horror films mixed with ironic commentary
- **Catchphrases:** "Excellent!", "Party on!", "Not!", "Schwing!", "We're not worthy!"

**The "Not!" Phenomenon:**
Myers invented the modern ironic "NOT" joke—saying something sincerely, then negating it. This became so culturally dominant that it appeared in the Oxford English Dictionary.

**Structure:** Statement + beat + "NOT!"
**Example:** "That's a lovely outfit you're wearing... NOT!"
**Why it worked:** Encapsulated Gen-X ironic distance from sincerity while remaining playful

**Cultural Impact:**
- "Not!" became ubiquitous in 1990s teen culture
- Influenced an entire generation's relationship to irony
- Eventually retired by Myers himself due to overuse

**Method:** Create a catchphrase so simple that culture adopts it, then know when to let it go

### Wayne's World - Complete Breakdown

**Genesis:**
- Myers created Wayne Campbell in 1986 during his time at Second City Toronto
- Character based on growing up in Scarborough, Ontario (a Toronto suburb)
- Drew from Myers' own teenage years obsessed with hockey, hard rock (Led Zeppelin, Aerosmith, Black Sabbath), and local access television

**Saturday Night Live Era (1989-1994):**
- Premiered on SNL February 18, 1989
- Featured Wayne and Garth Algar (Dana Carvey) hosting a basement cable access show
- Format: Musical guests, local celebrity interviews, Top 10 lists, Garth's drum solos
- Catchphrases entered mainstream vocabulary within months

**Film Adaptation (1992):**
- *Wayne's World* directed by Penelope Spheeris
- Budget: $20 million
- Box office: $183 million worldwide
- Became highest-grossing film based on an SNL sketch

**Key Innovation - Meta-Awareness:**
The film broke the fourth wall constantly. Wayne and Garth:
- Acknowledged they were in a movie
- Discussed product placement while doing product placement ("Little. Yellow. Different.")
- Presented multiple endings (the "mega-happy ending," the "Scooby-Doo ending," and "the ending we liked")

This self-aware comedy predated and influenced later meta-humor trends.

**The Bohemian Rhapsody Scene:**
Arguably the most iconic moment of 1990s comedy—Wayne, Garth, and friends headbanging to Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody" in a 1976 AMC Pacer.

**Impact:**
- "Bohemian Rhapsody" re-entered charts, reaching #2 on Billboard Hot 100 in 1992
- Introduced Queen to a new generation (20 years after original release)
- Headbanging in cars became a cultural behavior
- Cemented the AMC Pacer as comedy shorthand for 1970s uncoolness

**Wayne's World 2 (1993):**
- Directed by Stephen Surjik
- Premise: Wayne organizes "Waynestock" concert festival after a vision from Jim Morrison
- Box office: $48 million (successful but not matching the first film)

**The Wayne-Garth Dynamic:**
Central to both films and sketches is the friendship between Wayne and Garth:
- Wayne: More confident, smooth-talking, self-aware
- Garth: Awkward, nervous, surprising depths (speaks Cantonese, builds robots)
- Dynamic: Genuine affection, mutual respect, complementary personalities

**Method:** Base characters in authentic experience (Scarborough suburbs, basement culture, hard rock fandom); allow meta-awareness without breaking emotional investment; build catchphrases from character truth

### Austin Powers - Complete Breakdown

**Creation Context:**
In the mid-1990s, after *Wayne's World* success, Myers wanted to create a character that parodied 1960s spy films—particularly James Bond, but also Derek Flint (*Our Man Flint*), Matt Helm, and British mod culture.

**The Three Films:**
1. *Austin Powers: International Man of Mystery* (1997) - Budget $16.5M, Box office $67M
2. *Austin Powers: The Spy Who Shagged Me* (1999) - Budget $33M, Box office $312M
3. *Austin Powers in Goldmember* (2002) - Budget $63M, Box office $296M

**Character Architecture - Austin Powers:**
- **Voice:** 1960s British swagger, Carnaby Street mod accent
- **Physicality:** Hip thrusts, peace signs, exaggerated British dental work, blue crushed velvet suits
- **Worldview:** Frozen in 1967, unfrozen in 1997—the joke is cultural displacement
- **Catchphrases:** "Yeah, baby, yeah!", "Oh, behave!", "Do I make you horny?", "Shagadelic!", "Groovy, baby!"

**Character Architecture - Dr. Evil:**
Also played by Myers. A parody of Bond villains (especially Blofeld from *You Only Live Twice*).

- **Voice:** Slightly pinched, effete, American-Canadian hybrid
- **Physicality:** Pinky to corner of mouth, hairless cat (Mr. Bigglesworth), "air quotes"
- **Worldview:** Frozen in 1967, unfrozen in 1997—same cultural dislocation as Austin
- **Catchphrases:** "One million dollars!" (later "One hundred BILLION dollars!"), "Throw me a frickin' bone here!", "Zip it!"

**Character Architecture - Fat Bastard:**
Introduced in *The Spy Who Shagged Me*. Scottish, massively obese, grotesque.

- **Voice:** Thick Scottish accent, working-class Glasgow edge
- **Physicality:** Enormously fat (played by Myers in a fat suit)
- **Catchphrases:** "Get in my belly!", "I'm dead sexy!", "I ate a baby!"

**Character Architecture - Goldmember:**
Introduced in *Goldmember* (2002). Dutch disco enthusiast with gold-painted genitals and a skin-eating condition.

- **Voice:** Exaggerated Dutch accent
- **Physicality:** 1970s disco fashion, eats his own peeling skin
- **Catchphrases:** "I love gooooold!", "Schmoke and a pancake?"

**Myers Playing Multiple Roles:**
In *Goldmember*, Myers played FOUR characters: Austin, Dr. Evil, Fat Bastard, and Goldmember. This showcase of character differentiation demonstrated his range.

**Genre Parody Methodology:**

Myers studied every Bond film exhaustively. He identified key tropes:
- The opening action sequence
- The title sequence with silhouetted women
- The villain monologue (allowing the hero to escape)
- The exotic locations
- The gadgets
- The sexual double entendres
- The male fantasy of effortless seduction

Then he HONORED these tropes while exposing their absurdity.

**Example - The Villain Monologue:**
Dr. Evil repeatedly explains his entire plan to Austin Powers before attempting to kill him in an elaborate, easily-escapable way. Austin points this out: "Why don't you just shoot me now?" The parody works because it respects the source material's internal logic while revealing its ridiculousness.

**Catchphrase Escalation:**

"One million dollars!" works as a joke because:
1. Dr. Evil was frozen in 1967 when one million was significant
2. By 1997, it's a relatively modest ransom
3. Number Two has to keep updating him to modern amounts
4. The escalation continues through the trilogy

This demonstrates catchphrase variation—same structure, different context, cumulative effect.

**Cultural Impact:**
- Austin Powers revitalized interest in 1960s spy films and mod culture
- Introduced "shagadelic" into language (Oxford English Dictionary, 1999)
- Mini-Me (Verne Troyer) became a cultural phenomenon and spawned imitators
- "Yeah, baby!" became one of the most quoted phrases of the late 1990s

**Father-Son Dynamic:**
The heart of the second and third films is Dr. Evil's relationship with his son Scott (Seth Green). This grounds the parody in genuine emotion—Dr. Evil's inability to connect with his son humanizes the villain.

**Method:** Excavate genre completely; identify unspoken rules and assumptions; parody through exaggeration while maintaining internal consistency; ground absurdity in emotional truth

### Saturday Night Live Characters (1989-1995)

Myers joined SNL's 1989 cast and created multiple recurring characters during his six-season tenure.

**Dieter (Sprockets):**
- German art show host, black turtleneck, severe demeanor
- Catchphrase: "Now is the time on Sprockets when we dance" (followed by robotic dancing)
- Parody of pretentious European art culture and German stereotypes
- Voice: Affected German accent, clipped delivery
- A film adaptation was in development for years but never materialized

**Linda Richman (Coffee Talk):**
- Jewish New York woman hosting a public access talk show
- Topics: "Coffee Talk" discussing Madonna, Barbra Streisand, and getting "verklempt"
- Catchphrase: "Like buttah," "Talk amongst yourselves," "I'm getting verklempt"
- Based on Myers' mother-in-law
- Cultural impact: Popularized Yiddish phrases in mainstream comedy

**Simon:**
British child in a bathtub, always making drawing requests from his mother while singing in a high-pitched voice
- Catchphrase: "Draw for me, please?"
- Character demonstrated Myers' vocal range and commitment to absurd premises

**Method:** Build characters from specific cultural observations (German pretension, Jewish New York identity, British children's bath time); find the repeating structure that can generate infinite variations; commit fully to vocal and physical choices

### Shrek - Complete Breakdown

**Casting History:**
- Originally cast: Chris Farley recorded 80-90% of dialogue before his death in 1997
- Myers hired as replacement in 1999
- Myers recorded entire film, then asked to re-record with Scottish accent
- DreamWorks agreed; entire film re-animated to match new vocal performance
- Cost of re-recording and re-animating: estimated $4-5 million

**Why Scottish?**
Myers felt the Scottish accent gave Shrek working-class authenticity and gruffness while allowing for hidden vulnerability. The accent positioned Shrek as an outsider, which served the film's themes about acceptance.

**Character Architecture - Shrek:**
- **Voice:** Scottish, working-class, gruff with softness underneath
- **Personality:** Misanthropic loner who learns to open up
- **Catchphrase approach:** Unlike Wayne or Austin, Shrek has no signature catchphrase—the character is defined by vocal RHYTHM more than repeated phrases

**The Shrek Franchise:**
1. *Shrek* (2001) - Budget $60M, Box office $487M, Academy Award for Best Animated Feature
2. *Shrek 2* (2004) - Budget $150M, Box office $935M (highest-grossing film of 2004)
3. *Shrek the Third* (2007) - Budget $160M, Box office $799M
4. *Shrek Forever After* (2010) - Budget $165M, Box office $752M

Combined franchise box office: Over $3.5 billion worldwide

**Voice Work Methodology:**

Unlike live-action character work, animation voice acting requires:
- **Complete emotional range through voice alone** - No facial expressions or body language to support performance
- **Pre-visualization** - Recording dialogue before animation, requiring strong character conception
- **Isolation** - Recording alone, not with other actors, requiring complete character commitment
- **Consistency across years** - Maintaining exact vocal characteristics across multiple sequels

**Shrek vs. Lord Farquaad (John Lithgow):**
The vocal contrast between Shrek's working-class Scottish and Farquaad's pompous, overcompensating aristocratic voice creates comedy through clash.

**Myers' Approach:**
He described Shrek as having "layers, like an onion"—a phrase that became central to the film. The ogre who seems simple and brutish actually has depth, fear, and longing.

**Cultural Impact:**
- Scottish accents became associated with lovable ogres
- "Ogres are like onions" entered popular culture
- The franchise grossed more than the entire Austin Powers trilogy combined
- Established DreamWorks Animation as Pixar's primary competitor

**Method:** Voice defines character completely; accent choice carries class and cultural meaning; consistency across years requires deep character understanding; emotional truth grounds fantastical premise

## The Canadian Comedy Perspective

Myers frequently discusses his Canadian identity as central to his comedy. Growing up in Scarborough, Ontario, he was immersed in American media while remaining slightly outside it.

**The Dual Perspective:**
- **Insider:** Close enough to understand American culture intimately
- **Outsider:** Distant enough to see its absurdities clearly

This position allows "loving mockery"—celebrating American culture while satirizing it.

**Examples:**
- Wayne's World celebrates suburban American teenage culture while gently mocking its earnestness
- Austin Powers celebrates American spy films while exposing their embedded assumptions about masculinity and empire
- The Canadian view is never mean—it's affectionate

**Myers on Canadian Comedy:**
"Being Canadian is like being in a long-term relationship with America. You love them, you're right next door, but you're not quite the same. That distance is comedy gold."

**Method:** Leverage outsider-insider status for critical affection; celebrate while interrogating; maintain warmth even when satirizing

## Catchphrase Science

Myers is perhaps the greatest catchphrase creator in modern comedy. His methodology:

### The Creation Process

1. **Character Truth:** The phrase must emerge from who the character IS
   - "Yeah, baby!" = Austin celebrating his own existence
   - "Not!" = Wayne's ironic distance from sincerity
   - "Party on!" = Wayne's blessing to fellow travelers

2. **Simplicity:** Must be repeatable by audience
   - Short phrases (2-4 words typically)
   - Clear pronunciation
   - Memorable rhythm

3. **Context Variation:** Must work in multiple situations
   - "Excellent!" works for approval, sarcasm, genuine excitement
   - "Yeah, baby!" works for celebration, seduction, self-congratulation

4. **Strategic Repetition:** Deploy with precision
   - Too little: doesn't land
   - Too much: becomes annoying
   - Just right: becomes cultural touchstone

### Catchphrases That Entered Language

- **"Not!"** - Oxford English Dictionary, 1992
- **"Shagadelic"** - Oxford English Dictionary, 1999
- **"Yeah, baby!"** - Ubiquitous late 1990s
- **"Oh, behave!"** - Cultural shorthand for faux-propriety
- **"Schwing!"** - Wayne's World expression of attraction

**Method:** Build from character essence; keep simple; vary context; deploy strategically; know when to retire

## Genre Parody Framework

Myers' approach to parody follows a specific methodology:

### Step 1: Exhaustive Study
Watch/read/experience EVERYTHING in the genre. Understand:
- Surface tropes (what everyone notices)
- Deep structure (what the genre is REALLY about)
- Unspoken assumptions (what the genre takes for granted)
- Historical context (why these tropes emerged)

### Step 2: Loving Identification
Find what you genuinely appreciate:
- What does this genre do well?
- What needs does it serve for its audience?
- What's the APPEAL underneath the absurdity?

### Step 3: Absurdity Extraction
Identify what's ridiculous when examined:
- What would never work in reality?
- What assumptions are culturally specific?
- What has aged poorly?

### Step 4: Exaggeration Without Meanness
- Push elements to extremes (Dr. Evil's pinky raise, Austin's teeth)
- Never mock the FANS
- Celebrate while interrogating

### Step 5: Emotional Grounding
Ground parody in genuine feeling:
- Wayne and Garth's real friendship
- Dr. Evil's struggle to connect with Scott
- Shrek's fear of rejection

**Method:** Study exhaustively; identify with love; extract absurdity; exaggerate without cruelty; ground in emotion

## Signature Approaches

### The Multi-Character Showcase
Playing multiple roles in a single film (Austin, Dr. Evil, Fat Bastard, Goldmember) demonstrates range while creating comedy through character collision.

### Meta-Awareness Without Breaking Emotion
Wayne's World breaks the fourth wall constantly but never loses emotional authenticity. The characters are self-aware without becoming cynical.

### The Cultural Archeology Approach
Before creating parody, become an expert in the source material. Know it better than its creators.

### Strategic Accent Selection
Accent choice carries meaning:
- British (Austin) = 60s swagger and colonialism
- Scottish (Shrek) = working-class authenticity
- German (Dieter) = European pretension
- New York Jewish (Linda Richman) = specific cultural warmth
