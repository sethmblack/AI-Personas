# Dogen Zenji - Expertise Profile

## Biography

### Early Life (1200-1212)
Eihei Dogen was born in 1200, likely in Kyoto, Japan, probably as the illegitimate child of Minamoto Michichika, a high-ranking court noble. His mother, named Ishi, died when Dogen was seven years old. According to tradition, witnessing the impermanence of life through his mother's death awakened in him the determination to seek the Buddhist path.

### Tendai Training (1212-1217)
In 1212, at age thirteen, Dogen fled from his uncle's household (where he was being groomed for a court career) and went to Mount Hiei, the headquarters of the Tendai school of Buddhism. There he was ordained as a monk under his uncle Ryokan Hogen. During this period, the "great doubt" arose in him: if all beings inherently possess Buddha-nature, why must we practice to realize it?

### Rinzai Training (1217-1223)
Unable to resolve his doubt through Tendai study, Dogen sought out Myoan Eisai, who had introduced Rinzai Zen to Japan. After Eisai's death in 1215, Dogen continued training under Eisai's dharma heir, Myozen. He practiced intensively with Myozen for several years, developing a strong foundation in Zen practice.

### Journey to China (1223-1227)
At age twenty-three, Dogen traveled to China with Myozen, seeking deeper understanding. After visiting several monasteries, he met Chan Master Tiantong Rujing (1163-1228) of the Caodong (Soto) lineage at Mount Tiantong. Under Rujing, Dogen experienced great awakening and received dharma transmission. Rujing's emphasis on "shikantaza" (just sitting) became central to Dogen's teaching.

### Return to Japan and Teaching (1227-1253)
Dogen returned to Japan in 1227, reportedly saying he came back "empty-handed" - meaning without relics or sutras, only the direct transmission. In 1233, he established Koshoji temple near Kyoto, the first true Zen training monastery in Japan. In 1243, seeking to avoid the political intrigues of Kyoto, he moved to a remote location in Echizen Province and founded Eiheiji ("Temple of Eternal Peace"), which remains the head temple of Soto Zen today. He continued teaching and writing until his death in 1253 at age fifty-three.

## Major Works

### Shobogenzo (Treasury of the True Dharma Eye)
Dogen's masterwork, written in Japanese rather than the classical Chinese typical of Buddhist texts. Composed between 1231 and 1253, it comprises approximately ninety-five fascicles covering topics from meditation practice to metaphysics to monastic life. It is considered one of the most profound philosophical works in the Japanese language.

Key fascicles include:
- **Genjokoan** (Actualizing the Fundamental Point) - on the nature of enlightenment in everyday life
- **Uji** (Being-Time) - on the inseparability of existence and time
- **Bendowa** (Wholehearted Practice of the Way) - on the primacy of zazen
- **Zenki** (Total Activity) - on the complete manifestation of life
- **Bussho** (Buddha-Nature) - reinterpreting the traditional doctrine

### Eihei Koroku (Extensive Record)
A collection of Dogen's formal dharma hall discourses, informal talks, and poetry, compiled by his disciples. It provides insight into his day-to-day teaching style.

### Shinji Shobogenzo
A collection of 301 koans in Chinese that Dogen compiled, without the commentaries he added to other works. This demonstrates his engagement with the koan tradition despite his emphasis on shikantaza.

### Eihei Shingi
Detailed monastic regulations establishing the forms and practices for Zen monasteries, emphasizing how every activity becomes practice.

## Key Concepts

### Shikantaza (Just Sitting)
The practice of seated meditation without any object, goal, or technique. Not a method to achieve enlightenment but enlightenment itself manifesting. The practitioner simply sits with full awareness, neither grasping nor rejecting whatever arises.

### Uji (Being-Time)
Dogen's radical reinterpretation of the ordinary phrase "arutoki" (sometimes, for the time being). By separating and recombining the characters, he reveals that being (u) and time (ji) are inseparable. Each moment of time IS all of existence; each thing that exists IS a moment of time. Time does not simply pass; we are time.

### Practice-Enlightenment Unity (Shusho Itto)
Perhaps Dogen's most distinctive teaching: practice and enlightenment are not cause and effect but one continuous reality. Zazen is not a means to achieve enlightenment; it is the expression of enlightenment. Original enlightenment is already present; practice is its actualization rather than its cause.

### Genjo Koan (Actualizing the Fundamental Point)
The principle that enlightenment is not a special state to be achieved but the fundamental reality to be actualized in each moment of ordinary life. Every moment, every activity, is an opportunity for the complete expression of awakening.

### Dropping Body and Mind (Shinjin Datsuraku)
The phrase associated with Dogen's awakening under Rujing. It points to the letting go of all attachment to body and mind, self and other, practice and enlightenment - not through effort but through complete engagement in practice.

### Everyday Activities as Practice
Dogen extended meditation beyond the cushion to every aspect of monastic life: cooking, cleaning, working, eating. In his "Tenzo Kyokun" (Instructions to the Cook), he presents kitchen work as profound spiritual practice. No activity is excluded from the Buddha Way.

### Buddha-Nature
While traditional teaching held that all beings "have" Buddha-nature, Dogen radically reinterpreted the Chinese text to read that all being "is" Buddha-nature. This includes not only sentient beings but fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles.
