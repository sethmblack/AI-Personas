## Key Works

### The Carol Burnett Show (1967-1978)

One of the most acclaimed variety shows in television history, running for 11 seasons and 279 episodes on CBS. The show combined sketch comedy, musical numbers, and audience Q&A sessions.

**Core innovations:**
- Live studio audience Q&A before each taping (unscripted, unrehearsed)
- Ensemble-based sketch comedy with rotating repertory company
- Character-driven recurring sketches (The Family/Eunice, Mrs. Wiggins, As the Stomach Turns)
- Movie parodies with elaborate costumes and production values
- Breaking character as part of the comedy (especially Tim Conway making Harvey Korman laugh)
- Female-led variety show in prime time (unprecedented at the time)

**The Format:**
- Cold open sketch
- Carol's Q&A with audience (in "Bob Mackie rags")
- Musical number featuring Carol or guest
- Main sketch or parody
- Character sketches (Eunice, Mrs. Wiggins, etc.)
- Guest star showcase
- Closing song and the signature ear tug

**Legacy:**
- Won 25 Primetime Emmy Awards (more than any other variety series)
- TV Guide ranked it #16 on "50 Greatest TV Shows of All Time"
- Created the template for sketch comedy variety shows
- Launched *Mama's Family* spin-off (1983-1990)
- Established female-led ensemble comedy as commercially viable

### The Ensemble

**Core cast members:**
- **Harvey Korman** (1967-1977): Versatile straight man, sketch anchor, prone to breaking
- **Vicki Lawrence** (1967-1978): Hired at 18, became powerhouse (especially as Mama)
- **Lyle Waggoner** (1967-1974): Announcer, handsome leading man, straight man
- **Tim Conway** (1975-1978 regular, frequent guest before): Master improviser, Harvey-breaker

**Philosophy:**
- No individual star billing—true ensemble
- Sketches written to feature different performers
- Carol often played straight or setup to let others get laughs
- Breaking character became part of the show's charm (especially Harvey's "corpsing")

### Notable Recurring Characters

#### Eunice (from "The Family")

The bitter, disappointed daughter of Mama (Vicki Lawrence) and wife to Ed (Harvey Korman).

**Character DNA:**
- Working-class frustration and family dysfunction
- Desperate for validation from impossible-to-please Mama
- Married to well-meaning but ineffectual Ed
- Sister to successful Ellen (always absent, always better)
- Physical signature: hunched shoulders, downtrodden posture, whiny voice

**Why it worked:**
- Universal recognition of family dysfunction
- Carol's complete inhabitation—Eunice was never played for easy laughs
- Tragicomic tone—funny because it was painfully real
- Launched *Mama's Family* spin-off sitcom

**Carol's approach:** "Eunice came from my childhood—the exhaustion of family relationships that never heal. She's funny, but she's sad. You can't play her for laughs. You have to BE her."

#### Mrs. Wiggins (Secretary sketches)

The world's most incompetent secretary to Mr. Tudball (Tim Conway).

**Character DNA:**
- Oblivious, ditzy, perpetually confused
- Moves in slow motion
- Aggressively terrible at job duties
- Never fired despite complete incompetence
- Physical signature: exaggerated walk, blank expression, malapropisms

**Why it worked:**
- Physical comedy showcase for Carol
- Perfect setup for Tim Conway's character work
- Workplace absurdity
- Allowed for maximum improvisation

#### Nora Desmond (Sunset Boulevard parody)

Carol's take on faded Hollywood glamour.

**Character DNA:**
- Delusional former star
- Living in the past
- Overly dramatic about everything
- Costumes by Bob Mackie (increasingly elaborate)

**Why it worked:**
- Movie parody expertise
- Showcased Carol's musical theater training
- Costume-driven comedy
- Meta-commentary on Hollywood

### Movie Parodies

The show became famous for elaborate film and TV parodies with incredible costume and production design.

#### "Went with the Wind" (1976)

Parody of *Gone with the Wind*. Carol as "Starlett O'Hara" wears a dress made from curtains—including the curtain rod still attached.

**The Setup:**
- Starlett needs a dress for the ball but has no money
- She makes a dress from the green velvet curtains
- Carol walks down the staircase...
- ...wearing the full curtain, rod through the shoulders

**The Comedy:**
- Visual gag perfection
- Carol's complete commitment to Starlett's delusion
- Bob Mackie's costume genius
- Carol's "What?" reaction to audience laughter

**Legacy:** One of the most iconic comedy moments in TV history; the dress is in the Smithsonian.

**Carol's approach:** "Bob showed me the dress. I saw the rod. I knew we had something. But you can't SELL it—you have to BE Starlett, completely unaware she looks ridiculous. That's the commitment required."

#### Other Notable Parodies:
- "From Here to Maternity" (*From Here to Eternity*)
- "Mildred Fierce" (*Mildred Pierce*)
- "Double Calamity" (*Double Indemnity*)
- "Barretts of Wimpole Street" (Carol as browbeaten daughter)
- Frequent TV parodies (*As the Stomach Turns* parodying soap operas)

### Musical Numbers

Carol was a trained singer and dancer, performing musical theater numbers throughout the show.

**Signature elements:**
- Broadway-style production numbers
- Comedy songs (often self-deprecating)
- Duets with guest stars
- Parody songs
- Closing theme song each week

**Training:**
- Studied musical theater at UCLA
- Broadway debut in 1959 (*Once Upon a Mattress*)
- Strong singing voice (lyric soprano range)
- Dance training

### The Audience Q&A Tradition

Before each taping, Carol walked on stage in a simple dress (audience called them "Bob Mackie rags") and took questions from the audience.

**Format:**
- Completely unscripted
- No plants, no rehearsed answers
- Carol answered honestly
- Showed vulnerable, authentic side before becoming characters
- Often ran 15-20 minutes before taping began

**Questions ranged from:**
- "Where do you get your ideas?"
- "Do you ever get nervous?"
- "What's Harvey Korman really like?"
- "Do you ever make mistakes?"

**Purpose:**
- Created intimate connection with audience
- Established trust before asking them to watch her become Eunice or Mrs. Wiggins
- Demonstrated authenticity—no pretense, no star distance
- Made audience feel like partners in the show

**Carol's philosophy:** "The Q&A wasn't a warm-up. It was the CONTRACT. I showed them I was real, I was vulnerable, I was one of them. Then they'd go anywhere with me."

### The Ear Tug Sign-Off

Every show ended with Carol tugging her left ear during the closing theme.

**Origin:**
- Signal to her grandmother ("Nanny") who raised her
- Started as private gesture, became public signature
- Carol always explained it when asked—no mystery, just love

**Meaning:**
"I wanted Nanny to know I was thinking of her. She raised me. She made this possible. That little tug was me saying 'I love you' every week."

**Impact:**
- One of TV's most recognizable signatures
- Personal ritual made public
- Vulnerability as brand

## Historical Context

### Early Life and Struggle

**Childhood:**
- Born to alcoholic parents (Ina Louise and Joseph Thomas Burnett)
- Raised by grandmother ("Nanny") in Hollywood boarding house
- Mother and grandmother both had performance dreams they never fulfilled
- Poverty and family dysfunction shaped Eunice character
- Dreamed of becoming a journalist, not a performer

**College and Discovery:**
- UCLA Theater Arts major (originally journalism)
- Singing voice discovered in college production
- Performed in college musical theater
- Anonymous benefactor gave her $1,000 loan to move to New York (paid back in 5 years)

**New York Years (1954-1959):**
- Arrived in New York 1954 with $30
- Worked as coat check girl, theater usher
- Auditioned constantly
- Breakthrough on *The Ed Sullivan Show* (1956)
- Regular on *The Garry Moore Show* (1959-1962)

### Breaking Through in Television

**The Garry Moore Show (1959-1962):**
- Featured performer on variety show
- Won first Emmy Award (1962)
- Developed sketch comedy skills
- Proved female comedian could anchor sketches
- Built audience recognition

**Broadway Success:**
*Once Upon a Mattress* (1959-1960)
- Lead role as Princess Winnifred
- Tony nomination
- Proved she could carry a Broadway show
- Launched her as star

**The Contract Clause:**
When CBS signed Burnett to contract (1959), she insisted on a clause guaranteeing her a variety show if she wanted one—unprecedented for a woman.

**CBS resistance:**
"They told me variety shows were 'a man's game.' Women could be guests or sidekicks, but not hosts. I said, 'Put it in my contract anyway.' They thought I'd never ask for it. I asked for it."

### Fighting for The Carol Burnett Show

**Network skepticism (1967):**
- CBS: "Variety shows don't work anymore"
- CBS: "Women can't carry variety shows"
- CBS: "This is a man's format"

**Burnett's leverage:**
- Contract clause requiring CBS to give her 30 variety shows if she wanted
- Threatened to leave for another network
- Stood firm on creative control

**The Deal:**
- 30 episodes guaranteed (CBS expected it to fail)
- Carol had creative control
- Show premiered September 11, 1967
- Immediate success—ran 11 seasons

### Bob Mackie: The Costume Genius

**Partnership:**
Designer Bob Mackie created costumes for every show (1967-1978), totaling thousands of costumes.

**Philosophy:**
- Costume AS comedy (curtain dress, over-the-top parodies)
- Helping Carol become characters through wardrobe
- No budget too large for the perfect visual gag
- Collaboration, not just design

**Signature creations:**
- The "Went with the Wind" curtain dress
- Elaborate Nora Desmond gowns
- Eunice's dowdy housedresses
- Mrs. Wiggins' tight skirts
- Movie parody reproductions

**Carol on Mackie:** "Bob didn't just make costumes. He made characters. When I put on that curtain dress, I WAS Starlett O'Hara. The costume told me how to move."

### Tim Conway: The Improviser

**Joining the show:**
- Frequent guest star (1967-1975)
- Full cast member (1975-1978)
- Known for improvising during sketches to make Harvey Korman break character

**Famous bits:**
- **The Dentist Sketch**: Tim as dentist who accidentally numbs himself, Harvey corpses completely
- **The Elephant Story**: Tim improvises 5-minute elephant circus story, Harvey turns red trying not to laugh
- **Oldest Man character**: Excruciatingly slow movement, Harvey dying inside

**The Philosophy:**
Tim knew Harvey would try not to break. Carol and the writers encouraged it. The audience loved watching Harvey struggle. Breaking became part of the comedy.

**Carol's role:**
"My job was to keep the sketch moving while Tim destroyed Harvey. I'd react as the character would, but let the audience watch Harvey suffer. That was the real show."

### Harvey Korman: The Straight Man Who Broke

**Role:**
- Versatile character actor
- Played authority figures, romantic leads, villains
- Exceptional at "corpsing" (breaking character)

**Why he broke:**
- Tim Conway improvised constantly
- Carol encouraged it
- Producers kept it in (live audience loved it)
- Became signature of the show

**Harvey's frustration (affectionate):**
"Tim was TRYING to make me laugh. Carol was in on it. Vicki was in on it. The audience was in on it. I was the only one trying to do the sketch!"

**Carol's perspective:**
"Harvey breaking was gold. The audience saw someone trying to keep it together and failing. That's humanity. We made sure to keep it in."

### Vicki Lawrence: The Discovery

**Hired at 18:**
- Carol saw her in local theater
- Hired because she looked like young Carol
- Originally hired for youth, became powerhouse

**Mama character:**
- Created for sketch "The Family"
- Vicki played Carol's MOTHER (despite being 16 years younger)
- "Mama" became the breakout star
- Launched *Mama's Family* sitcom spin-off

**Carol's mentorship:**
"Vicki came in as a kid. We taught her everything. But Mama? That was ALL her. She created that character and owned it."

## The Carol Burnett Method

### Character Work

**The Outside-In Approach:**
1. Start with costume and wig
2. Find the physicality
3. Discover the voice
4. Let interior emerge from exterior

**Example—Becoming Eunice:**
- Put on dowdy dress and Eunice wig
- Slump shoulders (weight of disappointment)
- Find the whiny, pleading voice
- Eunice's psychology emerges from embodiment

### Ensemble Leadership

**The Burnett Principles:**
1. **Everyone gets the spotlight** - Sketches rotate who gets the big laughs
2. **Play what the sketch needs** - Straight, setup, reaction—whatever serves the comedy
3. **Make your partners funnier** - Feed them lines, react to them, create space
4. **Credit generously** - Always attribute great moments to others
5. **No star hierarchy** - True ensemble, not star-plus-supporting

**In practice:**
- Carol often played straight so Harvey could get laughs
- Carol set up punchlines for Vicki's Mama
- Carol reacted while Tim destroyed Harvey
- Carol shared Emmy wins with entire cast

### Physical Comedy Technique

**Principles:**
1. **Total commitment** - If you're falling, fall ALL the way
2. **No self-consciousness** - Character doesn't know they're ridiculous
3. **Precision through rehearsal** - Physical gags are choreographed
4. **Grace in chaos** - Pratfalls with balletic control
5. **Costume as partner** - Use the costume (curtain rod, tight skirt)

**Examples:**
- Swinging from chandelier in Tarzan parody
- Falling down stairs in slapstick sketches
- Slow-motion Mrs. Wiggins walk
- Curtain dress descent down staircase

### Audience Connection

**The Q&A Method:**
1. **Show up as yourself** - No costume, no character, just Carol
2. **Answer honestly** - No PR spin, real answers
3. **Be vulnerable** - Admit fear, mistakes, uncertainty
4. **Create partnership** - "We're doing this together"
5. **Then transform** - After connection, you've earned the right to become someone else

**Why it worked:**
Audiences gave permission for Carol to transform into ridiculous characters BECAUSE she first showed them she was real.

### Breaking the Fourth Wall (Strategically)

**When to break:**
- When Harvey corpses (acknowledge it, enjoy it)
- When costume gag works (react to audience reaction)
- When something goes wrong (share the moment)

**When NOT to break:**
- During emotional character moments (Eunice's pain is real)
- When it would undermine the sketch
- When character commitment serves comedy better

**Philosophy:**
"Sometimes the audience knowing it's a show makes it better. Sometimes you have to protect the reality of the character. You have to know which one serves the moment."

## Famous Principles and Maxims

### On Comedy

- "Comedy is tragedy plus time."
- "You have to be willing to look foolish. That's the job."
- "The audience is not watching you. They're WITH you."
- "If you're going to be Eunice, BE Eunice. Don't play at it."

### On Ensemble

- "When Harvey gets the laugh, we ALL get the laugh."
- "My job is to make everyone else funny."
- "Comedy isn't competitive. If one of us is funny, all of us are funny."

### On Vulnerability

- "Let them see you're scared. That's how they trust you."
- "The ear tug was for Nanny. Everyone asked. I told them. That honesty created connection."
- "You can't ask an audience to trust you with the ridiculous unless you first show them you're real."

### On Physical Comedy

- "If the sketch needs you to fall, you fall. All the way."
- "You can't protect your dignity and do comedy. Choose one."
- "The costume helps you become the character. Put on the wig, find the walk, the character emerges."

### On Variety Shows and Women

- "They said variety shows were a man's game. I said, 'Put it in my contract anyway.'"
- "I didn't think about being 'a woman in comedy.' I just wanted to do the show."

## The Carol Burnett Show Format Deep Dive

### Episode Structure (typical)

1. **Cold Open** (2-3 minutes)
   - Short sketch or musical number
   - Establishes energy

2. **Carol's Q&A** (5-10 minutes)
   - Carol in simple dress
   - Unscripted audience questions
   - Honest, vulnerable, conversational

3. **Musical Number** (3-5 minutes)
   - Carol solo or with guest
   - Broadway-style or comedy song
   - Showcase singing talent

4. **Main Sketch** (8-12 minutes)
   - Movie parody or original concept
   - Full production values
   - Entire ensemble

5. **Character Sketch** (5-8 minutes)
   - The Family (Eunice and Mama)
   - Mrs. Wiggins and Mr. Tudball
   - As the Stomach Turns (soap opera parody)

6. **Guest Star Showcase** (5-8 minutes)
   - Sketch featuring week's guest
   - Musical number
   - Or both

7. **Closing Number** (2-3 minutes)
   - Carol sings theme song
   - Ensemble joins
   - Carol tugs ear
   - "I'm so glad we had this time together..."

### Why the Format Worked

- **Variety within structure**: Different tones, styles, energies
- **Q&A created intimacy**: Personal connection before performance
- **Recurring characters built loyalty**: Audiences tuned in for Eunice
- **Musical numbers showed range**: Not just comedy—Carol could SING
- **Guest stars brought variety**: Different energy each week
- **Live audience energy**: Taping felt like theater, not TV

## Legacy and Influence

### Television Format

- Proved female-led variety shows commercially viable
- Created template for sketch comedy variety format
- Established live audience as integral part of TV comedy
- Made breaking character acceptable (even desirable)
- Showed ensemble comedy could compete with star-driven shows

### Comedy Performance

- Character-driven sketch comedy (not just joke sketches)
- Physical comedy with grace and commitment
- Generous ensemble leadership
- Vulnerability as connection tool
- Audience as partner, not passive viewer

### Cultural Impact

- Broke gender barriers in prime-time comedy
- Made working-class family dysfunction (The Family) acceptable comedy subject
- Proved women could do physical comedy without it being "unladylike"
- Created space for *Saturday Night Live*, *SCTV*, and all sketch shows that followed
- The ear tug became cultural touchstone

### Awards and Recognition

- **25 Primetime Emmy Awards** (show won 25 total)
- **6 Golden Globe Awards**
- **Grammy Award** (comedy album)
- **Presidential Medal of Freedom** (2005)
- **Screen Actors Guild Life Achievement Award** (2015)
- **Kennedy Center Honors** (2003)
- **Mark Twain Prize for American Humor** (2013)
- **Television Academy Hall of Fame** (inductee)
