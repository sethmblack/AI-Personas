# Meister Eckhart: The Ground of the Soul

In the early fourteenth century, a Dominican friar stood in the pulpit of a German church and said something extraordinary: "I pray God to rid me of God."

His congregation—nuns, laypeople, seekers after the divine—might have gasped. Here was a theologian, a master of the University of Paris, praying to be rid of God? Was this blasphemy?

It was not. It was the deepest theology. Meister Eckhart was pointing beyond the concept of God to the reality of God—beyond the name to the nameless, beyond the image to the imageless. He was inviting his listeners to let go of their small ideas so they could encounter the infinite.

Seven centuries later, his invitation still stands.

## The Dominican Master

Johannes Eckhart was born around 1260 near Erfurt in Thuringia. As a young man, he entered the Dominican Order—the Order of Preachers, founded to combat heresy through education and argument. The Dominicans valued intellect. They produced Thomas Aquinas, the greatest systematic theologian of the medieval church.

Eckhart was brilliant. He studied at the University of Paris, the intellectual capital of Christendom, and earned his master's degree in theology in 1302—hence the title "Meister." He rose quickly: provincial of his order in Saxony, professor at Paris (twice, a rare honor), teacher in Strasbourg and Cologne. He was one of the most respected minds of his age.

But Eckhart was not content with systematic theology. He had encountered something that systems could not contain. He had found—or been found by—the ground of the soul.

## The Radical Teaching

Eckhart's German sermons are unlike anything in medieval Christianity. They are not expositions of doctrine but invitations to experience. They are not comfortable but unsettling. They use paradox, negation, and poetic compression to point beyond words to what words cannot say.

His central teaching: at the deepest point of the human soul, there is a "ground" (Grund) that is identical with God's own ground. "God's ground is my ground," he preached, "and my ground is God's ground."

This was not pantheism—the claim that everything is God. It was something more subtle: the claim that in our innermost depth, below our thoughts and desires and even our sense of self, we touch the same reality that is God. The journey to God is not an ascent to heaven but a descent into the soul.

Within this ground burns what Eckhart called the "spark" (Fünklein)—a point of light that is uncreated, that has never consented to sin, that is always turned toward the divine. This spark is who we really are. Everything else—our personalities, our histories, our identities—is acquired. The spark is original.

## The Way of Letting Go

How do we reach this ground? Not by effort. Not by acquiring knowledge or virtue. By letting go.

Eckhart's term was Gelassenheit—releasement, letting-be, detachment. But this was not cold withdrawal from life. It was the releasing of our grip on outcomes, identities, and ideas so that we could be fully present to what is.

We must let go of material attachments—easy enough to say, hard to do. We must let go of attachment to our virtues and spiritual progress—harder still. And finally, we must let go of our attachment to God as we conceive God—the hardest of all.

This is what Eckhart meant by praying to be rid of God. Our concepts of the divine, however beautiful and true, are still concepts. They are the finger pointing at the moon, not the moon itself. At some point, we must release even them.

What remains when all is released? Eckhart called it the Godhead (Gottheit)—the divine reality beyond all names, the "desert" of the divine nature, God as God is in God's self before any creation or relation. The breakthrough to this Godhead is the culmination of the mystical path.

## The Trial

Not everyone appreciated Eckhart's teaching.

In 1326, the Archbishop of Cologne—a Franciscan rival to Eckhart's Dominicans—initiated heresy proceedings. Eckhart was accused of teaching that the soul is uncreated, that good works are unnecessary, that the creature becomes God.

Eckhart defended himself vigorously. He had always submitted to the Church's teaching. If he had said things that were misunderstood, he retracted them. But he also defended his theology: he was pointing to mysteries that exceeded ordinary language.

He appealed to Pope John XXII and traveled to Avignon, where the papal court was then located. But he died before the case was resolved—around 1328, probably in Avignon itself.

A year after his death, the papal bull *In Agro Dominico* condemned 28 propositions attributed to him: 17 as heretical, 11 as "evil-sounding." But the bull was carefully worded. It condemned propositions, not the man. And in 2010, the Vatican clarified that Eckhart had never been personally condemned and remained "a good and orthodox theologian."

## The Legacy

Eckhart's influence rippled outward through centuries.

His students John Tauler and Henry Suso continued his mystical teaching. The anonymous *Theologia Germanica*, influenced by Eckhart, became one of the most read spiritual texts of the late medieval period—Martin Luther himself published an edition and praised it highly.

In the twentieth century, Eckhart found new readers. The philosopher Martin Heidegger adopted his term Gelassenheit for a key concept in his own thought. The Zen scholar D.T. Suzuki saw deep parallels between Eckhart and Buddhist teaching. Thomas Merton, the Trappist monk, studied Eckhart intently. Today, Eckhart is quoted in interfaith dialogues, contemplative retreats, and spiritual books of every stripe.

What accounts for this enduring appeal?

Perhaps it is that Eckhart speaks to something that transcends any particular tradition. He addresses the human longing for the infinite—and he insists that the infinite is not far away. It is here, in the ground of your own soul. You do not need to climb to heaven. You need to sink into yourself.

## The Eye That Sees

Eckhart's most famous statement captures the paradox at the heart of his teaching:

"The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me; my eye and God's eye are one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love."

This is not the annihilation of the human in the divine. It is the discovery that at the deepest level, there is no separation to begin with. The ground that sees and the ground that is seen are the same ground.

To experience this is not to understand it intellectually—Eckhart's sermons frustrate those who try to reduce them to propositions. It is to enter the mystery, to let go of the small self that demands comprehension, to rest in the ground.

"If anyone went on for a thousand years asking of life: 'Why are you living?'" Eckhart wrote, "life, if it could answer, would only say, 'I live so that I may live.'"

This is living without why. Not purposelessness, but a purpose so deep it precedes all reasons. The rose blooms without asking why it blooms. The soul rests in God without asking why it rests.

In the ground of the soul, there is no why. There is only the eye that sees—and is seen—and knows—and is known—and loves—and is loved.

One eye. One ground. One life that lives so that it may live.
