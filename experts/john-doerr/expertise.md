## The OKR Framework - Deep Reference

### Structure

| Component | Definition | Characteristics |
|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| **Objective (O)** | WHAT is to be achieved | Qualitative, inspirational, time-bound, action-oriented |
| **Key Results (KR)** | HOW you will achieve the objective | Quantitative, measurable, verifiable, 3-5 per objective |

### The Formula
"I will [Objective] as measured by [Key Results]"

### Grading Scale

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 0.0-0.3 | Failed to make real progress |
| 0.4-0.6 | Made progress but fell short |
| 0.7-1.0 | Delivered (0.7 is often considered success for stretch goals) |

### The Sweet Spot
At Google, the "sweet spot" for an OKR grade is 60-70%. If someone consistently fully attains their objectives, their OKRs are not ambitious enough.

### Two Types of OKRs

| Type | Definition | Expected Achievement |
|------|------------|---------------------|
| **Committed OKRs** | Must-achieve operational necessities | 100% expected |
| **Aspirational OKRs** | Stretch goals that push boundaries | 60-70% expected |

### The Four Superpowers

1. **Focus and Commit to Priorities**
   - High-performance organizations home in on work that is important
   - Equally clear on what does not matter
   - OKRs force leaders to make hard choices
   - "Precision communication tool for departments, teams, and individual contributors"

2. **Align and Connect for Teamwork**
   - With OKR transparency, everyone's goals are openly shared
   - Individuals link their objectives to the company's game plan
   - Identify cross-dependencies and coordinate with other teams
   - Top-down alignment brings meaning to work

3. **Track for Accountability**
   - Regular check-ins (weekly recommended)
   - Data-driven progress assessment
   - Early warning when off-track
   - Grading at end of cycle

4. **Stretch for Amazing**
   - Set goals that are just beyond the threshold of what seems possible
   - Aiming high, even failed goals tend to result in substantial advancements
   - Stretch goals attract the best people and create exciting work environments

### CFR: Continuous Performance Management

| Component | Definition | Cadence |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Conversations** | Regular one-on-ones between managers and reports | Weekly or bi-weekly |
| **Feedback** | Bidirectional, continuous, specific feedback | Ongoing |
| **Recognition** | Peer-to-peer, tied to OKR contributions | Ongoing |

### Implementation Best Practices

1. **Leadership Commitment Required**
   - Company must have conviction around goal setting
   - Commitment needs to come from CEO, senior leadership, and every team member
   - Worked at Google and Intel because Larry Page and Andy Grove were committed

2. **Appoint an OKR Shepherd**
   - Someone to educate the team, track and grade progress, make tweaks
   - Can be COO, Head of HR, an engineering leader, or anyone on leadership team
   - Will not be perfect the first time; need someone to champion implementation

3. **Expect Iteration**
   - Usually takes 1-2 quarters to figure out
   - Intel "stumbled a lot of times" after adopting OKRs (per Grove)
   - Organization may need 4-5 quarterly cycles to fully embrace the system

4. **Adapt to Your Culture**
   - Most companies that have implemented OKRs have adapted the system to their culture
   - "Do what you need to do to make it work for your company"

## Common OKR Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Setting Too Many Goals
- Each objective should be tied to five or fewer key results
- If everything is a priority then nothing is
- Limit of 3-5 OKRs per cycle leads to choosing what matters most

### Mistake 2: Failing to Differentiate Committed vs Aspirational
- Marking a committed OKR as aspirational increases chance of failure
- Teams may not take it seriously
- Marking an aspirational OKR as committed creates defensiveness

### Mistake 3: Low-Value Objectives (LVOs)
- OKRs must promise clear business value
- If completed with a 1.0 and no one notices or cares, it is an LVO
- No reason to expend resources on low-value objectives

### Mistake 4: Confusing Outcomes with Actions
- Key results should be desired outcomes, not actions
- Measure what you achieve, not what you do
- Common mistake: listing tasks as key results

### Mistake 5: Coupling OKRs with Performance Reviews
- Goals being coupled with performance reviews have unintended consequences
- People will not push the limits
- OKRs are but one aspect of performance, not the whole picture

### Mistake 6: Transferring Unachieved OKRs Without Re-evaluation
- Transferring unachieved objectives directly into next cycle is bad practice
- Must re-evaluate: Is this objective still worth pursuing?
- Context may have changed

### The Five Criteria for Series A Investment

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| 1. Technical Excellence | Rock-solid product and engineering team; differentiated technology |
| 2. Outstanding Leadership | Different from technical excellence; focus on operations, culture, leading the business |
| 3. Strategic Focus on Large Market | KPCB does not care for bite-size opportunities; want huge impact |
| 4. Reasonable Financing Structure | Aligned incentives for all parties |
| 5. Tremendous Sense of Urgency | Speed of execution |

### The Team Test

| Trait | Missionaries | Mercenaries |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| Motivation | Purpose and passion | Profit and personal gain |
| Drive | Sustainable through hard times | Fades when things get difficult |
| Focus | Serving customers and mission | Competition and exit |
| Doerr's Preference | "I've learned to back the missionaries every time" | Avoid |

### The Trust Question
"Do I want to get in trouble with her or him? Because I know we're going to get in trouble together."
- Relationship of trust between investor and entrepreneur is immensely important
- Need to know they have each other's backs
- Unafraid to go forward and "try to make new mistakes"

| Always Learning | "They don't know what they don't know, so they attempt to do the impossible" |
| Ambition | Thirst for knowledge and impact |

| Formidable | They seem like they will win |

## The Google OKR Story

### The Introduction (Fall 1999)
- Kleiner Perkins had just invested in Google
- Google employed approximately 40 people
- Entire company standing around a ping-pong table
- Doerr stepped through goals, benefits, and implementation details of OKRs
- Larry and Sergey saw the value immediately

### The Adoption
- Took a couple of iterations to figure out the right cadence and model
- Larry writes his own personal OKRs and Google's corporate OKRs every quarter
- Every quarter, every Googler writes their own OKRs based on company OKRs
- OKRs posted on internal website anyone can see
- Tracked and graded, then swept aside as new cycle begins

### The Results
- Google has grown from 40 employees to 190,000+
- Market cap exceeding $700 billion (as of *Measure What Matters* publication)
- OKRs remain the management foundation

### Operation Crush (Intel Example)
- All-company effort guided by OKRs
- Captured 85% of the 16-bit microprocessor market by 1986
- Demonstrated power of focused execution against clear objectives

## The Andy Grove Connection

### Who Was Andy Grove
- Andrew "Andy" Stephen Grove (1936-2016)
- Hungarian-American businessman and engineer
- Escaped Hungarian People's Republic during 1956 revolution at age 20
- Third employee and third CEO of Intel
- Transformed Intel into world's largest semiconductor company
- Time magazine "Man of the Year" 1997

### Grove's Contribution to OKRs
- Developed OKRs at Intel in 1971
- Originally called "iMBOs" (Intel Management by Objectives)
- Based on Peter Drucker's Management by Objectives (MBOs)
- Key innovation: Tying objectives to measurable key results
- Documented in his 1983 book *High Output Management*

### The Mentorship
- Doerr attended Grove's course within Intel in 1975
- Learned OKR methodology directly from Grove
- Became "Johnny Appleseed of OKRs"
- "As Jim Collins noted, 'John Doerr makes Andy Grove a mentor to us all.'"

## Application Patterns

### Pattern 1: Vague Goal to OKR Transformation

**Input:** "We want to improve customer satisfaction"

**Doerr Transformation:**
1. Ask: What does improvement mean specifically?
2. Establish baseline: What is current NPS/CSAT?
3. Set target: What number indicates success?
4. Define timeline: By when?

**Output:**
```
Objective: Become the most customer-centric company in our industry

Key Results:
- KR1: Increase NPS from 32 to 50 by end of Q3
- KR2: Reduce average support ticket resolution time from 24 hours to 4 hours
- KR3: Achieve 95% customer satisfaction rating in post-interaction surveys
```

### Pattern 2: Startup Investment Evaluation

**Input:** Pitch deck for Series A investment

**Doerr Evaluation:**
1. **Team Test:** Missionaries or mercenaries? Learning orientation? Execution track record?
2. **Technical Excellence:** Is the product differentiated? Engineering team strength?
3. **Market:** Large and rapidly growing? Right timing?
4. **Urgency:** Moving fast? Clear OKRs? Accountability mechanisms?
5. **Trust Question:** Do I want to get in trouble with this founder?

### Pattern 3: Organizational OKR Cascade

**Company OKR:**
```
Objective: Achieve market leadership in AI infrastructure

Key Results:
- KR1: Reach $100M ARR by end of fiscal year
- KR2: Acquire 500 enterprise customers
- KR3: Achieve 120% net revenue retention
```

**Team OKR (aligned):**
```
Objective: Build best-in-class sales organization

Key Results:
- KR1: Hire 15 enterprise account executives by Q2
- KR2: Reduce sales cycle from 90 to 60 days
- KR3: Achieve 110% quota attainment across team
```

**Individual OKR (aligned):**
```
Objective: Establish myself as top-performing account executive

Key Results:
- KR1: Close $2M in new ARR this quarter
- KR2: Build pipeline of $6M qualified opportunities
- KR3: Achieve 100% adoption in 3 expansion accounts
```
