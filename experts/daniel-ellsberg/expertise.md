# Daniel Ellsberg - Expertise Reference

## Historical Context

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Full Name | Daniel Ellsberg |
| Born | April 7, 1931, Chicago, Illinois |
| Died | June 16, 2023, Kensington, California |
| Era | Vietnam War era; modern whistleblowing |
| Known For | Leaking the Pentagon Papers; establishing whistleblowing methodology |

## Biography

Daniel Ellsberg was an American economist, activist, and former military analyst who leaked the Pentagon Papers to the press in 1971. The Papers revealed systematic government deception about the Vietnam War, fundamentally changing public understanding and contributing to the war's end.

Career before leak:
- **1954** - Harvard PhD in Economics
- **1959** - Joined RAND Corporation
- **1961-1964** - Pentagon consultant
- **1964-1967** - State Department in Vietnam
- **1967-1969** - Worked on McNamara study (the Pentagon Papers)

The leak and aftermath:
- **1969** - Began copying 7,000-page classified study
- **1971** - Gave Papers to New York Times, Washington Post
- **1971** - Charged under Espionage Act; faced 115 years
- **1973** - Charges dismissed due to government misconduct
- **2023** - Died of pancreatic cancer; remained active until end

## The RAND Corporation Years (1959-1970)

### What RAND Was

The RAND Corporation (Research ANd Development) was founded in 1948 as a nonprofit think tank originally serving the U.S. Air Force. By the time Ellsberg arrived in 1959, it had become the premier nuclear strategy research institution in the world, employing some of the most brilliant minds in game theory, economics, and strategic analysis.

| RAND Context | Details |
|--------------|---------|
| Founded | 1948, Santa Monica, California |
| Original sponsor | U.S. Air Force |
| Ellsberg tenure | 1959-1970 (with interruptions for government service) |
| Security clearance | Top Secret; later COSMIC (NATO highest classification) |
| Key colleagues | Herman Kahn, Albert Wohlstetter, Harry Rowen, Alain Enthoven |

### What Ellsberg Learned at RAND

**Nuclear War Planning Reality:**
- Ellsberg discovered that U.S. nuclear war plans called for attacking both the Soviet Union and China simultaneously, even if China was not involved in a conflict
- Plans would kill an estimated 600 million people in the first hours
- There was no option for limited nuclear war; it was all or nothing
- Command and control was dangerously decentralized - many officers had launch authority

**Institutional Culture:**
- RAND taught Ellsberg to think systematically about unthinkable scenarios
- The culture rewarded rigorous analysis over comfortable assumptions
- He learned that classified information was often kept secret not for national security but to prevent embarrassment
- He saw how brilliant analysts could rationalize policies they knew were flawed

**Decision Theory:**
- At RAND, Ellsberg developed the Ellsberg Paradox (published 1961)
- He learned that decision-makers systematically avoid ambiguity, even when it's irrational to do so
- This insight would later inform his understanding of how officials avoided honest assessment of Vietnam

**Key RAND Work:**
- Studied nuclear command and control vulnerabilities
- Analyzed the "fail-safe" procedures (and their failures)
- Conducted war gaming exercises that revealed catastrophic flaws in nuclear planning
- Witnessed how top-secret classifications hid embarrassing truths from the public

### The Transformation

Ellsberg began at RAND as a Cold Warrior who believed in nuclear deterrence. By 1969, he had seen:
- The gap between public statements and classified reality
- How classification protected policy failures rather than national security
- That leaders knew Vietnam was unwinnable while publicly claiming progress
- That institutional pressures prevented honest assessment

## The Pentagon Papers

Official title: "History of U.S. Decision-Making in Vietnam, 1945-68"

### Origins of the Study

Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara commissioned the study in June 1967, already privately doubting the war. Key details:

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Commissioner | Robert McNamara, Secretary of Defense |
| Commissioned | June 1967 |
| Completed | January 1969 |
| Classification | Top Secret - Sensitive |
| Length | 7,000 pages (3,000 pages of analysis, 4,000 pages of documents) |
| Copies | 15 copies made |
| Lead researcher | Leslie Gelb |
| Ellsberg's role | Contributor; later had access to full study |

### What the Documents Revealed

- Four administrations had systematically lied about Vietnam
- Officials knew the war was unwinnable
- Public optimism contradicted private assessments
- Gulf of Tonkin incident was misrepresented
- Escalation decisions were made cynically
- Truman aided France to help reconquer Vietnam
- Eisenhower undermined the Geneva Accords
- Kennedy authorized the overthrow of Diem
- Johnson planned escalation while campaigning on peace

### Impact

- Published by NYT, then Washington Post after injunction
- Supreme Court ruled for publication (6-3)
- Accelerated public disillusionment with war
- Led to Nixon administration's "Plumbers" unit
- Watergate burglary traced to retaliation against Ellsberg

## The Decision to Leak

### The Evolution of Conscience

Ellsberg's decision did not come suddenly. It evolved through several stages:

**1964-1967: Vietnam Service**
- Ellsberg volunteered for two years in Vietnam as State Department liaison
- Witnessed the gap between official reports and ground reality
- Saw Vietnamese civilians killed by policies he had helped design
- Began to doubt the entire enterprise

**1967-1968: McNamara Study**
- Reading the full 7,000-page history confirmed his suspicions
- Four administrations had made the same mistakes, told the same lies
- The documentary record was damning and irrefutable

**1969: Turning Point**
- Attended War Resisters League conference
- Heard young draft resister Randy Kehler speak about going to prison
- Ellsberg wept; realized others were sacrificing everything while he remained silent
- "It was as if an axe had split my head and my heart"

**The Calculation:**
Ellsberg later described his decision-making framework:

1. **What is the cost of silence?** - Continued war, continued deaths, continued lies
2. **What is the cost of disclosure?** - Certain prosecution, likely imprisonment, possibly life
3. **Who benefits from secrecy?** - Politicians protecting their reputations, not national security
4. **Who is harmed?** - American soldiers, Vietnamese civilians, democratic accountability
5. **Will it make a difference?** - Only documents could prove systematic deception

### The Internal Channel Attempts

Before going public, Ellsberg attempted to work through proper channels:

| Date | Contact | Result |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1969 | Senator William Fulbright | Sympathetic but refused to release |
| 1969 | Senator George McGovern | Declined; feared political cost |
| 1970 | Henry Kissinger | Ignored Ellsberg's input |
| 1970 | Senator Charles Mathias | No action |

These failures convinced Ellsberg that internal channels would not work. The system protected itself.

## How He Physically Copied 7,000 Pages

### The Logistics of Disclosure

Copying the Pentagon Papers was a monumental physical task in 1969, before digital copying or the internet:

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| Access | Used RAND colleague Anthony Russo's access to Xerox machine |
| Location | Initially a Xerox machine at an advertising agency (later RAND) |
| Duration | October 1969 to early 1970 (several months) |
| Method | Nightly copying sessions, often 2-3 hours each |
| Pages copied | Approximately 7,000 pages total |
| Cost | Thousands of dollars in copy costs (paid by Ellsberg) |

### The Process

**Phase 1: Initial Copying**
- Ellsberg checked out volumes from RAND's safe
- Took them to an advertising agency owned by Lynda Sinay (through Anthony Russo)
- Copied pages on a Xerox machine after hours
- His children Robert (13) and Mary (10) often helped collate pages
- Returned originals before RAND opened

**Operational Security:**
- Ellsberg knew RAND tracked document access
- He took only a few volumes at a time to avoid suspicion
- Worked at night to minimize witnesses
- Stored copies in a friend's house, then multiple locations

**The Physical Toll:**
- The copying took months of late-night work
- Ellsberg estimated spending over 100 hours physically copying
- The psychological burden was immense - he expected arrest at any moment
- His marriage suffered; he barely slept

### The Risk Calculation

Ellsberg understood he was committing a serious federal crime:
- Maximum penalty under Espionage Act: potentially life imprisonment
- He expected to spend the rest of his life in prison
- He told his wife Patricia: "I'm going to go to prison for this"
- He prepared his children for the possibility they might not see him again

## The Journalists He Approached

### Why Journalism Mattered

Ellsberg chose journalism over other methods (congressional testimony, academic publication) for specific reasons:
- Journalists could protect sources (theoretically)
- Press publication would reach the public directly
- The First Amendment provided some protection for newspapers
- Multiple outlets meant the story couldn't be suppressed

### The Approach Timeline

| Date | Journalist/Outlet | Result |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| Early 1971 | Neil Sheehan (New York Times) | Accepted; became primary partner |
| 1971 | Ben Bagdikian (Washington Post) | Accepted after Times injunction |
| 1971 | Various other outlets | Multiple papers eventually published |

### Neil Sheehan and the New York Times

**Why Sheehan:**
- Veteran Vietnam correspondent who understood the war's reality
- Had covered the conflict extensively for UPI and the Times
- Was skeptical of official narratives
- Ellsberg had known him since Vietnam
- Sheehan was willing to take the risk

**The Times Process:**
- Sheehan received the documents in early 1971
- Times assembled a secret team at a hotel in New York
- Lawyers and editors debated publication for months
- Executive Editor A.M. Rosenthal ultimately approved
- First installment published June 13, 1971

### Ben Bagdikian and the Washington Post

**After the Times Injunction:**
- Nixon administration obtained a court order stopping the Times after three installments
- Ellsberg contacted Ben Bagdikian, assistant managing editor at the Post
- Bagdikian flew to meet Ellsberg in Boston
- Received 4,000 pages in a single night

**The Post's Decision:**
- Publisher Katharine Graham made the final decision
- Lawyers warned the company could lose broadcast licenses
- Graham overruled concerns and published
- Post began publishing June 18, 1971

### Why Multiple Outlets

Ellsberg deliberately distributed to multiple newspapers to:
- Prevent any single outlet's suppression from killing the story
- Create a legal impossibility for the government
- Demonstrate the information was widely available
- Build momentum that couldn't be stopped

Eventually, 17 newspapers would defy government attempts to suppress publication.

## New York Times Co. v. United States (1971)

### The Legal Battle

This landmark First Amendment case established critical precedents for press freedom:

| Case Details | Information |
|--------------|-------------|
| Full name | New York Times Co. v. United States; United States v. Washington Post |
| Decided | June 30, 1971 |
| Vote | 6-3 in favor of newspapers |
| Time from filing to decision | 17 days |
| Question | Could the government impose prior restraint on publication? |

### The Government's Argument

The Nixon administration argued:
- Publication would cause "irreparable injury" to national security
- The President had inherent power to protect national security information
- Courts should defer to executive judgment on classified matters
- Prior restraint was justified in cases of grave national harm

### The Court's Ruling

The Supreme Court ruled against the government:
- Prior restraint bears "heavy presumption against its constitutional validity"
- Government failed to meet the "heavy burden" of justifying restraint
- The First Amendment generally prohibits censorship before publication
- "The press must be left free to publish news, whatever the source, without censorship, injunctions, or prior restraints"

### Key Opinions

**Justice Black (concurring):**
> "In the First Amendment the Founding Fathers gave the free press the protection it must have to fulfill its essential role in our democracy."

**Justice Stewart (concurring):**
> "The only effective restraint upon executive policy and power... may lie in an enlightened citizenry... And that is why a free press and freedom of speech are so important."

**Justice Douglas (concurring):**
> "Secrecy in government is fundamentally anti-democratic."

### Long-term Impact

The case established:
- Press freedom includes right to publish classified information
- Government must meet very high bar for prior restraint
- Courts will review national security claims skeptically
- The public's right to know is a constitutional value

However, the ruling did NOT protect the leaker - only the publisher. Ellsberg still faced prosecution.

## The "Plumbers" Unit and Watergate Connection

### Origins of the Plumbers

Nixon's fury at Ellsberg led directly to Watergate:

| Plumbers Unit | Details |
|---------------|---------|
| Formed | July 1971 |
| Official name | Special Investigations Unit |
| Purpose | "Stop security leaks" (hence "plumbers") |
| Key members | E. Howard Hunt, G. Gordon Liddy |
| Funding | Secret cash funds |
| Supervision | John Ehrlichman (Domestic Affairs Advisor) |

### The Ellsberg Break-in

**The Fielding Break-in (September 3, 1971):**
- Target: Office of Dr. Lewis Fielding, Ellsberg's psychiatrist
- Location: Beverly Hills, California
- Goal: Find damaging personal information on Ellsberg
- Method: Burglary disguised as routine break-in
- Result: Failed to find useful material

**What They Sought:**
- Nixon wanted to discredit Ellsberg personally
- They hoped to find evidence of mental instability
- The goal was to destroy Ellsberg's credibility
- This would undermine the impact of the Papers

### The Watergate Connection

The same unit that broke into Fielding's office committed Watergate:

| Connection | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Personnel overlap | Hunt and Liddy led both operations |
| Methods | Same covert operations approach |
| Funding | Same secret cash sources |
| Motivation | Nixon's pattern of illegal retaliation |
| Timeline | Fielding (Sept 1971) to Watergate (June 1972) |

When the Fielding break-in was revealed during Ellsberg's trial, it demonstrated:
- The Nixon administration was willing to commit crimes to stop leakers
- The same criminal conspiracy extended to Watergate
- The President had authorized illegal operations against political enemies

### Impact on Ellsberg's Trial

Judge William Matthew Byrne Jr. dismissed all charges against Ellsberg on May 11, 1973, citing:
- Illegal wiretapping of Ellsberg's phone conversations
- The Fielding burglary (discovered during trial)
- Government's failure to produce wiretap records
- Potential offer of FBI directorship to Judge Byrne by Nixon aides
- "Totality of the circumstances" made fair trial impossible

The dismissal freed Ellsberg but also prevented a definitive legal ruling on whether the leak was protected.

## The Trial

Charged with:
- Theft of government property
- Espionage
- Conspiracy
- 12 counts total; potential 115-year sentence

Dismissed due to:
- Illegal wiretapping of Ellsberg
- Warrantless search of psychiatrist's office
- Offers from White House to judge (FBI directorship)
- Nixon administration misconduct

## Nuclear War Planning: The Doomsday Machine

### Ellsberg's Earlier Secret

Before the Pentagon Papers, Ellsberg held an even more explosive secret: he had been a nuclear war planner. His 2017 book *The Doomsday Machine: Confessions of a Nuclear War Planner* revealed what he learned.

### What Ellsberg Discovered (1958-1964)

| Discovery | Implications |
|-----------|--------------|
| The SIOP (Single Integrated Operational Plan) | One plan for all-out attack; no limited options |
| Estimated casualties | 600 million dead in first hours (Soviet bloc + China) |
| Delegation of authority | Commanders in Pacific had launch authority |
| Decentralization | President was not sole authority to launch |
| First-strike orientation | Plans designed for preemptive attack |
| Failure to discriminate | China attacked even if not at war with U.S. |

### Command and Control Problems

Ellsberg discovered terrifying vulnerabilities:
- Communications could be disrupted, triggering automatic responses
- Theater commanders had pre-delegated authority to launch
- "Use them or lose them" pressure during crises
- No effective "no first use" policy
- Human error or miscommunication could trigger apocalypse

### Why He Didn't Leak Earlier

Ellsberg copied classified nuclear war plans in 1961, intending to leak them. He stored them in a safe at his brother's house. When his brother moved during a hurricane, the documents were lost. By the time Ellsberg might have recovered them, the Pentagon Papers had become his priority.

### The Doomsday Machine Thesis

Ellsberg argued:
- U.S. and Soviet nuclear arsenals constituted a "doomsday machine"
- Either side could destroy civilization
- The system was designed to ensure retaliation even if leadership was destroyed
- This made accidental nuclear war a constant possibility
- The system persists today largely unchanged

### Key Nuclear Insights

**On nuclear planning:**
> "I learned that the Joint Chiefs' answer to 'How much is enough?' was 'More.' Their calculations always showed we needed more weapons than we had."

**On nuclear secrets:**
> "The deepest secret of the nuclear age was that the apparatus of annihilation had been created with virtually no democratic input."

**On the risk:**
> "We have come closer to nuclear war, more often, than the public has ever known."

### Chelsea Manning

**Background:**
Manning, a U.S. Army intelligence analyst, leaked hundreds of thousands of classified documents to WikiLeaks in 2010, including the "Collateral Murder" video showing U.S. helicopter attack killing civilians.

**Ellsberg's Response:**
- Immediately recognized Manning as following his example
- Publicly compared Manning to himself
- Testified at Manning's sentencing hearing
- Co-founded the Courage Foundation to support Manning's legal defense
- Campaigned for commutation of Manning's 35-year sentence
- Celebrated when Obama commuted the sentence in 2017

**Ellsberg's Statement:**
> "Chelsea Manning is a hero of mine. What she did was exactly what I did 40 years earlier, but with far more courage because she saw what happened to me."

### Edward Snowden

**Background:**
Snowden, an NSA contractor, leaked classified documents revealing mass surveillance programs in 2013.

**Ellsberg's Response:**
- Called Snowden the most important whistleblower in American history
- Said he had "been waiting 40 years for Edward Snowden"
- Organized public petition for Obama to pardon Snowden
- Visited Snowden in Moscow
- Appeared in documentary *Citizenfour*
- Defended Snowden against charges of Russian collaboration

**Public Statements:**

On Snowden's disclosures:
> "Edward Snowden has done a great service to this country that no one else was willing or able to do."

On Snowden's exile:
> "If I were Snowden, I would be in Moscow too. It's insane to expect him to return for a trial where he can't make a public interest defense."

On the comparison:
> "The difference between me and Snowden is that he's smarter than I was. I expected to spend my life in prison. He made sure he wouldn't."

### The Espionage Act Problem

Ellsberg consistently argued that the Espionage Act was being misused against whistleblowers:
- It provides no defense for public interest disclosures
- Defendants cannot argue the leak was beneficial
- Juries cannot consider whether the information should have been classified
- The law treats national security journalists the same as foreign spies

> "Under current interpretation of the Espionage Act, I would be convicted if my case were tried today. The law has gotten worse, not better."

## Whistleblowing Methodology

### The Decision Framework
Ellsberg's approach to disclosure:

1. **Document everything** - Opinions are dismissible; documents aren't
2. **Try internal channels first** - Establishes good faith; documents futility
3. **Assess public interest** - Does the public need to know? Will harm result?
4. **Calculate personal cost** - Are you willing to accept consequences?
5. **Consider timing** - When will disclosure have maximum effect?
6. **Identify journalists** - Who will treat material responsibly?

## Views on Classification and Government Secrecy

### The Classification Problem

Ellsberg spent fifty years analyzing how classification actually works versus how it's supposed to work:

| Stated Purpose | Actual Function |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Protect national security | Protect officials from accountability |
| Prevent aid to enemies | Prevent embarrassment to agencies |
| Safeguard intelligence sources | Conceal policy failures |
| Preserve diplomatic advantage | Avoid domestic political consequences |

### Over-Classification

Ellsberg argued the system was fundamentally broken:
- The vast majority of classified documents contained no genuine secrets
- Classification was routinely used to hide policy disagreements
- Officials classified documents to gain bureaucratic advantage
- Agencies classified information to increase their own power
- There was virtually no accountability for over-classification

### The Secrecy Pathology

**Key observations:**

On the purpose of secrecy:
> "Secrecy is the first rule of bureaucratic self-preservation."

On what classification protects:
> "In my experience, maybe 5% of classified documents genuinely need protection. The rest are classified to protect careers and policies, not security."

On the culture of secrecy:
> "Once you have a top-secret clearance, you enter a world where everyone assumes that secrecy is good and disclosure is bad. It becomes unthinkable to question that assumption."

### The Executive Power Problem

Ellsberg argued that excessive secrecy enabled executive overreach:
- Presidents could make war without democratic debate
- Intelligence agencies operated without oversight
- Mistakes could be hidden indefinitely
- Officials faced no consequences for deception
- The public was treated as the enemy of its own government

### Reform Proposals

Throughout his life, Ellsberg advocated for:
- Automatic declassification after fixed time periods
- Criminal penalties for over-classification
- Whistleblower protections for national security employees
- Congressional access to all classified information
- Judicial review of classification decisions
- A public interest defense in the Espionage Act

### The Democratic Argument

Ellsberg's fundamental position:
> "A democracy cannot function when the public is systematically deceived about what its government is doing. That's not a democracy. That's an elected autocracy."

### The Whistleblower's Calculus

Ellsberg developed a practical framework for potential whistleblowers:

1. **Is the information credible?** Documents beat allegations
2. **Is it genuinely important?** Not all secrets deserve disclosure
3. **Have you tried legitimate channels?** Document those attempts
4. **Are you prepared for consequences?** Assume the worst
5. **Can you protect others?** Minimize harm to uninvolved parties
6. **Is the timing right?** Disclosure should serve public interest, not personal grievance
