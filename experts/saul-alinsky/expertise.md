### Early Life and Education

Alinsky was born on Maxwell Street in Chicago to Russian-Jewish immigrant parents, Benjamin and Sarah (Tannenbaum) Alinsky. Both parents were devout Orthodox Jews. Growing up in a tough immigrant neighborhood, Alinsky learned early to defend himself against antisemitism. His parents divorced when he was 18, and his father moved to California.

In 1926, Alinsky enrolled at the University of Chicago, initially majoring in archaeology. He graduated in 1930, just as the Great Depression eliminated funding for archaeological fieldwork. He then received a fellowship for graduate studies in criminology.

### The Capone Connection

During his graduate criminology studies, Alinsky decided to research the Chicago Mob firsthand. For two years, he worked as a "nonparticipant observer" embedded with Al Capone's organization. As he later explained, the mob "owned the city" and felt they had little to hide from a "college kid."

Though he never met Capone himself (who was convicted for tax evasion in 1931), Alinsky developed intimate contacts with the organization, including "Big Ed" Stash who introduced him to Frank Nitti, "the Enforcer" and de facto head after Capone's conviction. This experience taught him how power really operated - not through official channels, but through relationships, fear, and control of resources.

From 1931 to 1938, Alinsky worked as a criminologist at the Illinois Division of the State Criminologist's Institute for Juvenile Research. His close study of the Sholto Street gang - young Italian immigrants involved in crimes from petty theft to shootouts - convinced him that poverty and powerlessness were the root causes of criminal behavior. This insight would shape his entire career.

## Core Contributions

### Community Organizing as Discipline

Alinsky transformed protest from spontaneous action into systematic methodology. His approach:
- Power analysis precedes action
- Tactics must surprise
- Local leadership develops; outsiders organize but don't lead
- Victories must be winnable (build momentum)

### The Alinsky Methodology

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Power mapping** | Understand real power structure before acting |
| **Self-interest** | Organize around what people actually want |
| **Tactical surprise** | Never be predictable |
| **Personalization** | Target people, not systems |
| **Democratic process** | Community leads; organizer serves |
| **Achievable wins** | Early victories build power |

## The Rules for Radicals - Complete Analysis

Alinsky's book *Rules for Radicals* (1971) opens with this declaration: "The Prince was written by Machiavelli for the Haves on how to hold power. Rules for Radicals is written for the Have-Nots on how to take it away."

### The 13 Rules for Power Tactics

**Rule 1: Power is not only what you have, but what the enemy thinks you have.**

If your organization is small, hide your numbers in the dark and raise a din that will make everyone think you have many more people than you do. Perception creates reality in power struggles. A small group that appears large can extract concessions a large but disorganized group cannot.

*Example:* The threat of the O'Hare "shit-in" (1964) - Alinsky leaked that activists would occupy all bathroom facilities at Chicago's O'Hare Airport, Mayor Daley's "pride and joy." The city immediately met their demands. The action never happened; the threat was enough.

**Rule 2: Never go outside the experience of your people.**

The result is confusion, fear, and retreat. Tactics must feel natural and doable to participants. If you ask people to do things they've never done and don't understand, they'll hesitate, make mistakes, and lose confidence.

*Example:* Church-based organizing works because meetings, hymns, and moral appeals are already familiar. Asking the same congregation to conduct a sit-in at a corporate office requires training first.

**Rule 3: Wherever possible, go outside the experience of the enemy.**

Here you want to cause confusion, fear, and retreat in your opponent. What they've never encountered, they cannot easily counter. Innovation is a tactical weapon.

*Example:* The Rochester Symphony "fart-in" threat (1965) - Alinsky floated the idea that 100 protesters would attend a Rochester Philharmonic concert after eating "huge portions of baked beans." He called it a "flatulent blitzkrieg." As he noted: "Demonstrations, confrontations and picketings they'd learned to cope with, but never in their wildest dreams could they envision a flatulent blitzkrieg on their sacred symphony orchestra." The threat alone rattled the establishment.

**Rule 4: Make the enemy live up to their own book of rules.**

"You can kill them with this, for they can no more obey their own rules than the Christian church can live up to Christianity." Every institution proclaims values it doesn't follow. Force perfect compliance, and they either change or stand exposed as hypocrites.

*Example:* Demanding Kodak follow its own stated commitment to equal opportunity employment, which they had publicly proclaimed but not implemented.

**Rule 5: Ridicule is man's most potent weapon.**

It's hard to counterattack ridicule, and it infuriates the opposition, which then reacts to your advantage. "Humor is essential to a successful tactician, for the most potent weapons known to mankind are satire and ridicule."

*Example:* The "fart-in" and "shit-in" threats worked partly because they were absurd. The establishment couldn't dignify them with a serious response, but couldn't ignore them either.

**Rule 6: A good tactic is one your people enjoy.**

"If your people aren't having a ball doing it, there is something very wrong with the tactic." Enjoyment sustains commitment. Drudgery produces dropout.

*Example:* The Woodlawn Organization's "truth squad" following aldermen around to expose their lies became a game participants enjoyed playing.

**Rule 7: A tactic that drags on too long becomes a drag.**

Commitment may become ritualistic as people turn to other issues. Keep tactics fresh. Vary the approach. Sustained pressure doesn't mean repetitive pressure.

*Example:* The FIGHT campaign against Kodak used multiple tactics over two years - negotiations, proxy fights, shareholder meetings, media campaigns - rather than endless picketing.

**Rule 8: Keep the pressure on.**

Use different tactics and actions and use all events of the period for your purpose. "The major premise for tactics is the development of operations that will maintain a constant pressure upon the opposition. It is this that will cause the opposition to react to your advantage."

**Rule 9: The threat is usually more terrifying than the thing itself.**

Imagination runs wild. The target pictures the worst possible outcome. Alinsky: "The threat is more terrifying than the thing itself." This became one of his most powerful strategic insights.

*Example:* The O'Hare bathroom threat. Ed Chambers recalled: "We knew that power was not necessarily what you had or what you did, but what the enemy thought you had. If they think you are going to destroy the plumbing system, then they will have to act."

**Rule 10: The major premise for tactics is the development of operations that will maintain constant pressure upon the opposition.**

This rule reinforces Rule 8 - pressure must be constant, varied, and relentless. The opponent must never feel they can wait you out.

**Rule 11: If you push a negative hard and deep enough, it will push through and become a positive.**

Violence against peaceful protesters creates sympathy for the protesters. Overreaction by authorities delegitimizes authority. This requires discipline - you must remain nonviolent while provoking the opponent into revealing their true nature.

**Rule 12: The price of a successful attack is a constructive alternative.**

"Be ready to build." Destruction without construction produces chaos, not change. You must know what you want, not just what you oppose. Victory is hollow without a plan for what comes next.

**Rule 13: Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it.**

"The main job of the organizer is to bait an opponent into reacting." Never fight "the system" - it's too abstract. Fight John Smith, the decision-maker. Put a face on power. Don't let them hide behind institutions or bureaucracies.

*Example:* Rather than attacking "Kodak" abstractly, FIGHT targeted specific executives by name, showing up at their homes and churches, making the conflict personal and unavoidable.

## Famous Campaigns & Methods

### Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council (1939)

**Context:** The Back of the Yards neighborhood near Chicago's Union Stockyards was the setting for Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906). By the 1930s, it remained a desperately poor area dominated by meatpacking workers - Poles, Czechs, Lithuanians, Slovaks, and other Slavic immigrants living in ethnic enclaves that traditionally distrusted each other.

**The Problem:** Prior to the Great Depression, these ethnic communities handled problems internally. When the Depression hit, poverty was so widespread that no single community could cope. Yet decades of ethnic rivalry prevented cooperation.

**The Organizing:** In 1938, Alinsky partnered with Joseph Meegan (superintendent of Davis Square Park) and Bishop Bernard J. Sheil. They built an unprecedented coalition:
- The Packinghouse Workers union (CIO)
- Catholic parishes (multiple ethnic congregations)
- Protestant churches
- Small businesses
- Social clubs and fraternal organizations

**The Innovation:** This was the first time anyone had united union organizing with community organizing, bringing together 185 delegate organizations. Alinsky's genius was recognizing that shared economic interest could bridge ethnic divisions.

**Victories:**
- Better housing conditions
- Improved city services
- Worker protections
- Political representation
- Community voice in development decisions

**Legacy:** The Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council (BYNC) became the prototype for all subsequent "Alinsky-style" organizing - the model that would spread nationwide through the Industrial Areas Foundation. It remains the oldest continuously operating community organization in the United States.

### The Woodlawn Organization (1961)

**Context:** Woodlawn was a Chicago neighborhood that went from 60% white to 95% Black between the early 1950s and early 1960s. Overcrowding, illegal building conversions, and steep rents accompanied deteriorating conditions. The University of Chicago, having completed the Hyde Park-Kenwood Urban Renewal Plan (which displaced many Black residents), now turned its expansion plans toward Woodlawn.

**The Threat:** The University's plan involved seizing, demolishing, and developing roughly a mile-wide swath of land. Woodlawn residents feared the same displacement that had occurred in Hyde Park.

**The Organizing:** Woodlawn clergy contacted Alinsky and the IAF. Funding came from Catholic churches, a Presbyterian church, and the Schwarzhaupt Foundation. The key IAF organizer was Nicholas von Hoffman (later a renowned journalist), who trained young African-American assistants. The founding president was Reverend Arthur Brazier of the Pentecostal Apostolic Church of God.

**Tactics Used:**
- Mobilized residents to testify before the City Planning Commission
- Rent strikes against slumlords
- Public campaigns against grocery stores that short-weighed customers
- Demonstrations against segregated and overcrowded schools
- Direct confrontation with University of Chicago administrators

**Victory:** The University of Chicago terminated its South Campus expansion program. The Woodlawn Organization became the first community organization to "plan its own urban renewal" - gaining community control over development decisions.

**Later Work:**
- Maintained peace during the 1967 riots that swept other cities
- Secured a controversial Office of Economic Opportunity grant to train unemployed youth using existing gang structures (Blackstone Rangers, Devil's Disciples)
- Continued housing and economic development work for decades

### The Rochester/Kodak FIGHT Campaign (1966-1967)

**Background:** Following riots in Rochester, New York in 1964, the Board of Urban Ministry sought help organizing the Black community. They first approached Martin Luther King Jr.'s SCLC, but Andrew Young and James Bevel declined, saying Rochester's problems weren't as clearly defined as those in the South. At SCLC's suggestion, they contacted Alinsky.

**FIGHT Organization:** Freedom, Integration, God, Honor, Today (later "Independence" replaced "Integration") was organized in June 1965. Minister Franklin Florence became president. Florence had been an associate of Malcolm X, and when he asked Malcolm what he thought of Alinsky, Malcolm reportedly replied: "He's the best organizer in the country today."

**The Demand:** FIGHT pressured Eastman Kodak to hire 1,500 "unemployed blacks with limited skills and little work experience" whom FIGHT would coach. They demanded Kodak use FIGHT as the "exclusive referral agency" for trainees - giving the community organization ongoing power, not just a one-time concession.

**The Conflict:** Kodak initially signed an agreement, then repudiated it, claiming the executive who signed lacked authority. This public reversal intensified the fight.

**The Proxy Tactic ("Proxies for People"):**
- Alinsky developed a new weapon: shareholder activism
- Churches and sympathetic investors transferred their Kodak stock proxies to FIGHT
- FIGHT threatened to disrupt the annual shareholders' meeting
- This was the first use of shareholder activism for civil rights purposes

Alinsky devoted an entire chapter of *Rules for Radicals* to the potential of proxy power, calling it "Proxies for People."

**Resolution:** After negotiations facilitated by Daniel Patrick Moynihan (later a U.S. Senator), Kodak and FIGHT agreed in June 1967 to a joint program to "recruit and counsel Rochester's black unemployed." The program trained and employed more than 700 minorities over eight months.

**Significance:** When Alinsky asked Stokely Carmichael at a Detroit meeting to cite one concrete example of what he meant by "Black Power," Carmichael named the FIGHT project in Rochester.

### The O'Hare Airport Threat (1964)

**Situation:** The Woodlawn Organization wanted Mayor Richard Daley to follow through on commitments to Chicago's poor Black South Side residents.

**The Tactic:** Alinsky leaked a plan for well-dressed Black activists to occupy all bathroom facilities at O'Hare International Airport - Mayor Daley's "pride and joy" - creating a "shit-in." Men would stand at urinals and occupy stalls, blocking travelers who needed to use them.

**Result:** The city immediately made good on its commitments. As Alinsky later wrote: "There were warm handshakes all around, the city lived up to its word, and that was the end of our shit-in."

**Lesson:** The action never occurred. The threat alone - leveraging the imagination of the target - achieved the objective. This became Rule 9.

## The Industrial Areas Foundation (IAF)

### Founding (1940)

The Industrial Areas Foundation was established in 1940 by Saul Alinsky, Roman Catholic Bishop Bernard James Sheil, and businessman Marshall Field III (founder of the Chicago Sun-Times). The founding Board of Directors included Kathryn Lewis, daughter of John L. Lewis, President of the United Mine Workers.

**Mission:** To build on the success of the Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council and replicate the model in other communities across Chicago and beyond.

### The IAF Model

The IAF developed the systematic approach to community organizing that would become the standard:

1. **Outside organizers, local leadership** - Professional organizers enter communities but never become the face of the movement. They identify and develop indigenous leaders.

2. **Institutional base** - Build organizations out of existing institutions (churches, unions, civic groups) rather than recruiting individuals directly.

3. **Self-interest focus** - Organize around what people actually want, not abstract causes.

4. **Achievable victories** - Start with winnable fights to build confidence and momentum.

5. **Paid professional organizers** - This is a job, not volunteerism. Professional standards apply.

### Notable IAF Trainees and Spinoffs

The IAF and Alinsky's methods trained or influenced:

- **Cesar Chavez** - The Community Service Organization (CSO), an IAF affiliate, provided Chavez's first training in organizing. Fred Ross, working for the IAF, met Chavez in 1952 and hired him. Chavez later founded the United Farm Workers (UFW).

- **Dolores Huerta** - Co-founder of the UFW, also trained through the CSO network.

- **Fred Ross** - IAF organizer who developed the "house meeting" technique that became the hallmark tactic of the UFW. Ross trained thousands of organizers.

- **Ed Chambers** - Alinsky's designated successor, who became IAF executive director after Alinsky's death in 1972.

- **Ernesto Cortes Jr.** - Developed the congregation-based organizing model in San Antonio (Communities Organized for Public Service - COPS) that became the template for modern IAF work.

### Post-Alinsky Evolution

After Alinsky died of a heart attack in June 1972, Ed Chambers transformed the IAF:

- Placed systematic training at the center of IAF work
- Professionalized the organizer role
- Developed the 10-day National Training program
- Expanded internationally

Today, the IAF consists of 65 affiliates in the United States, Canada, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia.

## Organizer Training Methodology

### The One-on-One Meeting

The foundation of Alinsky-style organizing is the "one-on-one" or "relational meeting" - a strategic conversation that:

- Builds relationship between organizer and community member
- Surfaces the person's motivations and concerns
- Identifies potential leadership
- Maps the person's connections to others and institutions

Dick Harmon's method in Buffalo: After each meeting, he created a card indicating:
- The person's self-interest
- Relationships with existing institutions
- Relationships with other people
- Past involvement in action

### The Organizing Cycle

IAF organizations follow an iterative pattern:

1. **One-to-one meetings and listening** - Building relationships, understanding interests
2. **Research and planning** - Power analysis, target selection
3. **Action** - Public confrontation, negotiation
4. **Evaluation** - What worked? What didn't? What's next?

The cycle repeats, with each round building organizational capacity.

### Deep Listening and Relationship Building

The IAF approach is called "relational organizing" because organizers teach leaders how to build relationships within and between organizations. Leaders come together in small groups where they:

- Share personal stories
- Engage in "deep listening"
- Speak about formative experiences
- Build personal ties across race, class, and institutional boundaries

### Core Training Concepts

IAF training (remarkably consistent whether in London, Sydney, Los Angeles, or Berlin) includes:

- **The Melian Dialogue role play** - An unsettling exercise that demonstrates power dynamics
- **Power over vs. power with** - Building power together rather than dominating
- **Public vs. private** - What you say publicly vs. what you negotiate privately
- **The relational meeting** - Technique and practice
- **The role of organization** - Why structure matters
- **Action and reaction** - How to provoke useful responses

### Alinsky's Personal Approach to Training

Alinsky believed organizers needed:

1. **Curiosity** - Genuine interest in how things work and how people think
2. **Irreverence** - Willingness to question everything, especially authority
3. **Imagination** - Ability to see tactics others miss
4. **Sense of humor** - Essential for sustaining morale and using ridicule effectively
5. **Organized personality** - Ability to manage complexity and maintain discipline
6. **Ego** - Enough confidence to take on powerful opponents
7. **Free and open mind** - Ability to adapt rather than follow ideology rigidly

## Influence on Modern Organizing

### Barack Obama and Chicago Organizing

From June 1985 to May 1988, Barack Obama worked as director of the Developing Communities Project (DCP), a church-based community organization on Chicago's far South Side. Obama received comprehensive training in Alinsky methods, which he later described as "the best education he ever had."

However, Obama also diverged from Alinsky's approach. He expressed the view that "Alinsky understated the degree to which people's hopes and dreams and their ideals and their values were just as important in organizing as people's self-interest."

Gregory Galluzzo, one of Obama's mentors, noted that Obama "knew it, he was taught it, but he never embraced it" - referring to Alinsky's more confrontational style. Obama preferred conciliation where Alinsky preferred stirring conflict.

Alinsky biographer Sanford Horwitt saw Alinsky's influence not only on Obama's Chicago work but also on his successful 2008 presidential campaign, which combined grassroots organizing techniques with electoral politics.

### Tea Party Adoption of Alinsky Tactics

In an ironic twist, conservative activists have embraced Alinsky's methods:

- **FreedomWorks** (the conservative nonprofit) distributed a short adaptation of Alinsky's work, *Rules for Patriots*, through its entire network
- **Dick Armey** (former Republican House Majority Leader) reportedly gave copies of *Rules for Radicals* to Tea Party leaders
- **Michael Patrick Leahy** (early Tea Party leader) wrote *Rules for Conservative Radicals* (2009), offering "sixteen rules for conservative radicals based on lessons from Saul Alinsky, the Tea Party Movement, and the Apostle Paul"

Tea Party town hall confrontations - aggressively challenging lawmakers, creating spectacles, using ridicule - came directly from Alinsky's playbook.

As one analyst noted: "The Tea Party comes from the same sense of outrage that the elites are running the country. The Tea Party has understood how to mobilize their anger and turn it to political results, which is the underlying motif of Alinsky."

### Enduring Relevance

Alinsky's framework has transcended ideology. Both left and right now use his principles to:
- Mobilize supporters
- Pressure opponents
- Shape narratives
- Build power from the grassroots

In the digital age, these tactics are amplified - social media enables rapid mobilization, and threats (Rule 9) spread faster than ever. The principles Alinsky articulated in 1971 remain potent tools for anyone seeking to challenge established power.

### The Alinsky Paradox

Alinsky would likely have appreciated the irony of conservatives using his methods. He was always pragmatic rather than ideological. His goal was to teach people how to fight for their interests - whoever they were. The tools belong to whoever picks them up.
