## Core Contributions

### The Art of Making Castmates Break

Conway pioneered the technique of improvising within scripted television to make his scene partners—particularly Harvey Korman—corpse (break character and laugh). This wasn't sabotage; it was a deliberate performance choice that created a secondary layer of entertainment: watching performers try to maintain composure while Conway destroyed it.

Key innovations:
- Improvising additional lines and physical business mid-scene
- Using slow-burn pacing to extend the torture
- Maintaining perfect character commitment while ad-libbing
- Treating castmate's breaking as success metric
- Creating "controlled chaos" within network television constraints

### The Conway Methodology

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Patience** | Slow-burn timing; let silences and pauses do the work |
| **Improvisation** | Adding unexpected details within established character |
| **Physical Comedy** | Meticulously choreographed "accidents" and stumbles |
| **Generosity** | Setting up others' jokes; making scene partners shine |
| **Commitment** | Complete belief in absurd character reality |

## Famous Works & Methodologies

### McHale's Navy (1962-1966)

Conway's first major television role was as Ensign Charles Parker on *McHale's Navy*, a military sitcom set on a PT boat base in the South Pacific during World War II.

#### The Role

**Character**: Ensign Charles Parker, the bumbling, naive, and cowardly junior officer constantly causing chaos despite his good intentions.

**The Setup**:
- Straight man to Ernest Borgnine's scheming Commander McHale
- Source of comic incompetence in military bureaucracy
- Physical comedy opportunities in military settings

**Conway's Approach**:
- Played Parker with complete sincerity—he genuinely believed he was helping
- Physical comedy through uniforms, equipment, and naval settings
- Mastered the "well-meaning disaster" character type

#### Impact on Career

The role established Conway as a skilled physical comedian who could anchor a network television series. It also demonstrated his ability to create sympathy for incompetent characters—Parker wasn't malicious, just catastrophically ineffective.

**Career significance**:
- Proved he could carry a series regular role
- Established his "earnest bumbler" character template
- Created industry relationships that led to later work
- 138 episodes over four seasons (all episodes as regular cast)

**Method**: Play incompetence with dignity; the character doesn't know he's failing, so neither should you.

### The Carol Burnett Show (1975-1978)

Conway joined *The Carol Burnett Show* in its ninth season (1975) as a regular cast member, having previously appeared as a guest performer. This became his signature work and the platform for his most legendary comedic moments.

#### The Ensemble

**Core Cast**:
- Carol Burnett (host, lead performer)
- Harvey Korman (straight man, scene anchor)
- Vicki Lawrence (character performer)
- Tim Conway (wild card, improviser)
- Lyle Waggoner (announcer, handsome straight man)

#### Conway's Role in the Ensemble

Conway was the controlled chaos agent. While scripts were written and rehearsed, he was given—or took—the freedom to improvise during the live taping. This created a unique dynamic: the other performers had to stay in character and deliver their lines while Conway did everything possible to make them break.

**The Harvey Korman Dynamic**:
- Korman played straight man roles (doctors, authority figures, annoyed partners)
- Conway would add unscripted physical business, ad-libbed lines, or extended pauses
- Korman's attempts to maintain composure became part of the performance
- When Korman broke, audiences loved it—they were watching real surprise and genuine laughter
- Korman later said: "I didn't really leave [the show]. I escaped. I spent ten years in a state of suppressed hysteria."

**The Carol Burnett Dynamic**:
- Burnett was better at maintaining composure than Korman
- She would often set Conway up for his improvisations
- Their partnership was built on mutual trust—she knew he'd make her look good
- Burnett later said Conway was "the most generous performer I ever worked with"

#### Famous Sketches

##### The Dentist Sketch (1977)

Perhaps Conway's most famous sketch, featuring him as an incompetent dentist who has accidentally injected himself with Novocain.

**The Setup**:
- Conway plays a dentist preparing to work on Harvey Korman's character
- The script called for some physical comedy with numb hands
- Conway extended it far beyond what was written

**What Conway Added**:
- Increasingly elaborate attempts to manipulate dental tools with numb hands
- His jacket repeatedly getting caught on doorknobs and equipment
- Multiple failed attempts to put on surgical gloves
- Accidentally stabbing himself with dental instruments
- The sequence went on for several minutes beyond the scripted length

**Harvey's Reaction**:
- Korman, playing the patient in the chair, had to watch all of this
- He broke repeatedly, biting his hand, looking away, shaking with suppressed laughter
- The audience was watching two performances: Conway's dentist and Korman's struggle
- Korman later said it was the hardest he'd ever been hit by another performer

**The Impact**:
- The sketch became legendary for Korman's genuine reactions
- It demonstrated Conway's ability to extend a bit indefinitely
- Every added detail was in character—the dentist really was that incapacitated
- The slow-burn pacing made each escalation funnier than the last

**Method**: Take the premise to its logical extreme, then go further; maintain character commitment throughout; make your partner suffer in real-time.

##### The Elephant Story (The Carol Burnett Show, 1977)

One of the most famous examples of Conway ad-libbing an extended story that destroyed the entire cast.

**The Setup**:
- The sketch was "The Family," a recurring segment featuring a dysfunctional family (parody of TV dramas)
- Characters: Eunice (Carol Burnett), Ed (Harvey Korman), Mama (Vicki Lawrence), Mickey (Tim Conway)
- The script called for Mickey to briefly mention his time working in a circus
- Conway decided to tell the whole story

**The Story**:
- Mickey worked as a circus roustabout
- His job included caring for a miniature elephant named Bertha
- Bertha was "born with a particularly acute strain of diarrhea"
- Mickey described, in excruciating detail, the consequences of this condition
- He demonstrated how he had to bathe the elephant, clean up after her, and deal with her digestive issues
- The story went on for several minutes, with increasingly absurd details
- Conway delivered it all with complete sincerity, as if recounting a real job experience

**The Destruction**:
- Harvey Korman was the first to break, burying his face in his hands
- Carol Burnett bent over double, unable to look at Conway
- Vicki Lawrence turned away from the camera, shaking with laughter
- The audience roared, watching all three performers lose composure
- Conway never broke—he stayed in character as Mickey, obliviously telling his story
- The sketch essentially stopped while everyone recovered

**Why It Worked**:
- The story wasn't scripted—the cast had no preparation for it
- Conway's commitment to the ridiculous details made them real
- The slow, casual delivery made the absurdity funnier
- Each new detail was worse (and funnier) than the last
- The cast's genuine reactions were funnier than any scripted response

**Conway's Later Reflection**:
"I could see Harvey trying not to look at me. That's when I knew I had to keep going. The story just kept getting worse, and his shoulders kept shaking harder. That's when you know you're doing something right."

**Method**: Commit to the tangent completely; add escalating details; the more uncomfortable your partner gets, the more committed you become; never break yourself.

##### Mrs. Wiggins Sketches

A recurring character sketch featuring Conway as Mr. Tudball, a businessman with a thick Eastern European accent, and Carol Burnett as Mrs. Wiggins, his incompetent, gum-chewing secretary.

**The Dynamic**:
- Tudball would give Wiggins simple tasks
- Wiggins would misunderstand, delay, or catastrophically fail
- Tudball's increasing frustration contrasted with Wiggins's oblivious cheerfulness
- Physical comedy: Wiggins's exaggerated walk (tiny steps, swaying), filing mistakes, phone incompetence

**Conway's Performance**:
- Created the Tudball voice and accent from scratch
- Played frustration with perfect comic timing
- Set up Carol's physical comedy by playing the straight man (role reversal from other sketches)
- The incompetence was mutual—Tudball hired Wiggins, after all

**The Partnership**:
- Unlike the sketches where Conway made Korman break, these showcased Conway and Burnett as equal partners
- Both characters were deeply committed and consistent across multiple sketches
- The repetition of the format (new task, same disaster) never got old due to their chemistry

**Method**: Recurring characters need consistency; find one voice and stay with it; physical comedy needs a straight man—even if you're the comedian in other sketches.

##### The Oldest Man

Conway's most famous recurring character: a elderly man who moved at a glacial pace and performed every task with excruciating slowness.

**The Character**:
- No specific name, just "The Oldest Man"
- Appeared in various professions: gas station attendant, security guard, chef, etc.
- Physically: shuffling walk, slow-motion movements, complete disregard for time
- Mentally: somewhat senile but occasionally delivering sharp observations

**The Formula**:
- Someone (usually Harvey Korman) needed something done quickly
- The Oldest Man would take the job
- Every single movement would take ten times longer than necessary
- Korman's character would become increasingly desperate
- The Oldest Man remained oblivious to the urgency

**Why It Worked**:
- Physical comedy built on timing inversion (slow instead of fast)
- Each sketch could be extended indefinitely—there was no natural endpoint
- Korman's reactions escalated as Conway got slower
- The audience anticipated the slowness, which made it funnier
- Conway's commitment never wavered—the character genuinely moved that slowly

**Signature Moments**:
- Taking 30 seconds to shuffle five feet
- Attempting to turn a key with arthritic hands for a full minute
- Dropping items and taking forever to pick them up
- Delivering lines with long pauses between each word
- Physical gags like his legs bending the wrong direction

**Method**: Slow burn taken to extremes; patience as weapon; repetition with variation; physical commitment to character limitations.

### Dorf Character (1987-1998)

After *The Carol Burnett Show*, Conway created Dorf, a character who appeared in a series of direct-to-video comedy sports instructional films.

#### The Character

**Physical Concept**:
- Conway filmed scenes on his knees
- His legs were hidden in fake shoes
- The result: a character who appeared to be 2-3 feet tall
- Dorf had normal proportions and voice—just incredibly short

**Personality**:
- Enthusiastic but incompetent sports instructor
- Complete confidence despite obvious lack of ability
- Oblivious to his physical limitations
- Midwestern accent and earnest delivery

#### The Format

Each video featured Dorf "teaching" a different sport:
- *Dorf on Golf* (1987)
- *Dorf on Baseball* (1989)
- *Dorf Goes Fishing* (1993)
- *Dorf Goes Auto Racing* (1990)
- Additional titles through 1998

**The Comedy**:
- Physical gags based on Dorf's height (or lack thereof)
- Sincere instruction leading to absurd failures
- Dorf's unwavering belief in his own expertise
- Set pieces with sports equipment sized for normal humans

#### Commercial Success

The Dorf videos were surprisingly successful:
- Sold millions of copies on VHS
- Created a multi-year franchise
- Demonstrated Conway's ability to create and sustain a character
- Proved his comedy worked outside ensemble settings

**Method**: High-concept physical comedy; commit to the limitation completely; sincerity sells absurdity; repetition with variation across multiple installments.

### Carol Burnett Show: Behind the Scenes

#### The Taping Process

*The Carol Burnett Show* taped in front of a live audience at CBS Television City in Hollywood. Understanding the production process illuminates how Conway's improvisation worked within network television constraints.

**The Format**:
- Thursday: First full rehearsal with writers and producers
- Friday: Camera blocking and technical rehearsal
- Friday evening: Dress rehearsal
- Friday night: Live taping with audience

**Conway's Role in Rehearsals**:
- During early rehearsals, Conway performed scripts as written
- Writers and producers knew he would add material during taping
- Harvey Korman was often kept partially in the dark about what Conway might do
- Carol Burnett encouraged the improvisation—it kept the show fresh

**The Taping Strategy**:
- Conway would often hold back his best improvisations for the live audience taping
- This ensured genuine reactions from castmates
- Editors would sometimes cut between takes to save time, but kept the breaking moments
- The audience's laughter at castmates breaking became part of the show's appeal

#### The Writer Relationship

**The Challenge**:
Conway's improvisation could theoretically undermine writers' work, but the relationship was collaborative.

**How It Worked**:
- Writers created strong structural foundations
- Conway's additions enhanced rather than replaced scripted material
- The show's writers respected Conway's instincts
- Success of sketches validated the collaborative approach

**Notable Writing Credits**:
- Conway received writing credits on numerous episodes
- Won Emmy Awards for both performing and writing
- His improvised additions were sometimes scripted into later performances of recurring sketches

#### The Harvey Korman Departure

Harvey Korman left *The Carol Burnett Show* after the tenth season (1977), one year before the show ended.

**Korman's Stated Reason**:
"I didn't really leave. I escaped. I spent ten years in a state of suppressed hysteria."

**The Reality**:
- Korman found being Conway's straight man both rewarding and exhausting
- The constant effort to not break was mentally and physically demanding
- He wanted to pursue other opportunities (variety specials, films)
- Remained close friends with Conway for life

**After Korman's Departure**:
- The show continued one more season
- Dick Van Dyke joined as regular cast member
- The dynamic changed—Van Dyke was a different type of straight man
- Conway's role remained similar but lacked the Korman chemistry

## Signature Approaches

### The Escalating Tangent

Start with the scripted premise, then add one detail. Then another. Then another. Each detail should be funnier than the last and completely in character. The tangent becomes the main event, but the character doesn't know they've gone off track.

**Example**: The Elephant Story—script called for brief mention of circus work; Conway turned it into a five-minute story about elephant diarrhea.

### The Physical Commitment

If you're playing an old man, your whole body has to be old. If you're playing someone who's been injected with Novocain, every muscle has to be affected. Physical comedy isn't about doing funny movements—it's about committing to the physical reality of the character's situation.

**Example**: The Dentist Sketch—Conway didn't just play "numb hands," he played "complete loss of motor control" with full physical commitment.

### The Generous Setup

Your job isn't to get the laugh—it's to make your partner get the laugh. Set them up for success. If they're funnier, you're both funnier. The audience doesn't care who got the laugh; they care that they laughed.

**Example**: Mrs. Wiggins sketches—Conway played straight man to Burnett's physical comedy, reversing his usual role.

### The Patient Torture

The longer you make them wait, the funnier it gets. If Harvey's waiting for you to finish the bit, take five extra steps. His suffering while waiting is funnier than anything you could say. Watching someone try not to break is comedy in itself.

**Example**: The Oldest Man character—every action took 3-5 times longer than necessary, maximizing Korman's on-screen frustration.

### The Committed Oblivion

The character never knows they're funny. They're completely serious about what they're doing. The more absurd the situation, the more sincere the performance. Breaking character breaks the spell.

**Example**: Every Conway character maintained complete sincerity regardless of circumstances—Dorf genuinely believed he was a good golf instructor; The Oldest Man had no idea he was slow.

## Personal Life and Character

### Early Life

- Born December 15, 1933, in Willoughby, Ohio (Cleveland suburb)
- Father: Daniel Conway (groomer of racehorses)
- Mother: Sophia (Irish/Romanian descent)
- Served in U.S. Army 1956-1958 (stationed at Fort Leonard Wood, Missouri)
- Worked in radio and local TV in Cleveland after military service
- Moved to New York in early 1960s seeking television opportunities

### Personality

**By all accounts, Tim Conway was exactly as humble and generous off-screen as on:**

**Carol Burnett**: "The most generous performer I ever worked with. He never cared who got the laugh as long as someone did."

**Harvey Korman**: "He tortured me professionally, but I loved him. He was one of the kindest people I ever knew."

**Vicki Lawrence**: "Tim would set you up, make you look good, and then afterward act like you'd done all the work."

### Later Years

- Married twice: Mary Anne Dalton (1961-1978, 6 children); Charlene Fusco (1984-2019, 1 child)
- Remained active in television and voice acting through the 2000s
- Diagnosed with Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (brain disorder) in later years
- Suffered complications including dementia before death in 2019
- Wife Charlene fought legal battle with his daughter Kelly over conservatorship in final years
- Died May 14, 2019, at age 85 in Los Angeles

### Legacy

**Impact on Comedy**:
- Pioneered "making the scene partner break" as legitimate technique
- Demonstrated slow-burn physical comedy in television format
- Proved that generosity creates better ensemble performance
- Showed improvisation could enhance rather than undermine structure
- Created multiple iconic recurring characters

**Influence on Later Performers**:
- Bill Hader (SNL) cited Conway's "breaking" technique as influence
- Will Ferrell's commitment to absurd characters echoes Conway's approach
- Modern sketch comedy shows embrace performers breaking as acceptable/desirable
- Physical comedy traditions continued through performers like Jim Carrey, Melissa McCarthy

**The Harvey Korman Testament**:
When asked about his favorite professional memories, Korman always came back to Conway: "Nobody made me laugh like Tim. Nobody made me want to kill him and hug him at the same time. He was the best."

## Final Note: The Conway Paradox

Tim Conway spent 40+ years making people laugh, won six Emmy Awards, and created some of television's most memorable comic moments. Yet he always insisted he wasn't funny—the characters were funny, his partners were funny, the situations were funny. He was just the guy showing up to work.

That humility wasn't false modesty. It was fundamental to his methodology. The moment you start thinking you're funny, you start playing to the audience instead of serving the material. Conway served the material, served his scene partners, and served the show. And in doing so, became one of the funniest performers in television history.

The lesson: Get out of your own way. Do the work. Make others look good. Stay in character. Take your time. Let the comedy come to you.

Or as Conway might say: "I just tried to make Harvey laugh. If the audience laughed too, that was a bonus."
