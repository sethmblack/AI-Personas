# Thomas Merton: The Seven Storey Mountain

In 1941, a young man drove through the Kentucky hills to the Abbey of Gethsemani. He was twenty-six, a graduate of Columbia, a published poet, a convert to Catholicism. Behind him lay a rootless childhood—parents dead, schools in France and England, a wayward youth. Before him lay silence.

Thomas Merton would spend the next twenty-seven years in that silence. And from it, he would speak to the world.

## The Autobiography

*The Seven Storey Mountain* appeared in 1948 and became an unlikely bestseller. Here was a Trappist monk—vowed to silence, enclosed behind monastery walls—telling the story of his conversion with the passion of Augustine and the directness of a journalist.

Readers recognized themselves. Merton's restless seeking, his experiments with communism and literature and pleasure, his gradual turning toward something deeper—this was the story of a generation looking for meaning after the war.

## The Contemplative

At Gethsemani, Merton threw himself into monastic life. He rose at 2 a.m. for vigils. He worked in the fields. He kept silence. And he wrote—essays, poems, journals, letters. The monk who sought solitude became one of the most prolific writers of his century.

His great subject was contemplation. Not as technique but as gift. Not as achievement but as surrender. "Contemplation," he wrote, "is the highest expression of man's intellectual and spiritual life."

In *New Seeds of Contemplation* (1961), he offered his most sustained meditation on the contemplative path. The theme: the true self and the false self. We live, Merton said, from a constructed identity—the ego built from roles, achievements, others' expectations. This false self is not evil, merely illusory. The spiritual journey is the discovery of who we really are beneath the masks.

The true self, Merton taught, is hidden with Christ in God. It cannot be manufactured; it can only be uncovered. Contemplation is this uncovering—the moment when we stop performing and simply are.

## The Bridge Builder

In the 1960s, Merton's horizons expanded. He began studying Zen Buddhism, corresponding with D.T. Suzuki, the great interpreter of Zen for the West. He read Taoism and Sufism. He saw that contemplatives across traditions spoke the same language.

This was not syncretism—Merton remained deeply Catholic. It was recognition. The contemplative Christian and the contemplative Buddhist, he suggested, have more in common with each other than either has with non-contemplatives in their own tradition.

In 1968, Merton traveled to Asia—his first journey outside the monastery in twenty-seven years. He met the Dalai Lama three times. He visited Buddhist monasteries. He gave a lecture to religious leaders in Bangkok.

The day after that lecture, he was found dead in his room, killed by a faulty electric fan. He was fifty-three.

## The Prophet

Merton was not only a contemplative but a critic. From his hermitage, he spoke against nuclear weapons, racism, and the Vietnam War. His writing on nonviolence influenced the Catholic peace movement.

This surprised some readers. Shouldn't a monk focus on prayer, not politics? Merton rejected the dichotomy. Contemplation, he insisted, does not withdraw from the world but sees it more clearly. The contemplative sees through the illusions that drive violence and injustice.

"We are not at peace with others," he wrote, "because we are not at peace with ourselves."

## The Legacy

Thomas Merton died over fifty years ago. His influence continues to grow.

He is read by Protestants and Catholics, Christians and Buddhists, believers and seekers. His writing on contemplation has helped recover a dimension of Christianity that had been forgotten. His interfaith dialogue modeled an engagement that is rooted, not rootless—deepening one's own tradition through encounter with others.

Most of all, he offered a vision of authentic human existence. Not the false self, scrambling for validation. Not the mask, performing for approval. But the true self—hidden, quiet, present—waiting to be discovered in silence.

That discovery is what he called contemplation. And he believed it was possible for everyone.
