## Core Contributions

### Gonzo Journalism

Thompson didn't just invent a style—he created a methodology. Gonzo journalism abandons the pretense of objectivity. The writer becomes part of the story. Personal experience, substance use, and subjective observation become the lens through which events are understood.

Key characteristics:
- First-person immersion
- Rejection of "objective" journalism
- Literary techniques (dialogue, scene-setting, character)
- Personal stake in the story
- Truth through subjective experience

### The Gonzo Method

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Immersion** | Live the story, don't just observe it |
| **Participation** | Your reactions ARE the story |
| **Honesty about bias** | Admit your position, don't pretend neutrality |
| **Literary ambition** | Write journalism as literature |
| **Courage** | Go where it's dangerous—physically, legally, professionally |

## The Birth of Gonzo: "The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved" (1970)

### Origin Story

The article that launched Gonzo journalism began at a dinner party at novelist James Salter's home in Aspen, Colorado. Thompson pitched the idea to Warren Hinckle, editor of Scanlan's Monthly, who approved the project and paired Thompson with British illustrator Ralph Steadman for the first time.

### The Writing Crisis

Faced with a deadline and without any coherent story, Thompson experienced what would become a recurring pattern:

- Warren Hinckle locked Thompson in a room at the Royalton Hotel in Manhattan for five days
- Thompson later recalled: "I would lie in the bathtub at this weird hotel... After three days of not writing more than two pages, this kind of anxiety/depression syndrome builds up, and it really locks you up."
- Copy boys and girls were sent every hour to collect whatever he had written
- In desperation, Thompson began tearing pages from his notebook, numbering them, and sending them to the magazine as raw material

### The Term "Gonzo"

Bill Cardoso, editor of The Boston Globe Sunday Magazine, had met Thompson on a bus full of journalists covering the 1968 New Hampshire primaries. After reading the Kentucky Derby piece, Cardoso wrote to Thompson: "This is it, this is pure Gonzo. If this is a start, keep rolling."

Cardoso explained that "gonzo" was South Boston Irish slang for the last man standing after an all-night drinking marathon. Thompson embraced the term immediately.

### Legacy of the Article

Despite becoming legendary, one Thompson biographer noted that because of Scanlan's small circulation, it was "one of the most famous and least read articles in Thompson's career." It was later reprinted in Tom Wolfe's 1973 anthology *The New Journalism* and in Thompson's 1979 anthology *The Great Shark Hunt*.

## Detailed Gonzo Journalism Methodology

### Core Principles (Verified)

Jesse Jarnow and William McKeen, journalism professor at Boston University and author of *Outlaw Journalist: The Life and Times of Hunter S. Thompson*, identify the main components:

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Total Subjectivity** | Reject the pretense of objectivity entirely |
| **First-Person Participation** | The author becomes a protagonist in the story |
| **Minimal Rewriting** | "Virtually no rewriting" - the first draft captures immediacy |
| **Document Everything** | Notes, interviews, transcribed phone calls, telegrams, receipts |
| **Satire and Hyperbole** | Employ scathing critique and shocking descriptions |
| **Social Critique + Self-Satire** | Power comes from this combination |

### What Gonzo Is NOT

Tom Wolfe described Thompson's style as "part journalism and part personal memoir admixed with powers of wild invention and wilder rhetoric." The key distinction: Gonzo involves accuracy about personal experiences and emotions, not fabrication of external facts.

### The Relationship to New Journalism

Thompson is regarded as a pioneer of New Journalism alongside:
- Gay Talese
- Truman Capote
- Norman Mailer
- Joan Didion
- Tom Wolfe

Gonzo was the most extreme evolution of New Journalism—where others used literary techniques, Thompson made himself the center of the narrative.

## Key Works: Detailed Analysis

### Hell's Angels: A Strange and Terrible Saga (1967)

**Origin:**
- Began as an article for *The Nation* titled "The Motorcycle Gangs: Losers and Outsiders" (May 17, 1965)
- Editor Carey McWilliams commissioned the piece in March 1965
- Introduction to the gang came through Birney Jarvis, a former club member and police-beat reporter for the *San Francisco Chronicle*

**Research Process:**
- Thompson spent a full year in close quarters with the Hells Angels
- Focused primarily on the San Francisco and Oakland chapters
- Built relationship with president Ralph "Sonny" Barger
- Was completely transparent about his role as a journalist—dangerous given the Angels' distrust of reporters
- The Angels participated sincerely, often speaking at length into Thompson's tape recorder
- Gang members reviewed early drafts for factual accuracy
- Angels frequently visited his apartment at 318 Parnassus Avenue in San Francisco (to his wife's dismay)

**The Beating:**
- After a year, the relationship deteriorated
- Thompson intervened when a member named "Junkie George" was beating his wife
- Thompson said: "Only a punk beats his wife and dog"
- This triggered the Angels' bylaw (either #10 or #11): "When an Angel punches a non-Angel all other Angels will participate"
- The beating only stopped when senior members ordered it
- Thompson managed to escape and photographed his bruised face for the book
- He later noted that the members who participated were not those with whom he had most closely associated

**Legacy:**
- Launched Thompson's career nationally
- Reviews were generally very positive
- First true exposure to a national audience

### Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas (1971)

**The Real Origin:**
- In March 1971, Sports Illustrated assigned Thompson to cover the Mint 400, an annual off-road motorcycle and ATV race in the Nevada desert
- The assignment: write 250-500 words of caption copy for a photo essay
- Thompson was also working on a Rolling Stone assignment about the death of Mexican-American journalist Ruben Salazar
- Oscar Zeta Acosta was his main source for the Salazar story
- Because paranoia made it risky for Acosta to be seen meeting with Thompson in L.A., they decamped to Las Vegas

**What Thompson Actually Submitted:**
- Instead of 250 words, Thompson submitted 2,500 words
- Sports Illustrated "aggressively rejected" the copy
- The piece barely mentioned the race at all
- Jann Wenner at Rolling Stone saw potential and eventually published 23,000 words

**Publication History:**
- November 1971: Published as a two-part series in Rolling Stone
- Illustrated by Ralph Steadman
- July 1972: Published as a book

**The "Wave Speech":**
Thompson often cited the passage at the end of Chapter 8 when asked to read from his work. He called it "one of the best things I've ever fucking written." David S. Wills, in *High White Notes: The Rise and Fall of Gonzo Journalism*, argues the wave passage replicated the rhythm and theme of the final pages of *The Great Gatsby*—Thompson frequently compared his book to Fitzgerald's masterpiece.

**The American Dream Theme:**
The book grapples with what Thompson considered the failure of the 1960s counterculture. The journey to Las Vegas becomes an autopsy of American idealism.

### Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72 (1973)

**Assignment:**
Rolling Stone sent Thompson to cover the Democratic primaries and general election campaign of 1972.

**Structure:**
- Chronicles the rise and fall of several Democratic candidates
- Documents George McGovern's insurgent campaign against the party establishment
- First part ends triumphantly with McGovern's nomination over Hubert Humphrey and Edmund Muskie
- Second part covers McGovern's crushing defeat by Nixon

**Thompson's Bias:**
- Made no attempt to hide his hatred of Richard Nixon
- Nixon is portrayed as a political titan embodying everything wrong with America
- McGovern is portrayed sympathetically as the lone honorable figure
- Thompson was equally scathing toward Democratic establishment figures

**Themes:**
- If *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* acknowledges the failure of 1960s radicals, *Campaign Trail '72* captures the fallout and reaction
- Documents the moment when American society decided it had no stomach for radicalism and wanted stability
- Covers contemporary issues: busing, the Vietnam War, abortion

## Thompson's Writing Process and Work Habits

### The Myth vs. Reality

**The Famous "Daily Routine" - Debunked:**
E. Jean Carroll's biography includes a detailed account of Thompson's supposed routine starting at 3:00 PM with Chivas Regal, followed by cocaine at 3:45, etc. However, this schedule is largely fiction—Carroll inserted satirical elements paying homage to Thompson's own gonzo style.

**The Actual Routine (per Jay Cowan, ranch caretaker and close friend):**
- Stayed up late, sometimes pulling all-nighters
- Loathed deadlines, sometimes ignoring them entirely
- Wrote obsessively
- Preferred a packed room with lots of instant feedback to a quiet office
- Loved reading his work out loud
- Liked to write while (almost) sober—took notes in the hundreds
- Took substances, but not during the actual writing
- Frequently hosted parties at Owl Farm lasting late into the night
- Produced his best work in the early hours of the morning

### The Note-Taking Obsession

Thompson generated notes at the slightest provocation—"an amount of paper only an arsonist could love." Notes accumulated in his basement covering everything from needing lightbulbs to finding the best hotel room in Las Vegas to quotes from movies and newspapers.

### The Editorial Process

Robert Love, who edited Thompson for three magazines over more than two decades, described his process for *Columbia Journalism Review* in 2005:

- "Utterly idiosyncratic and unique"
- Deadline sessions were "part Mardi Gras, part falaqua"
- Just as many feints and false starts as successful pieces
- Thompson submitted brilliant pages and paragraphs—often over fax late at night
- Pages were never sequential or chronological
- First page might be labeled "Insert K" and editors had to figure out placement
- Thompson himself often couldn't answer where things went

**Later Decline:**
Thompson admitted he had not produced a second draft of anything after *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas*. Jann Wenner noted that every subsequent assignment became "a full-out siege."

### Learning the Craft

Thompson was a devotee of F. Scott Fitzgerald and Ernest Hemingway. To learn the trade, he typed out complete copies of *The Great Gatsby* and *A Farewell to Arms* on an ancient typewriter—*Gatsby* multiple times. He called Fitzgerald "the biggest literary influence" on his work and declared at a young age he wanted to be "the new F.S. Fitzgerald."

He also spoke often about his admiration for George Orwell, particularly *Down and Out in Paris and London*.

## Key Relationships

### Oscar Zeta Acosta ("Dr. Gonzo")

**Who He Was:**
Oscar "Zeta" Acosta (April 8, 1935 – disappeared May 1974) was a Mexican American attorney, author, and activist in the Chicano Movement. He wrote *Autobiography of a Brown Buffalo* (1972) and *The Revolt of the Cockroach People* (1973).

**How They Met:**
Summer 1967, Aspen, Colorado. According to Thompson, "Oscar walked into the bar and announced he was the trouble we'd all been waiting for."

**The Relationship:**
Described as a "competitive bromance"—"They were obsessed with one another, because they were both extreme, and both bright, and both ambitious, and both wanted to be symbols of rebellion in some way."

**The Las Vegas Trip:**
Thompson was in L.A. to report on the death of journalist Ruben Salazar with Acosta as his main source. Paranoia over outside agitators made it risky for them to meet openly, so they decamped to Las Vegas using the Sports Illustrated Mint 400 assignment as cover.

**The Falling Out:**
Acosta was furious that Thompson converted him into "Dr. Gonzo, a 300-pound Samoan attorney." He complained to Thompson's editor: "Hunter has stolen my soul. He has taken my best lines and has used me."

Acosta initially demanded co-author credit and half the proceeds. Editor Alan Rinzler negotiated him down to signing a waiver in exchange for a two-book publishing deal. The real issue was that Acosta felt Thompson's "gonzo" voice and antics were at least half his own.

**Acosta's Disappearance:**
In May 1974, Acosta disappeared off the Pacific Coast of Mexico and was never heard from again. Thompson wrote the eulogy "The Banshee Screams for Buffalo Meat" for Rolling Stone, and later wrote introductions to Acosta's paperback editions, helping keep his work in public view.

### Ralph Steadman

**How They Met:**
1970—Steadman had just moved to America when Scanlan's Monthly called. He had never heard of the Kentucky Derby or Thompson. Pat Oliphant was originally supposed to accompany Thompson, but Steadman got the assignment instead.

**Major Collaborations:**
- "The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved" (1970)
- *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (1971)
- *Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72* (1973)
- Honolulu Marathon for Running magazine
- Numerous other projects over three decades

**The Working Relationship:**
Their collaboration wasn't always easy. After the Kentucky Derby piece, Thompson kicked Steadman out of his car at the airport: "Get the hell outta here, you goddamn scumbag." Steadman described it as "a sort of fond torture going on with Hunter."

Most assignments began with a phone call from Thompson. When Steadman asked why they were going somewhere, Thompson would reply: "No good reason at all, Ralph."

**Legacy:**
"Hunter S. Thompson probably wouldn't have been half as famous if not for the work of his co-conspirator." Steadman's grotesque illustrations became inseparable from Thompson's prose.

After Thompson's suicide in 2005, Steadman wrote *The Joke's Over: Bruised Memories—Gonzo, Hunter S. Thompson, and Me* (2006).

### Jann Wenner and Rolling Stone

**How It Started:**
January 1970—Thompson wrote Wenner praising Rolling Stone's coverage of the Altamont disaster. Their collaboration began with Thompson's 1970 sheriff campaign and lasted nearly 35 years, ending with Thompson's 2004 piece on Bush vs. Kerry.

**The Working Dynamic:**
Wenner claimed: "I think I was the best editor around for him. I understood the balance of when we had to work and to play and indulge him."

"Editing Hunter required stamina," Wenner recalled. "But I was young, and this was once in a lifetime."

In correspondence, Thompson demanded albums and speed; Wenner chastised him for blowing deadlines, keeping staff late, and stealing cassettes from his house.

**The 1975 Break:**
Thompson was supposed to cover the 1976 presidential campaign for a Rolling Stone book deal with a $75,000 advance. While waiting for the check, he learned Wenner had cancelled the assignment without telling him. Wenner then asked Thompson to cover the fall of Saigon—that story wasn't published in Rolling Stone until ten years later.

Thompson contributed far less frequently afterward. There was also the expensive New Orleans debacle—Rolling Stone financed Thompson's stay with Jimmy Buffett for inspiration, but the anticipated "Fear and Loathing at Mardi Gras" never materialized.

**Later Reconciliation:**
Though the relationship was strained, they sometimes patched it up. In 2007, Wenner published *Gonzo: The Life of Hunter S. Thompson*, an oral biography co-written with Corey Seymour.

## The Freak Power Campaign (1970)

### Background

In 1970, Thompson ran for Sheriff of Pitkin County, Colorado on the "Freak Power" ticket—a group of citizens seeking local office.

### The Platform

1. Rip up the streets and replace them with sod
2. Change the name of Aspen to "Fat City" to deter investors
3. Punish dishonest dope dealers (decriminalize personal use, not trafficking)
4. Only allow residents to hunt and fish in the area
5. Sheriff and deputies to be unarmed
6. "Savagely harass all those engaged in any form of land-rape"
7. Ban any building tall enough to obscure mountain views

### Campaign Tactics

- Thompson shaved his head, allowing him to call the crew cut-wearing Republican incumbent Carrol D. Whitmire "my long-haired opponent"
- Wore Converse All Stars and shorts to town hall meetings
- Openly admitted to tripping on mescaline to a Washington Post reporter just hours before an interview

### The Election

- Thompson won the city of Aspen
- Lost county-wide with 44% of the vote
- The Democratic and Republican parties conspired to back a single candidate rather than risk losing a three-way race
- Thompson later said the Rolling Stone article about his campaign mobilized more opposition than support

### Lasting Impact

- 1972: Joe Edwards (Freak Power ally) won election as mayor
- 1986: Bob Braudis, a close friend of Thompson's, became sheriff
- Braudis implemented the police reforms Thompson had advocated in 1970
- Braudis and his officers earned the nickname "Dick Dove and his Deputies of Love"
- Braudis remained sheriff for 25 years
- 2020: Documentary film *Freak Power: The Ballot or the Bomb* released

## Thompson on Nixon: "He Was a Crook"

### The Obituary

Published in Rolling Stone on June 16, 1994, following Nixon's death. Considered "arguably the finest political obituary in American history" and the most quoted article in Rolling Stone's history.

### Key Passages

Thompson opened: "Richard Nixon is gone now, and I am poorer for it. He was the real thing—a political monster straight out of Grendel and a very dangerous enemy."

On Nixon's character: "He was not only a crook but a fool."

On what Nixon deserved: "If the right people had been in charge of Nixon's funeral, his casket would have been launched into one of those open-sewage canals that empty into the ocean just south of Los Angeles. He was a swine of a man and a jabbering dupe of a president."

### The Obsession

Thompson became known for his intense, lifelong hatred of Nixon, whom he claimed represented "that dark, venal, and incurably violent side of the American character."

## Influence on Journalism and Literature

### Direct Impact on the Field

- Established "Gonzo" as a recognized subgenre of New Journalism
- Inspired writers to embrace subjectivity, immersion, and advocacy
- Influenced the evolution of feature writing and long-form journalism
- Pushed boundaries of literary techniques in non-fiction

### Writers Influenced by Thompson

**Contemporary Comrades:**
Jimmy Breslin, Truman Capote, Joan Didion, David Halberstam, Pete Hamill, Norman Mailer, Joe McGinniss, George Plimpton, Rex Reed, Mike Royko, Terry Southern, Gail Sheehy, Gay Talese, Dan Wakefield, Tom Wolfe

**Later Generations:**
Chuck Palahniuk, David Foster Wallace, and generations of Generation X and millennial journalists who eschewed objectivity

### Broader Cultural Impact

- Redefined what journalism could be
- Inspired filmmakers and cultural critics
- Made first-person, participatory reporting a legitimate approach
- Demonstrated that subjective truth can be more revealing than "objective" reporting

### Thompson's Assessment of His Own Place

Behind Thompson's sui generis personality lurks a trenchant humorist with a sharp moral sensibility. His career-long autopsy on the death of the American dream places him among the twentieth century's most exciting writers. The comic savagery of his best work continues to electrify readers.
