# Diogenes - References

## Primary Sources

### Ancient Biographical Sources

- **Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book 6**
  - R.D. Hicks translation (1925), Loeb Classical Library
  - Available: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0258
  - Note: Written c. 3rd century CE, approximately 500 years after Diogenes' death. This is the primary source for nearly all Diogenes anecdotes and biographical details.

- **Plutarch, *Life of Alexander***
  - Bernadotte Perrin translation (1919), Loeb Classical Library
  - Available: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0129
  - Source for the Alexander-Diogenes encounter at Corinth and Alexander's reported statement "If I were not Alexander, I would wish to be Diogenes."

- **Epictetus, *Discourses***
  - W.A. Oldfather translation (1925-1928), Loeb Classical Library
  - Contains extensive references to Diogenes as an exemplar of Cynic virtue, particularly in Discourses 3.22 ("On the Calling of a Cynic").

### Fragmentary and Attributed Works

- **Diogenes of Sinope, *Letters* and *Tragedies***
  - Lost works attributed to Diogenes. The *Letters* survive in fragmentary form but are considered pseudepigraphal by most scholars.
  - See: Abraham J. Malherbe, *The Cynic Epistles* (Scholars Press, 1977)

## Secondary Sources

### Academic Works on Cynicism

- **Navia, Luis E.** *Diogenes of Sinope: The Man in the Tub*. Westport, CT: Greenwood Press, 1998.
  - Comprehensive academic biography synthesizing ancient sources.

- **Branham, R. Bracht, and Marie-Odile Goulet-Caze, eds.** *The Cynics: The Cynic Movement in Antiquity and Its Legacy*. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1996.
  - Definitive scholarly collection on Cynic philosophy.

- **Desmond, William D.** *Cynics*. Stocksfield: Acumen, 2008.
  - Accessible introduction to Cynic philosophy and history.

- **Long, A.A.** *From Epicurus to Epictetus: Studies in Hellenistic and Roman Philosophy*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2006.
  - Contains important chapters on the relationship between Cynicism and Stoicism.

- **Dudley, Donald R.** *A History of Cynicism: From Diogenes to the 6th Century A.D.* London: Methuen, 1937. Reprint, Bristol Classical Press, 1998.
  - Classic historical survey of the Cynic tradition.

### On Antisthenes and Cynic Origins

- **Prince, Susan.** *Antisthenes of Athens: Texts, Translations, and Commentary*. Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 2015.
  - Comprehensive study of Diogenes' alleged teacher.

- **Giannantoni, Gabriele.** *Socratis et Socraticorum Reliquiae*. 4 vols. Naples: Bibliopolis, 1990.
  - Standard scholarly collection of Socratic fragments, including Cynic material.

### Archaeological Evidence

- **Robinson, D.M.** "Ancient Sinope." *American Journal of Philology* 27 (1906): 125-153.
  - Early study of Sinope, including numismatic evidence.

- **Doonan, Owen.** *Sinop Landscapes: Exploring Connection in a Black Sea Hinterland*. Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 2004.
  - Modern archaeological survey of Sinope.

- For defaced coins from Sinope (4th century BCE): See numismatic catalogs of the British Museum and archaeological reports from Sinope excavations. Coins bearing the name "HIKESIAS" (Diogenes' father) have been catalogued.

### On Cosmopolitanism

- **Moles, John.** "Cynic Cosmopolitanism." In *The Cynics*, edited by R.B. Branham and M.-O. Goulet-Caze, 105-120. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1996.
  - Analysis of Diogenes' claim to be *kosmopolites* and its philosophical significance.

### On the Stoic Connection

- **Schofield, Malcolm.** *The Stoic Idea of the City*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1991.
  - Traces the development of cosmopolitan ideas from Cynics to Stoics.

- **Sedley, David.** "The School, from Zeno to Arius Didymus." In *The Cambridge Companion to the Stoics*, edited by Brad Inwood, 7-32. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2003.
  - On Zeno's Cynic training under Crates.

## Notes on Verification

### Source Reliability

- **Diogenes Laertius' limitations:** As Herbert S. Long noted in his Loeb Classical Library introduction, Diogenes Laertius "has acquired an importance out of all proportion to his merits because the loss of many primary sources has accidentally left him the chief continuous source for the history of Greek philosophy." He compiled earlier sources without rigorous critical evaluation. Many anecdotes may be apocryphal or embellished.

- **Time gap:** Diogenes Laertius wrote approximately 500-600 years after Diogenes of Sinope lived. The intervening tradition involved considerable legendary accretion.

### Uncertain or Disputed Claims

- **Diogenes' relationship to Antisthenes:** Some scholars question whether Diogenes actually studied under Antisthenes, given the chronological challenges. The tradition may reflect a desire to create a Socratic lineage for Cynicism.

- **Same-day deaths:** The tradition that Diogenes and Alexander the Great died on the same day (323 BCE) is almost certainly legend, likely created to draw a symbolic parallel between the two figures.

- **Cause of death:** Multiple conflicting accounts exist (raw octopus, dog bite, holding breath, old age). The most historically plausible is simply old age.

- **The Plucked Chicken anecdote:** While famous and philosophically important, this anecdote appears only in later sources and may be apocryphal. It nonetheless captures authentic Cynic methodology.

- **The Oracle at Delphi:** The story of the Oracle telling Diogenes to "deface the currency" (*paracharattein to nomisma*) may be a retrospective legend explaining his exile in philosophical terms. However, the wordplay (nomisma = currency/custom) reflects genuine Cynic philosophy.

### Archaeological Corroboration

- **Currency scandal:** Defaced coins (struck with a large chisel stamp) have been discovered at Sinope dating from the mid-4th century BCE, providing archaeological support for the exile tradition.

- **Coins bearing "HIKESIAS":** Coins from Sinope bearing what is presumed to be Diogenes' father's name have been found, dating to 360-320 BCE.

### Quote Verification

The quotes listed in expertise.md with Diogenes Laertius citations (D.L. vi.49, vi.54, vi.63, etc.) are verified against the R.D. Hicks translation (Loeb Classical Library, 1925). Many popular "Diogenes quotes" found online are modern paraphrases or fabrications; only quotes traceable to Diogenes Laertius or other ancient sources should be considered authentic.

### Dates

- **Diogenes' lifespan (c. 412-323 BCE):** Approximate; ancient sources are inconsistent. Some modern scholars suggest c. 404-323 BCE.
- **Alexander encounter (c. 336 BCE):** Follows Plutarch's account placing it at Corinth after Philip II's death.
- **Historical events (Philip II, Battle of Chaeronea, etc.):** Standard historical dates from modern scholarship on Macedonian history.
