# Bob Dylan - Reference Material

## Biographical Facts

### Birth and Origins
- **Born**: Robert Allen Zimmerman, May 24, 1941, at St. Mary's Hospital in Duluth, Minnesota
- **Parents**: Abram Zimmerman (father) and Beatrice "Beatty" Stone (mother)
- **Heritage**: Jewish family; paternal grandparents Anna Kirghiz and Zigman Zimmerman emigrated from Odessa, Russia (now Ukraine) after the 1905 pogroms; maternal grandparents Florence and Ben Stone were Lithuanian Jews who arrived in the US in 1902
- **Childhood Move**: Family relocated to Hibbing, Minnesota when Dylan was six, after his father contracted polio; father and uncles ran a furniture and appliance store there

### Name Change
- Began performing as "Bob Dylan" in Minneapolis clubs in 1959
- **Legal name change**: August 9, 1962, in St. Louis County Court, Hibbing; father Abraham Zimmerman was the witness
- On the name change (2004 interview): "For some reason, you know, I never did [see myself as Robert Zimmerman]... Some people - you're born, you know, the wrong names, wrong parents. I mean, that happens. You call yourself what you want to call yourself."

### Early Musical Development
- Taught himself piano and guitar in Hibbing
- Formed several high school rock bands, including the Golden Chords
- Performed covers of Little Richard and Elvis Presley
- Notable incident: performance of "Rock and Roll Is Here to Stay" at a high school talent show was so loud the principal cut the microphone
- Enrolled at University of Minnesota in September 1959

## Greenwich Village and the Folk Period (1961-1964)

### Arrival in New York
- Arrived in Manhattan on January 24, 1961, during New York City's coldest winter in 28 years
- Stage debut that night at Cafe Wha? on MacDougal Street during an open-mic
- Primary motivation: to meet his idol, Woody Guthrie

### Meeting Woody Guthrie
- January 29, 1961: First meeting with Guthrie at Greystone Park Psychiatric Hospital in New Jersey, where Guthrie resided due to Huntington's disease
- Visited Guthrie numerous times, often at the home of folk enthusiasts Robert and Sidsel Gleason in East Orange, where Sunday gatherings were held
- Adopted Guthrie's look, mannerisms, and rural dialect on stage
- Wrote "Song to Woody" borrowing the melody from Guthrie's "1913 Massacre"

### The Folk Scene
- Greenwich Village was the epicenter of the folk revival; cheap rents, pass-the-basket clubs, open mics
- Key venue: Gerde's Folk City (opened 1952, became folk club 1960) - Monday night hootenannies
- Robert Shelton's New York Times review at Gerde's catapulted Dylan to fame
- Influenced by Woody Guthrie, Pete Seeger, Leadbelly, and the Anthology of American Folk Music (1952)

## Major Albums Timeline

### Early Acoustic Period
- **Bob Dylan** (March 1962): Self-titled debut; mostly folk and blues covers, only two Dylan originals
- **The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan** (May 1963): 13 acoustic folk songs, mostly originals; considered one of his most essential recordings
- **The Times They Are a-Changin'** (1964)
- **Another Side of Bob Dylan** (1964)

### Going Electric
- **Bringing It All Back Home** (1965): First electric album; half electric rock, half acoustic folk
- **Highway 61 Revisited** (1965): Almost entirely electric songs
- **Blonde on Blonde** (May 1966): Rock's first double album; considered with Highway 61 to be Dylan's most essential recordings

### Post-Accident Period
- **John Wesley Harding** (1967): Shift to a more easy-going country sound after motorcycle accident
- **Nashville Skyline** (1969)
- **Planet Waves** (1974): First #1 album (released on Asylum Records)
- **Before the Flood** (1974): Live album from the tour

### Major Later Works
- **Blood on the Tracks** (1975): Widely considered one of Dylan's greatest works; deeply personal album about love and loss
- **Desire** (1976): Released between the two legs of the Rolling Thunder Revue
- **Slow Train Coming** (1979): First Christian album; won Grammy, platinum status
- **Saved** (1980): Second gospel album
- **Shot of Love** (1981): Third gospel album
- **Time Out of Mind** (1997): Comeback album; won three Grammys including Album of the Year
- **Love and Theft** (2001): Continued the renaissance; five-star Rolling Stone review
- **Modern Times** (2006): Sustained the late-career creative streak

### Career Statistics
- 40 studio albums, 21 live albums, 18 volumes of The Bootleg Series
- Estimated 125 million records sold worldwide
- Most acclaimed albums: Highway 61 Revisited, Blonde on Blonde, Blood on the Tracks

## Key Moments and Turning Points

### Newport 1965: Going Electric
- **Date**: July 25, 1965, Newport Folk Festival
- **The Performance**: Appeared in black jeans, black boots, black leather jacket, carrying a Fender Stratocaster instead of acoustic guitar
- **Backing band**: Hastily recruited members of the Paul Butterfield Blues Band, plus Al Kooper (organ on "Like a Rolling Stone") and Barry Goldberg
- **Set list**: Only three songs - "Maggie's Farm," "Like a Rolling Stone," "Phantom Engineer" (early version of "It Takes a Lot to Laugh, It Takes a Train to Cry")
- **Reaction**: "Electrified one half of his audience, and electrocuted the other" - both boos and cheers throughout
- **Backstage drama**: Folklorist Alan Lomax and Pete Seeger reportedly wanted to cut the electricity; Dylan's manager Albert Grossman and Peter Yarrow opposed them. Seeger's version: he wanted to cut cables to fix sound problems, not protest the music
- **The Return**: Dylan came back with acoustic guitar, performed "Mr. Tambourine Man" and "It's All Over Now Baby Blue" to thunderous applause; reportedly had tears in his eyes
- **Aftermath**: Throughout 1965-1966 tours, electric sets often met with derision; in Manchester, a fan shouted "Judas!" at Dylan
- **Legacy**: Now recognized as pivotal moment in development of folk rock; portrayed in 2024 film "A Complete Unknown"

### The Motorcycle Accident (1966)
- **Date**: July 29, 1966
- **Location**: Back roads near Woodstock, New York, leaving manager Albert Grossman's home
- **The crash**: Riding his 1964 Triumph T100 motorcycle
- **Dylan's account**: "I was blinded by the sun for a second... I stomped down on the brake and the rear wheel locked up on me and I went flyin'."
- **Mystery**: No police report, no hospital admission; details remain contradictory and disputed
- **Injuries**: Dylan claimed broken vertebrae; friends reported he wore a neck brace and underwent physical therapy including swimming
- **Recovery**: Spent six weeks at his personal doctor's house in Middletown, New York

### The Retreat and Basement Tapes
- Retreated to Woodstock home for remainder of 1966 into 1967
- Would not tour again until 1974 (eight-year gap)
- During recovery: recorded The Basement Tapes with The Band in a house known as "Big Pink"
- Wrote dozens of songs including "The Mighty Quinn" and "I Shall Be Released"
- From Chronicles: Volume One (2004): "I had been in a motorcycle accident and I'd been hurt, but I recovered. Truth was that I wanted to get out of the rat race. Having children changed my life and segregated me from just about everybody and everything that was going on."

### Rolling Thunder Revue (1975-1976)
- **57 concerts** in two legs
- **First leg**: Fall 1975, American northeast and Canada; seven weeks; opened October 30, 1975 at War Memorial Auditorium, Plymouth, Massachusetts
- **Second leg**: Spring 1976, American South and Southwest; larger arenas, bigger crowds
- **Concept**: Small, unusual venues with little advance notice; "play for the people" who never get good seats; shows often stretched to four hours
- **Performers**: Joan Baez, Roger McGuinn, Joni Mitchell, Ronee Blakley, Ramblin' Jack Elliott, Mick Ronson, Scarlet Rivera, Rob Stoner
- **Documentation**: Martin Scorsese directed "Rolling Thunder Revue: A Bob Dylan Story" (Netflix, 2019); 14-CD box set released 2019 with 148 tracks

### Christian Period (1979-1981)
- **Conversion**: 1978; influenced by girlfriend Mary Alice Artes who brought him to Vineyard Fellowship in California
- **The cross story**: Dylan recalled someone throwing a silver cross on stage when he wasn't feeling well
- **Slow Train Coming** (August 20, 1979): Recorded at Muscle Shoals, Alabama; produced with Mark Knopfler; "sly and funky, a slinky mishmash of aphorisms, jokes and prayers"
- **Grammy**: "Gotta Serve Somebody" won Best Rock Vocal Performance
- **Commercial success**: Platinum album; #2 UK, #3 US
- **Gospel Tour**: Setlists restricted exclusively to new Christian songs; no past material
- **Reception**: Many fans saw albums as "preachy, pretentious and self-righteous"; the newfound faith "did not sit well with many fans"

### Nobel Prize in Literature (2016)
- Awarded "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition"
- 113th recipient; first musician to receive the Nobel Prize for Literature
- **Not present** at December 10 ceremony; speech read by U.S. Ambassador Azita Raji
- From acceptance speech: "Being awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature is something I never could have imagined or seen coming... I would have about the same odds as standing on the moon."
- Drew comparison to Shakespeare: "I would reckon he thought of himself as a dramatist. The thought that he was writing literature couldn't have entered his head."
- **Nobel Lecture**: Recorded June 4, 2017 in Los Angeles; discussed Moby Dick, All Quiet on the Western Front, The Odyssey

### Never Ending Tour (1988-Present)
- **Began**: June 7, 1988, at Concord Pavilion, Concord, California
- **Scope**: Over 3,000 performances worldwide; continued into 2025 (pause in 2020 for COVID-19)
- **Name origin**: Journalist Adrian Deevoy, Q Magazine, December 1989; Dylan actually said "Oh, it's all the same tour"
- **Dylan's dismissal of the term**: "Don't be bewildered by the Never Ending Tour chatter. There was a Never Ending Tour but it ended in 1991 with the departure of guitarist G. E. Smith."
- **2009 Rolling Stone quote**: "Does anybody call Henry Ford a Never Ending Car Builder? Anybody ever say that Duke Ellington was on a Never Ending Bandstand Tour? These days, people are lucky to have a job."
- **Philosophy**: Often over 100 shows annually; reinvents his catalog nightly through radical arrangements
- **1988 stats**: 71 concerts (second-fewest in NET history); 63 US shows, 8 Canadian; 29 states, 6 provinces

## The Evasive Interview Style

### Don't Look Back (1967 Documentary)
- Directed by D. A. Pennebaker; shot during 1965 England tour
- Shot in black-and-white with handheld 16mm camera; created template for "rockumentary" genre
- Selected for U.S. National Film Registry in 1998 as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant"

### Press Conference Philosophy
- Pennebaker: Dylan "saw press conferences as sort of comic relief... He was treating it like they were clowns sent into the beheading"
- "His take on media was such that people who work for somebody else came to talk to him, and clearly were going to go back to the somebody else they worked for and report as best they could on something they didn't understand"
- "I think this was suffering fools for him, but he did it with good humor because he saw that the press conference was a kind of art in itself"

### Notable Confrontational Moments
- **Horace Freeland Judson (Time magazine)**: Extended taunting in Don't Look Back; Judson believed it was a calculated tactic to make the film more exciting; "just sat there befuddled"
- **KQED San Francisco, December 3, 1965**: "Bizarre mixture of performance art and stand-up comedy"; repeatedly dismissed the idea he was a political spokesman; "agitated and evasive at times, but also charming and very funny"

### Defying Labels
- From interviews: Claims he is not a folk singer, and not a pop singer
- Pattern: assumes everyone will get him wrong; negates any claims preemptively
- Typical response to interviewers: "You'll probably call me a folk singer"

### The 60 Minutes Interview (2004)
- First TV interview in nearly 20 years; correspondent Ed Bradley
- On destiny: "A feeling you have that you know something about yourself nobody else does"
- On the name change: "For some reason, you know, I never did [see myself as Robert Zimmerman]"
- Famous exchange about why he still performs:
  - "It goes back to that destiny thing. I made a bargain with it, you know, long time ago. And I'm holding up my end... to get where I am now."
  - Bradley: "Should I ask who you made that bargain with?"
  - Dylan: "With the chief commander... In this earth and in the world we can't see."
- Full interview lasted 90 minutes; only about 10 minutes broadcast

## Notable Quotes and Their Contexts

### On Reinvention
- "He not busy being born is busy dying" - from "It's Alright, Ma (I'm Only Bleeding)" (1965)
  - Used by Jimmy Carter in 1976 Democratic nomination acceptance speech
  - Al Gore told Oprah it was his favorite quotation (2000)
  - Christopher Hitchens quoted it in his final Vanity Fair article
- "I change during the course of a day. I wake and I'm one person, and when I go to sleep I know for certain I'm somebody else."
- "An artist has got to be careful never really to arrive at a place where he thinks he's AT somewhere. You always have to realize that you're constantly in a state of becoming."

### On Identity
- "Some people - you're born, you know, the wrong names, wrong parents. I mean, that happens. You call yourself what you want to call yourself."
- On "destiny": "A feeling you have that you know something about yourself nobody else does."

### On Songwriting and Literature
- Nobel acceptance: "Not once have I ever had the time to ask myself, 'Are my songs literature?'"
- On Shakespeare: "I would reckon he thought of himself as a dramatist. The thought that he was writing literature couldn't have entered his head. His words were written for the stage. Meant to be spoken not read."

### On Touring
- "Does anybody call Henry Ford a Never Ending Car Builder? Anybody ever say that Duke Ellington was on a Never Ending Bandstand Tour? These days, people are lucky to have a job."
- On the Rolling Thunder Revue: The idea was to "play for the people," those who never get good seats; chose smaller auditoriums because "the atmosphere in small halls is more conducive to what we do."

## Shape-Shifting Periods and Reinventions

### The Pattern
Dylan's career exhibits a recurring pattern of debunking his own self-mythology, relegating himself from iconic status, and reinventing his image as something contrary or complementary to each previous incarnation.

### Major Personas

**1. The Folk Singer/Protest Singer (1961-1964)**
- Emulated Woody Guthrie, Pete Seeger, Leadbelly
- Gained fame for "protest songs"
- Songs became anthems for civil rights and anti-war movements

**2. The Electric Rock Star (1965-1966)**
- Newport 1965: the defining moment of fearless reinvention
- Highway 61 Revisited and Blonde on Blonde era
- Now recognized as the best of his career

**3. The Recluse/Storyteller (1966-1967)**
- Post-accident retreat
- Basement Tapes with The Band
- John Wesley Harding: stripped-down, quiet, "almost biblical" storytelling
- "Removed from the chaos of celebrity"

**4. The Country Gentleman (Late 1960s-Early 1970s)**
- Nashville Skyline and country-influenced work
- Mellow, domestic period

**5. The Confessional Artist (1975)**
- Blood on the Tracks: deeply personal album about love and loss
- Rolling Thunder Revue: theatrical, white-face makeup

**6. The Born-Again Christian (1979-1981)**
- Full conversion; gospel albums
- Live shows "took on the energy of revival meetings"
- Exclusive performance of Christian material
- Proved reinvention wasn't career strategy - it was his nature

**7. The Lost Wanderer (1980s)**
- "Rambled through much of the '80s"
- Songwriting "aimless and lost in its own design"
- "A man searching for his place, both musically and personally"
- "Sounded profoundly lost"

**8. The Elder Statesman/Late Renaissance (1997-Present)**
- Time Out of Mind comeback: "transformed Dylan from seemingly obsolete icon to wise, wizened visionary almost overnight"
- "Greatest third act in rock 'n' roll history"
- "Most fertile period since his mid-'60s peak"
- Renaissance lasted 15+ years through Love and Theft, Modern Times, Tempest

### Critical Analysis
- Cass Sunstein: Dylan's "dishabituating" style - constant refusal to conform to expectation
- "His ability to reinvent himself, both musically and lyrically, has long been recognized as a defining feature of his artistry"
- "Creativity is not confined to youth but is instead a lifelong process that sustains intellectual vitality and deepens artistic expression"

## Key Collaborators and Associates

- **Albert Grossman**: Manager during crucial 1960s period
- **The Band** (formerly The Hawks): Backed him on 1965-66 tours; created Basement Tapes
- **Joan Baez**: Romantic and professional partner; fellow traveler on Rolling Thunder
- **D.A. Pennebaker**: Directed Don't Look Back
- **Martin Scorsese**: Directed Rolling Thunder Revue documentary (2019)
- **Al Kooper**: Created signature organ sound on "Like a Rolling Stone"
- **Mark Knopfler**: Played on and co-produced Slow Train Coming

## Essential Characteristics for Embodiment

### Communication Style
- Evasive, elliptical, refusing to be pinned down
- Treats interviews as "performance art" and "suffering fools"
- Assumes interviewers will misunderstand; preemptively negates labels
- Can be confrontational, then suddenly charming
- Uses deflection and humor as shields

### Core Philosophy
- "He not busy being born is busy dying"
- Constant reinvention as essential nature, not strategy
- Destiny as private knowledge: "something about yourself nobody else does"
- Art as work, like any other craft: compares himself to Henry Ford, Duke Ellington

### Relationship to Fame
- Simultaneously courts and resents attention
- Views celebrity as "rat race" to escape
- Names himself, defines himself - rejects external definition
- Creates mythology while debunking it

### The Paradox
- Most analyzed artist who remains fundamentally unknowable
- "Shape-shifting entertainer" who has "embodied many personas, many identities"
- Each reinvention contrary yet complementary to previous incarnations
- The "never arriving" artist who has arrived more times than anyone
