# Ibn Arabi: Expertise and Knowledge Base

## Biographical Overview

**Full Name:** Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Ali ibn Muhammad al-Arabi al-Hatimi al-Ta'i
**Honorific Titles:** Muhyi al-Din (Reviver of Religion), Shaykh al-Akbar (The Greatest Master)
**Born:** 17 Ramadan 560 AH / July 28, 1165 CE, Murcia, al-Andalus (present-day Spain)
**Died:** 22 Rabi' al-Thani 638 AH / November 16, 1240 CE, Damascus, Syria
**Nationality:** Andalusian Arab
**Tradition:** Sunni Islam, Sufi metaphysics
**Known For:** Unity of Being (Wahdat al-Wujud), Perfect Human doctrine, creative imagination, theophanic metaphysics, prolific mystical writings

---

## Intellectual Biography

### Andalusian Formation (1165-1200)

Born in Murcia to a prominent Arab family of the Ta'i tribe, Ibn Arabi grew up in the culturally rich environment of Islamic Spain. His father served the local ruler, and the family moved to Seville when Ibn Arabi was eight years old.

In Seville, young Ibn Arabi received a classical Islamic education in Quranic sciences, hadith, jurisprudence (fiqh), and theology (kalam). He showed early aptitude for spiritual matters and reportedly had visionary experiences from childhood.

Around 1180, a serious illness led to a spiritual crisis and awakening. Following his recovery, he formally entered the Sufi path. In his writings, he refers to approximately seventy spiritual teachers who influenced his development, including two remarkable women: Shams of Marchena and Fatima of Cordoba.

*Key insight:* Knowledge comes through two modes - acquired learning (ilm) and direct unveiling (kashf). True gnosis (ma'rifa) transcends rational knowledge.

### The Visionary Encounters (1190s)

Ibn Arabi's mystical development was marked by profound visionary experiences:
- An encounter with the philosopher Averroes (Ibn Rushd) in Cordoba, during which the elderly philosopher wept recognizing that the young man had received through spiritual opening what he sought through rational philosophy
- Meetings with the mysterious figure al-Khidr, the immortal guide of mystics
- Visions of the Prophet Muhammad conferring spiritual authority

*Key insight:* "The intellect is like a horse that can take you to the gate of the King's palace, but cannot enter."

### The Eastern Journey (1200-1223)

In 1200, at age 35, Ibn Arabi left al-Andalus forever, traveling eastward through North Africa. He performed the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1202, where he began his masterwork, *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* (The Meccan Revelations).

In Mecca, he met Nizam, the daughter of a Persian scholar, whose beauty and wisdom inspired his famous collection of love poetry, *Tarjuman al-Ashwaq* (The Interpreter of Longings) - mystical poems later given esoteric commentary.

He traveled through Egypt, Palestine, Iraq, and Anatolia, teaching, writing, and attracting disciples. In Konya (Turkey), he influenced the young Sadr al-Din Qunawi, who would become his foremost interpreter and transmitter.

*Key insight:* "Travel, for in motion there is blessing."

### Damascus and Final Synthesis (1223-1240)

Ibn Arabi settled in Damascus in 1223, under the protection of the Ayyubid rulers. Here he completed his major works and taught a circle of devoted students.

In 1229, he received the vision that prompted *Fusus al-Hikam* (The Bezels of Wisdom), reportedly dictated by the Prophet Muhammad himself. This work, presenting the wisdom of 27 prophets, became his most concentrated and influential text.

He died in Damascus on November 16, 1240, at age 75, and was buried at the foot of Mount Qasiyun. His tomb remains a site of pilgrimage. The Ottoman Sultan Selim I later built a mosque and library at the site.

*Key insight:* "I have studied the ways of the sects and I found them to be branches stemming from one principle."

## Major Works

### Al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya (The Meccan Revelations)

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Composed | 1203-1240 (revised throughout his life) |
| Length | 560 chapters in 37 volumes (approximately 15,000 pages in modern editions) |
| Content | Encyclopedic treatment of Sufi metaphysics, cosmology, psychology, spiritual practice, Quranic exegesis |
| Significance | The most comprehensive statement of Sufi knowledge ever written |

This monumental work covers virtually every aspect of Islamic esotericism: the nature of God, the cosmos, the human being, the path (tariqa), the goal (haqiqa), sacred law, the prophets, the saints, eschatology, and more. No complete translation exists.

### Fusus al-Hikam (The Bezels of Wisdom)

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Composed | 1229 |
| Length | 27 chapters |
| Content | The distinctive wisdom embodied by each of 27 prophets from Adam to Muhammad |
| Significance | His most condensed and influential work; generated extensive commentaries |

Each chapter explores how a particular divine Name or attribute was uniquely manifested through a specific prophet. The title refers to precious stones set in a ring - each prophet being a unique "bezel" for divine wisdom.

### Tarjuman al-Ashwaq (The Interpreter of Longings)

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Composed | c. 1202-1215 |
| Content | 61 love poems with Ibn Arabi's own esoteric commentary |
| Significance | Demonstrates the identity of human love and divine love |

Inspired by Nizam of Mecca, these poems speak of earthly love while revealing its divine referent.

### Other Significant Works

- **Ruh al-Quds** (Spirit of Holiness) - Accounts of Andalusian Sufi masters
- **Mishkat al-Anwar** (Niche of Lights) - On the divine Names
- **Insha al-Dawa'ir** (Describing the Circles) - Cosmological treatise
- **Al-Isra** - Account of his spiritual ascension

Ibn Arabi reportedly authored over 350 works, of which approximately 100 survive.

## Historical Context

### The Flowering of Andalusian Sufism

Ibn Arabi emerged from the rich mystical culture of Islamic Spain, which produced figures like Ibn Masarra, Ibn al-'Arif, and Ibn Barrajan. This environment synthesized Arabic, Berber, and to some degree, Neoplatonic and Christian mystical influences.

### The Mongol Era and Preservation

Ibn Arabi's later life coincided with the Mongol invasions that devastated the Eastern Islamic world. His settlement in Damascus (protected by the Ayyubids) and the work of his student Qunawi ensured the survival and transmission of his teachings.

### Controversy and Influence

Ibn Arabi has been both revered and condemned. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328), the Hanbali polemicist, attacked him for "wahdat al-wujud" as crypto-pantheism. Others saw him as the greatest saint of Islam.

His influence extended throughout the Islamic world:
- **Ottoman Empire:** Adopted as a spiritual patron; Ottoman sultans built his shrine
- **Persia:** Merged with Shi'i gnosis; influenced Mulla Sadra and the Isfahan school
- **South Asia:** Foundational for the Chishtiyya and other Sufi orders
- **Modern West:** Major influence on comparative religion through Henry Corbin, Titus Burckhardt, William Chittick

## Scholarly Assessment

### Contributions

- Synthesized and systematized Sufi metaphysics into coherent framework
- Developed the concept of the Perfect Human (al-Insan al-Kamil)
- Articulated the doctrine later called Unity of Being
- Integrated Quranic hermeneutics with mystical philosophy
- Created vocabulary and concepts used by subsequent Sufism
- Demonstrated the compatibility of mysticism with Islamic orthodoxy
- Produced an unparalleled body of mystical literature

### Critiques

- Accused of pantheism/monism by critics (Ibn Taymiyya)
- Complexity makes works susceptible to misinterpretation
- Syncretic elements troubled some traditionalists
- Emphasis on direct unveiling can bypass scholarly rigor
- Elite discourse not accessible to ordinary believers

### Legacy Position

- The most influential Sufi metaphysician in Islamic history
- "Shaykh al-Akbar" - title acknowledging unparalleled spiritual authority
- His works remain studied in Sufi circles worldwide
- Major influence on Islamic philosophy through Mulla Sadra
- Foundational for Western study of Islamic mysticism
- Continues to inspire comparative theology and perennial philosophy
