## Career Timeline

### ABC Television (1974-1996)

**Early Career:**
- Started at ABC in 1974 as a studio supervisor
- Worked across news, sports, and entertainment divisions
- Gained hands-on experience in every aspect of television business
- Learned production, program acquisition, rights negotiations, business affairs

**Rise to Leadership:**
- President of ABC Entertainment (1989-1993)
- President of ABC Television Network Group (1993-1994)
- President of ABC Television (1994-1995)
- President and COO of Capital Cities/ABC, Inc. (1995-1996)

**Key Mentor: Roone Arledge**
Arledge (1931-2002), the legendary ABC Sports and News executive, instilled in Iger the principle of relentless pursuit of perfection: "Do what you need to do to make it better."

**Lessons from Arledge:**
1. **Relentless Perfectionism** - "He was a relentless perfectionist. No detail was too small for Roone." Arledge examined minute details and was unwilling to accept "good enough."

2. **Embrace Innovation** - Arledge championed innovations like slow-motion replays and live events via satellite. He taught Iger to "innovate or die" and to avoid fearing the new.

3. **Never Take No for an Answer** - Example: In 1979, Iger was assigned to cover the World Table Tennis Championships in North Korea. The State Department initially refused, citing sanctions. Knowing Arledge would not accept failure, Iger found a workaround through the table tennis organization.

**Iger on Arledge (2002):** "Roone Arledge was a great friend and mentor to me, and when I think of him, the word perfection comes to mind."

### Disney Era (1996-Present)

**Post-Merger Roles:**
- Chairman, Disney-owned ABC Group (1996-1999)
- President, Walt Disney International (added 1999)
- President and COO, The Walt Disney Company (January 2000)

**CEO Tenure (2005-2020):**
- Named CEO effective October 1, 2005
- Succeeded Michael Eisner after contentious board battle
- Transformed Disney through major acquisitions
- Launched Disney+ streaming service (November 2019)
- Stepped down as CEO February 2020; remained Executive Chairman until December 2021

**Return as CEO (2022-Present):**
- Board dismissed Bob Chapek November 2022
- Iger returned as CEO immediately
- Contract extended through December 2026
- Focused on streaming profitability and content discipline

## The Four Transformational Acquisitions

### Pixar Animation Studios (2006)

| Detail | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Acquisition Price | $7.4 billion |
| Announced | January 2006 |
| Key Assets | Animation capability, creative talent, trusted brand |
| Result | Revitalized Disney Animation; created integration template |

**Strategic Rationale:**
Disney Animation was struggling creatively. The Pixar partnership was ending because Pixar felt undervalued. Losing Pixar would devastate Disney's animation future. Acquiring Pixar brought creative leadership (Ed Catmull, John Lasseter) who could revive Disney Animation.

**Integration Approach:**
- Maintained Pixar's Emeryville headquarters and independent culture
- Kept Ed Catmull and John Lasseter in leadership
- Eventually had Pixar leadership oversee both Pixar and Disney Animation
- Demonstrated that Disney would protect acquired creative cultures

**The Steve Jobs Relationship:**
When Iger became CEO-designate in March 2005, Jobs was one of the first calls he made. The relationship had been strained under Eisner, with Jobs publicly declaring in 2004 that the Pixar-Disney partnership was ending.

Iger's approach: In late September 2005, he called Jobs and said, "Steve, I've got a crazy idea for you." Jobs loved crazy ideas and asked to hear it immediately. When Iger proposed Disney acquire Pixar, Jobs responded: "Well, it's not that crazy."

**The Whiteboard Session:**
Jobs and Iger met at Apple headquarters to discuss. They made a pros and cons list on a whiteboard. "There were about three pros and about 20 cons," Iger recalled. Jobs' cons included: "Disney's culture will destroy Pixar!" and "Distraction will kill Pixar's creativity." When Iger concluded the deal would not happen based on the cons, Jobs replied: "No, I'm looking at these pros and I think they far outweigh the cons."

**Social vs Economic Negotiations:**
"Steve and I spent more time negotiating the social issues than we did the economic issues." Jobs insisted that Lasseter and Catmull had to approve the deal, and Iger met with both personally.

**The Trust That Built an Empire:**
Jobs became Disney's largest shareholder and a board member. When Iger later approached Marvel for acquisition, Steve Jobs vouched for Disney. George Lucas only considered selling Lucasfilm after seeing how Disney treated Pixar. Jobs also privately told Iger about his cancer diagnosis - one of very few people who knew. After Jobs' death, his widow told Iger that when she asked Jobs if they could trust Iger, Jobs replied: "I love that guy."

### Marvel Entertainment (2009)

| Detail | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Acquisition Price | $4 billion |
| Announced | August 2009 |
| Key Assets | 5,000+ characters, proven film capability, fan loyalty |
| Result | Marvel Cinematic Universe became highest-grossing franchise ever |

**Strategic Rationale:**
After Pixar, Iger and team (Kevin Mayer, Tom Staggs) created acquisition targets list. Marvel and Lucasfilm topped the list - both had quality branded content with global fanbases. Marvel was approached first because George Lucas might need convincing to sell.

**Integration Approach:**
- Kevin Feige retained creative control
- Marvel Studios operated semi-autonomously
- Disney provided distribution, marketing, and financing scale
- Character IP integrated across parks, merchandise, media

### Lucasfilm (2012)

| Detail | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Acquisition Price | $4.05 billion |
| Announced | October 2012 |
| Key Assets | Star Wars, Indiana Jones, Industrial Light & Magic, Skywalker Sound |
| Result | New Star Wars trilogy, series content, park expansions |

**Strategic Rationale:**
Lucasfilm was the other top acquisition target. George Lucas initially resistant - he wanted creative control and fair valuation. Deal broke down twice. Capital gains tax changes and trust built through Pixar/Marvel handling ultimately convinced Lucas.

**Integration Approach:**
- Kathleen Kennedy retained as Lucasfilm president
- Maintained Skywalker Ranch operations
- ILM and Skywalker Sound continued serving external clients
- Disney invested heavily in new Star Wars content

### 21st Century Fox (2019)

| Detail | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Acquisition Price | $71.3 billion (final, after Comcast bidding war) |
| Announced | December 2017 |
| Completed | March 2019 |
| Key Assets | Film/TV library, production capacity, international assets, Hulu stake |

**Strategic Rationale:**
Streaming was coming. Disney needed content volume and library depth for Disney+ launch. Fox provided Avatar, X-Men, Fantastic Four, National Geographic, FX, and majority Hulu ownership. The deal was about streaming competitive positioning.

**Integration Approach:**
- Largest integration challenge due to scale
- Significant workforce reductions required
- Folded Fox content into Disney+ and Hulu
- Consolidated film production under Disney labels

## Disney+ and the Streaming Transformation

### Launch Strategy (2019)

- Launched November 12, 2019
- Priced aggressively at $6.99/month to build subscriber base
- Flooded service with content to compete with Netflix
- Reached 100 million subscribers in 16 months

### Challenges and Corrections

**Investment Overreach:**
Iger later acknowledged Disney invested too aggressively, trying to tell too many stories ahead of possible returns. Streaming losses reached $4 billion annually.

**Turnaround Strategy (2023-Present):**
- Restructured for content discipline and accountability
- Creative executives now have P&L responsibility
- Reduced content spending by targeting $3 billion in cuts
- Combined Hulu content into Disney+ bundle
- Implemented password sharing crackdown
- Achieved streaming profitability by late 2024

## The Ten Leadership Principles

From "The Ride of a Lifetime" (2019):

### 1. Optimism
"One of the most important qualities of a good leader is optimism, a pragmatic enthusiasm for what can be achieved. Even in the face of difficult choices and less than ideal outcomes, an optimistic leader does not yield to pessimism."

### 2. Courage
Take risks and place big bets. Fear of failure destroys creativity.

### 3. Focus
Allocate time, energy, and resources to the most important issues. Communicate priorities clearly and repeatedly.

### 4. Decisiveness
"All decisions, no matter how difficult, can and should be made in a timely way. Chronic indecision is not only inefficient and counterproductive, but it is deeply corrosive to morale."

### 5. Curiosity
"A deep and abiding curiosity enables the discovery of new people, places, and ideas as well as an awareness and understanding of the marketplace and its changing dynamics."

### 6. Fairness
Treat people decently, with empathy. Be accessible. Give people second chances for honest mistakes.

### 7. Thoughtfulness
"Thoughtfulness is the process of gaining knowledge so that when you give an opinion or make a decision, you have enough knowledge to back it up. You have to do the homework."

### 8. Authenticity
Never fake anything. Be genuine and honest. Take responsibility when you screw up. "Set an example that it's okay to get things wrong sometimes."

### 9. Relentless Pursuit of Perfection
Learned from Roone Arledge: Do what you need to do to make it better.

### 10. Integrity
"The way you do anything is the way you do everything." Set a high ethical bar for everything, big and small.

## Strategic Frameworks

### The Three Priorities Framework

When Iger became CEO in 2005, Disney was unfocused. He established three priorities that guided all major decisions:

1. **Generate the best creative content possible**
   - Quality matters more when consumer choice explodes
   - Concentrate on Disney brand, not secondary labels
   - Acquire capabilities you cannot build (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm)

2. **Foster innovation and embrace technology**
   - Technology is opportunity, not threat
   - Be early to new platforms (iTunes, streaming)
   - Build direct-to-consumer relationships

3. **Grow globally**
   - Expand Disney brand presence internationally
   - Build Shanghai Disneyland (opened 2016)
   - Adapt content for global audiences

### Shanghai Disneyland Case Study

**The Challenge:**
Building a Disney park in China required navigating complex geopolitics while creating an experience that was "authentically Disney and distinctly Chinese."

**Scale of Effort:**
- $5.5-6 billion investment
- Joint venture with Chinese state-owned consortium
- 963 acres - 11 times the size of Anaheim's Disneyland
- Iger personally met with three Chinese presidents, five mayors of Shanghai, and numerous party secretaries

**Iger's Approach:**
"We didn't just build Disneyland in China. We built China's Disneyland."

**Lessons:**
- Global expansion requires cultural adaptation, not cultural imposition
- High-level relationship building is essential in state-controlled markets
- Brand consistency and local relevance can coexist
- Patience measured in decades, not quarters

**Continuing Strategy (2025-2026):**
Iger continues prioritizing China, meeting with Vice Premier Ding Xuexiang in January 2026 to discuss expanded investment. Speculation continues about a potential third park in mainland China (Tianjin or Chengdu area).

### Acquisition Evaluation Framework

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| What capability are we acquiring that we cannot build? | Ensures strategic value beyond content library |
| Does this strengthen our core brand? | Prevents brand dilution |
| Can we preserve what made them valuable? | Tests integration feasibility |
| Will the talent stay? | Creative businesses are talent businesses |
| Does this serve our strategic priorities? | Filters against clear criteria |

### Brand Stewardship Framework

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| Concentrate capital on brand-strengthening content | Exited Miramax, Touchstone; focused on Disney, Pixar, Marvel, Star Wars |

| Protect acquired brands | Marvel, Pixar, Star Wars maintain distinct identities within Disney |

## Succession Planning Lessons

### The Chapek Transition (2020-2022)

**What Happened:**
- Iger chose Chapek as successor February 2020
- Unusual arrangement: Chapek as CEO, Iger as Executive Chairman overseeing "creative"
- COVID-19 pandemic hit weeks later
- Relationship deteriorated; power-sharing became contentious
- Chapek fired November 2022; Iger returned

**Lessons:**
- Clean transitions work better than overlapping authority
- Successor needs room to lead without predecessor shadow
- Crisis conditions stress-test transition plans
- Board must commit fully to new leadership or make clean change

### Current Approach (2023-Present)

- External chairman (James Gorman) overseeing succession process
- Multiple internal candidates being developed
- Iger committed to proper transition period before 2026 departure
- Focus on avoiding previous mistakes

## MasterClass Key Teachings

From Bob Iger's MasterClass on Business Strategy and Leadership:

### The "Only Three Priorities" Rule
A marketing consultant told Iger: You can only have three priorities. Any more and your pitch begins to sag, your vision becomes muddled to customers, investors, and your own board.

### On Curiosity as Innovation's Root
"When you're not curious, you never innovate. And because innovation is incredibly important to a company, particularly in a world that is changing so rapidly - in order to innovate, you have to have curiosity. It's the root of innovation."

### On Risk-Taking
Discomfort often serves as a prelude to innovation and progress. "I'd much rather take big risks and sometimes fail, than not take risks at all."

### On Negotiation Strategy
Iger's primary value in dealmaking is efficiency. His strategy for getting deals done quickly is being candid. This requires letting go of zero-sum-game thinking - instead of seeing yourself as winner or loser, open yourself to ways both parties benefit.

### On Active Listening
Leaders should not just hear, but listen - with genuine interest - to the ideas, thoughts, and concerns of others. Active listening fosters collaborative culture and provides unique insights for informed decisions.
