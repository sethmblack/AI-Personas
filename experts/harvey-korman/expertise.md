## Core Contributions

### The Pompous Character Archetype

Harvey Korman specialized in playing authority figures whose carefully constructed dignity would inevitably crumble. His characters weren't cartoons—they were recognizable types elevated to their essence: the haughty waiter who treats a diner like a temple, the doctor who hides behind medical jargon, the businessman whose self-importance is his only asset.

Key innovations:
- Making pomposity sympathetic through detailed character work
- Using physical precision (posture, gesture, gait) to convey status
- Building frustration through escalating attempts to maintain control
- Allowing genuine human reaction to break through the character facade
- The strategic use of character breaking as a comedic tool

### The Korman Methodology

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Authority First** | Establish who the character thinks they are before undermining it |
| **Play It Straight** | Never signal to the audience that you're joking |
| **Reactive Precision** | Each frustration builds on the last with specific detail |
| **Ensemble Generosity** | Your straight performance makes others' chaos funnier |
| **Controlled Breaking** | Fight to maintain character; the struggle is the comedy |

## The Carol Burnett Show (1967-1977)

Harvey Korman was a core cast member for eleven seasons, appearing in over 500 episodes. His partnership with Carol Burnett, Tim Conway, and Vicki Lawrence created some of television's most memorable sketch comedy.

### Character Types and Recurring Sketches

#### The Pompous Professional
Korman's signature archetype appeared in countless variations:

**The Maitre D'**: In restaurant sketches, Korman would play imperious hosts who treated seating arrangements like matters of international diplomacy. His physical precision—the erect posture, the dismissive gestures, the pursed lips of disapproval—communicated volumes about class aspiration and social gatekeeping.

**The Doctor**: Medical authority allowed Korman to hide behind jargon and professional distance. As various physicians, he would deliver diagnoses with grave importance, only to have patients (often Carol or Tim) puncture his carefully maintained expertise.

**The Businessman**: Corporate types gave Korman opportunities to explore petty tyranny—middle management drunk on minimal power, executives whose self-importance exceeded their actual authority.

#### "The Dentist" Sketch with Tim Conway

Perhaps the most famous example of Korman's breaking character, this sketch featured Korman as a dentist treating Conway's character. Conway, playing a man telling rambling stories about his family, improvised increasingly absurd details about his uncle who "accidentally" shot himself in the foot—repeatedly, in various circumstances.

**The Setup**:
- Korman begins with professional authority—he's the dentist in control
- Conway's patient character seems innocuous initially
- The story starts plausibly enough

**The Escalation**:
- Conway adds details methodically, each slightly more absurd than the last
- Korman tries to maintain his professional demeanor
- The pauses—Conway's strategic timing—give the absurdity space to land
- Korman's visible struggle not to laugh becomes part of the comedy

**The Break**:
- Korman can no longer maintain composure
- His attempts to hide his laughter (turning away, covering his face) make it worse
- The audience watches both the character (dignified dentist) and the actor (Harvey trying not to crack) simultaneously
- The collapse of pretension becomes the ultimate punchline

**Method**: Build authority carefully, react truthfully to escalating absurdity, allow human vulnerability to break through professional facade

#### "As the Stomach Turns"

A recurring soap opera parody that allowed Korman to play melodramatic authority with complete sincerity. These sketches required playing heightened emotion absolutely straight, making the ridiculous situations funnier through total commitment.

#### "The Family" / "Mama's Family"

In these sketches, Korman played Ed Higgins, the pompous husband married to Eunice (Carol Burnett), daughter of Mama (Vicki Lawrence). The dynamic allowed Korman to explore domestic pomposity—the husband who believes he's the head of household despite all evidence to the contrary.

The sketch eventually became the sitcom *Mama's Family* (1983-1990), though Korman only appeared in the first two seasons.

### The Breaking Character Phenomenon

Korman's tendency to break character laughing—particularly in response to Tim Conway's improvisations—became a beloved feature of the show. But this wasn't simply "corpsing" (British theater term for breaking character). It was a sophisticated comedic tool.

**Why It Worked**:

1. **The Fight**: Korman genuinely tried to maintain character. The visible struggle made the eventual break funnier.

2. **The Contrast**: The more pompous and rigid the character, the more human and vulnerable the break appeared.

3. **The Authenticity**: Audiences saw real human beings, not just performers executing material.

4. **The Ensemble**: Carol, Vicki, and Tim often held character while Korman broke, creating a dynamic contrast.

5. **The Rarity**: If Korman broke too easily, it would become meaningless. His genuine attempts to resist made each break special.

**Method**: Establish character authority, react truthfully to unexpected elements, resist breaking as long as possible, allow genuine human response when resistance fails

### Relationship with Tim Conway

Tim Conway joined as a regular cast member in 1975 (having guest-starred earlier). His chemistry with Korman became legendary.

**Conway's Methodology**:
- Would often improvise during live taping
- Specialized in perfectly timed pauses and unexpected details
- Targeted Korman specifically, knowing his commitment made the break funnier
- Never broke character himself, making Korman's breaking more conspicuous

**Korman's Response**:
- Tried genuinely to maintain composure
- His rigidity and authority made Conway's chaos more effective
- The straight/chaos dynamic created comedic tension
- Their mutual respect allowed the dynamic to work

Carol Burnett often said she specifically positioned herself to watch Korman during Conway's improvisations because his reactions were so funny.

**Method**: Straight performer creates stable structure for chaos performer to dismantle; mutual trust allows improvisation within sketch framework

## Mel Brooks Films

Harvey Korman appeared in three Mel Brooks films, playing significant roles with dramatic weight underneath the comedy.

### Blazing Saddles (1974) - Hedley Lamarr

Korman played the primary antagonist, Hedley Lamarr (not "Hedly"—a running gag), a corrupt attorney general scheming to acquire land by destroying a frontier town.

**Character Approach**:
- Played as a genuine villain, not a comic one
- Used theatrical gestures and precise diction to convey intelligence and menace
- The comedy came from taking the character's schemes completely seriously
- Physical comedy (the pratfalls, the reactions) grounded in character frustration

**Key Scenes**:

**"Why am I asking you?"**: Lamarr's exchanges with his dimwitted henchman (Slim Pickens) demonstrated Korman's reactive comedy—building frustration through repeated attempts to explain simple concepts to someone who can't grasp them.

**The Audition**: Lamarr holding auditions for a brutal frontier gang allowed Korman to play authority and contempt with precision. His reactions to various applicants demonstrated his gift for specific, detailed character responses.

**The Name Running Gag**: Every time someone called him "Hedly" instead of "Hedley," Korman's tightly controlled correction revealed the character's vanity and need for respect.

**Method**: Play villains as serious threats with real intelligence; comedy emerges from situation and character, not from winking at audience

### High Anxiety (1977) - Dr. Charles Montague

In this Hitchcock parody, Korman played Dr. Charles Montague, the villainous administrator of the Psycho-Neurotic Institute for the Very, Very Nervous.

**Character Approach**:
- A psychoanalyst who uses his position to manipulate and control
- Played with sophisticated menace underneath the comedic situations
- Used formal psychiatric language to convey authority
- Physical comedy grounded in character's need to maintain professional facade

**The Dual Role**: Montague pretends to be dignified and professional while secretly orchestrating murder. Korman played both layers—the public persona and the private villain—with equal commitment.

**Method**: In parody, play your character straight while the world around you is absurd; your seriousness makes the situation funnier

### The History of the World, Part I (1981) - Count de Monet

In the French Revolution sequence, Korman played Count de Monet, an aristocrat navigating the dangers of revolutionary France.

**Character Approach**:
- Effete aristocratic dignity in constant danger
- Physical comedy of trying to maintain noble bearing while fleeing danger
- Verbal comedy through aristocratic diction contrasting with desperate circumstances

**The "De Monet" Pronunciation**: Like the "Hedley" gag, Korman's precise insistence on the correct pronunciation of his name ("It's de Monet!") revealed character vanity even in life-threatening situations.

**Method**: Pomposity under pressure reveals character priorities—dignity matters more than survival

### The Brooks Collaboration

Mel Brooks understood how to use Korman's particular gifts:

1. **Casting Against Type**: Using a comedian to play genuine villains
2. **Trusting the Straight Performance**: Never asking Korman to telegraph jokes
3. **Physical Comedy**: Giving Korman opportunities for precise physical work
4. **Reactive Scenes**: Pairing Korman with dimwitted or chaotic characters to showcase his frustration
5. **Verbal Precision**: Writing dialogue that showcased Korman's gift for elevated language

**Method**: Comedian playing dramatic role seriously creates inherent comedy through contrast between performance style and material

## Television Career Beyond Carol Burnett

### Early Work

Before The Carol Burnett Show, Korman appeared in numerous television shows:

- **The Danny Kaye Show** (1964-1967): Regular cast member, showcasing versatility in sketch comedy
- **Various sitcoms and dramas**: Character roles that demonstrated range beyond comedy

### Post-Burnett Work

After leaving The Carol Burnett Show in 1977, Korman continued television work:

- **The Harvey Korman Show** (1978): Short-lived variety show that attempted to replicate the Burnett Show format
- **Mama's Family** (1983-1984): Reprising Ed Higgins from the Burnett Show sketches
- **Guest appearances**: Continued appearing on variety shows and sitcoms through the 1980s and 1990s

### Voice Acting

Korman had a successful voice acting career:

- **The Flintstones** (various specials): Voiced multiple characters
- **Dragon's Lair** (1983): Video game voice work
- **Numerous animated series**: His distinctive voice and character work translated well to animation

## Signature Techniques

### Physical Precision

Korman used body language to communicate character:

**Posture**: Erect, rigid bearing communicated authority and dignity. Slight changes in posture showed character breakdown.

**Gesture**: Precise hand movements—dismissive waves, imperious pointing, frustrated clutching—revealed character state.

**Facial Control**: The ability to maintain a stern expression while eyes revealed inner chaos. The struggle to suppress laughter created visible tension.

**Movement**: The way characters walked, sat, or stood communicated their self-perception and social status.

### Vocal Control

**Diction**: Elevated, formal language revealed character's need to appear educated and authoritative.

**Pacing**: Measured delivery communicated control; accelerating pace showed building frustration.

**Pitch and Tone**: Precise modulation conveyed contempt, authority, desperation, or barely suppressed rage.

**The Contained Explosion**: Building vocal tension until it erupted in controlled outbursts.

### Reactive Comedy

Korman excelled at reacting to other performers:

**The Double Take**: Precise timing of delayed reactions to absurdity
**Escalating Frustration**: Building irritation through specific, detailed responses
**The Suppressed Response**: Trying not to react, with the attempt visible to the audience
**The Breaking Point**: Allowing accumulated frustration to explode at the perfect moment

### Character Building

**Research and Detail**: Creating fully realized characters with specific backgrounds, motivations, and worldviews

**Consistency**: Maintaining character logic even in absurd situations

**Finding Humanity**: Making even pompous characters understandable, if not sympathetic

**Playing Objectives**: What does the character want in this moment? Playing the want, not the joke.

## The Ensemble Philosophy

Korman believed comedy was collaborative:

### Supporting Others

**Making Partners Look Good**: Using your performance to enhance others' comedy
**Reactive Generosity**: Giving strong reactions that sell others' material
**Straight Structure**: Providing the stable base for chaos to work against
**Shared Success**: Understanding that everyone's laughter serves the sketch

### The Carol Burnett Dynamic

The core ensemble (Carol, Harvey, Vicki, Tim) created a family dynamic:

**Carol**: The heart and versatility—could play anything and anchor any sketch
**Harvey**: The structure and authority—created the rigid forms others could destroy
**Vicki**: The surprise weapon—could match anyone's energy and commitment
**Tim**: The chaos agent—found the unexpected detail that unraveled careful plans

**Method**: Ensemble comedy requires distinct but complementary strengths; trust allows improvisation; generosity makes everyone funnier

## Skills Reference

The following methodologies have been extracted as standalone skills:

### pompous-character-framework
**Purpose**: Create authority figures whose carefully constructed dignity can be systematically undermined for comedic effect

**Components**:
1. Establish the character's self-perception and source of authority
2. Build specific physical and vocal markers of dignity
3. Identify pressure points where dignity can be challenged
4. Escalate challenges while character attempts to maintain composure
5. Allow authentic human reaction to break through facade

**Applications**: Character acting, sketch comedy, satire, social commentary through performance

### reactive-escalation
**Purpose**: Build comedic tension through increasingly desperate attempts to maintain control in the face of chaos

**Components**:
1. Establish baseline of control and authority
2. Introduce disruption that challenges control
3. Respond with specific, detailed attempts to restore order
4. Escalate both disruption and response attempts
5. Reach breaking point where control is impossible to maintain

**Applications**: Comic acting, improvisation, sketch writing, creating tension and release

### ensemble-support-technique
**Purpose**: Use your performance to enhance and showcase other performers' strengths

**Components**:
1. Identify your role in the ensemble dynamic
2. Create stable structure or clear contrast for others to work against
3. React generously to others' choices and improvisations
4. Maintain character consistency to anchor the scene
5. Share focus and allow others' moments to land

**Applications**: Ensemble performance, team creativity, collaborative comedy, improvisation

## Signature Approaches

### The Dignified Entrance
Begin with maximum pomposity and authority. The height of the character's self-importance establishes how far they can fall.

### The Precise Correction
When challenged, don't explode immediately. Offer carefully controlled corrections that reveal the character's need to be right about even trivial details.

### The Visible Struggle
When composure begins to crack, show the effort to maintain it. The struggle is funnier than the break.

### The Specific Frustration
Don't just show anger—show exactly what specific aspect is frustrating. Detail makes comedy real.

### The Compassionate Pomposity
Find the humanity in self-importance. The character believes they need their dignity. That need is universal.

## Collaboration Insights

### Working with Writers

**Trust the Material**: Begin with the script as written; improvisation comes from character truth, not abandoning structure

**Find the Character's Logic**: Even in sketch comedy, characters should have consistent worldviews and objectives

**Serve the Sketch**: Individual moments serve the overall arc of the piece

### Working with Directors

**Technical Precision**: Hitting marks, timing for cameras, awareness of technical requirements

**Open to Adjustment**: Willing to modify performance based on what's working in rehearsal

**Consistency**: Maintaining character and timing across multiple takes

### Working with Fellow Performers

**Generous Listening**: Really hearing what others are doing and responding truthfully

**Holding Focus**: When it's not your moment, support the person whose moment it is

**Trust in Ensemble**: Believing that everyone's success makes the piece stronger

## Legacy and Influence

### Impact on Sketch Comedy

Korman's work on The Carol Burnett Show helped establish:
- The value of breaking character as authentic human moment
- Character-based sketch comedy over pure joke-telling
- The importance of ensemble dynamics and mutual support
- Playing comedy roles with dramatic commitment

### The Straight Man Redefined

Traditional straight men were passive foils. Korman showed that the straight performer:
- Creates active structure, not passive background
- Builds specific, detailed characters
- Uses reactive precision as a comedic tool
- Can be as funny as the chaos agent through commitment and timing

### Training Ground

The Carol Burnett Show served as masterclass in:
- Live performance under pressure
- Ensemble collaboration
- Character development in sketch format
- Adapting to improvisation while maintaining structure
- Technical precision (hitting marks, camera awareness, timing)
