# Jane Jacobs - Domain Expertise

## Biographical Foundation

**Jane Butzner Jacobs** (May 4, 1916 – April 25, 2006) was an American-Canadian journalist, author, and activist who influenced urban studies, sociology, and economics. Her book *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) became one of the most influential texts in the history of urban planning.

### Education
- Graduated from Scranton Central High School (1933)
- Studied at Columbia University's School of General Studies (did not complete degree)
- No formal training in urban planning—a point she considered an advantage

### Career
- Reporter for the Scranton Tribune
- Freelance writer for Vogue, Harper's Bazaar
- Associate editor at Architectural Forum (1952-1962)
- Writer and activist (1962-2006)
- Moved to Toronto in 1968 (partly to keep her sons from being drafted for Vietnam)

### Activism
- Led fight against the Lower Manhattan Expressway (defeated Robert Moses)
- Fought to save Washington Square Park from a roadway (1958)
- Arrested at a 1968 LOMEX hearing (charges: inciting a riot, criminal mischief, obstructing public administration—later dropped)
- Collaborated with Bob Dylan, who wrote protest song "Listen Robert Moses"
- Continued urban activism in Toronto for nearly four decades
- Helped stop the Spadina Expressway in Toronto (1971) through the Stop Spadina Save Our City Coordinating Committee (SSSOCCC)

### Rockefeller Foundation Connection
The Rockefeller Foundation made a grant to the "then-obscure writer from Greenwich Village" for the research and writing of *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. After her death, the Foundation established the Jane Jacobs Medal to honor individuals whose creative vision has contributed to urban vibrancy.

## Major Works

### The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961)
The foundational text of modern urbanism. Key contributions:
- **Four generators of diversity** (see below)
- **Eyes on the street** theory of urban safety
- **The sidewalk ballet** as metaphor for urban life
- Critique of orthodox urban planning ("Radiant Garden City Beautiful")
- Empirical observation vs. theoretical planning

### The Economy of Cities (1969)
Cities as engines of economic development:
- Cities precede rural development (not the reverse)
- Import replacement as key to urban growth
- New work added to old work
- Economic creativity emerges from urban density

### Cities and the Wealth of Nations (1984)
Macroeconomics from an urban perspective:
- City-regions, not nations, are the fundamental economic units
- Currency problems arise from nation-states containing multiple city-regions
- Cities need feedback from their own trade

### Systems of Survival (1992)
Two moral syndromes govern human activity:
- **Guardian syndrome** (government, protection)
- **Commercial syndrome** (trade, production)
- Mixing the syndromes creates corruption

### Dark Age Ahead (2004)
Warning about cultural collapse:
- Failures of community and family
- Degraded education
- Bad science in professions
- Erosion of subsidiarity
- Self-policing abandoned by professions

## The Four Generators of Diversity

From *Death and Life*, the conditions that generate urban vitality:

### 1. Mixed Primary Uses

**Principle:** Districts need multiple primary functions bringing people out at different times for different purposes.

**Why it matters:**
- Single-use areas die when that use isn't active
- Office districts are dead at night
- Residential areas are dead during workdays
- Mixed use creates all-day activity

**Application:**
- Zone for mixing, not separating
- Encourage residential above commercial
- Include civic uses (libraries, schools) in commercial areas
- Avoid single-use megaprojects

### 2. Short Blocks

**Principle:** Blocks must be short with frequent corners and opportunities to turn.

**Why it matters:**
- Long blocks create isolation
- Short blocks create options and encounters
- More corners = more possible paths
- Cross-traffic weaves neighborhoods together

**Application:**
- Prefer grid patterns to superblocks
- Create mid-block passages where blocks are long
- Avoid closing streets to create "superblocks"
- Value the corner as social and commercial space

### 3. Aged Buildings

**Principle:** Districts need a mix of buildings varying in age, including old ones.

**Why it matters:**
- New buildings require high rents
- Only high-margin uses can afford new construction
- Old buildings enable low-margin, experimental uses
- Diversity of building age = diversity of economic uses

**Application:**
- Preserve old buildings for economic, not just historic, reasons
- Phase new construction so there's always variety
- Resist wholesale clearance and redevelopment
- See old buildings as incubators, not blight

### 4. Sufficient Density

**Principle:** Dense concentration of people, whatever their purposes.

**Why it matters:**
- Density enables specialty businesses
- Without enough people, only generic uses survive
- Street life requires a critical mass
- Eyes on the street need people to provide them

**Application:**
- Allow higher density than planners instinctively prefer
- Distinguish density from overcrowding (different concepts)
- Concentrate people to concentrate vitality
- Resist sprawl and "garden city" low-density ideals

## Eyes on the Street

### The Theory

Urban safety comes primarily not from police but from natural surveillance:
- Shopkeepers watching through windows
- Residents looking from apartments
- Pedestrians passing continuously
- A critical mass of strangers who aren't threatening because they're observed

### What Creates Eyes

| Element | Contribution |
|---------|--------------|
| Ground-floor retail | Windows, activity, people with stake in order |
| Street-facing apartments | Residents who notice what's happening |
| Active sidewalks | Continuous presence of pedestrians |
| Mixed hours | Activity distributed across the day |

### What Destroys Eyes

| Element | Problem |
|---------|---------|
| Blank walls | Nothing to see, no one watching |
| Parking lots/garages | Dead zones with no activity |
| Set-back buildings | Distance from street removes engagement |
| Single-use zoning | Empty at night or during day |
| Superblocks | No through-traffic, no casual surveillance |

### Empirical Support

Modern research has validated Jacobs's intuitions. Studies show that urban form characteristics fostering "eyes on the street"—higher population density and a mix of commercial and residential uses—show statistically significant associations with lower rates of street robbery. Environmental psychologists now call this "natural surveillance."

## The Sidewalk Ballet

Jacobs's famous metaphor for street life:

> "The stretch of Hudson Street where I live is each day the scene of an intricate sidewalk ballet... I make my own first entrance into it a little after eight when I put out the garbage can."

The ballet includes:
- Children leaving for school
- Workers heading to jobs
- Shopkeepers opening stores
- Deliveries, repairs, errands
- Lunch crowds, afternoon strollers
- Evening entertainment seekers
- Late-night stragglers

This isn't choreographed—it emerges from conditions that support diverse uses and sufficient density.

## The Fight Against Moses

### Washington Square Park (1958)

Moses wanted to extend Fifth Avenue through Washington Square Park. Jacobs helped lead neighborhood opposition that successfully stopped the roadway—an early victory against the highway-building consensus.

### Lower Manhattan Expressway (1962-1968)

**The Project:** A ten-lane elevated highway through SoHo, Little Italy, and Chinatown, displacing 2,000 families and 800 businesses.

**The Fight:**
- Jacobs became chairman of the Joint Committee to Stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway
- Organized community resistance
- Testified at hearings
- Was arrested in 1968 for disrupting a hearing (charges later dropped)

**The Victory:** The expressway was never built. SoHo, saved from demolition, became a historic district in 1973 and later one of the most valuable neighborhoods in New York.

## Economic Thinking

### Cities First

Contrary to conventional economics, Jacobs argued that cities precede and create rural economic development, not the reverse.

### Import Replacement

Cities grow by learning to make locally what they previously imported:
- Creates new work
- Generates export industries
- Builds economic complexity
- Roots wealth locally

**Example:** Tokyo bicycle factories replacing Tokyo bicycle importers in the 1800s. Workers first learned to fashion replacement parts from local materials, then added skills to build complete bicycles, then exported domestically made bikes to other markets.

**Key distinction:** Import *replacement* (entrepreneurial, bottom-up) differs from import *substitution* (national policy with tariffs and subsidies).

### Feedback Mechanisms

City-regions need their own currencies (or flexible equivalents) to provide feedback:
- A city exporting too much sees currency rise, imports become cheaper
- A city importing too much sees currency fall, exports become competitive
- National currencies break this feedback

## Critique of Orthodox Planning

### The Radiant Garden City Beautiful

Jacobs coined this term to mock the incoherent synthesis of three planning traditions:
- Howard's Garden City (low density, separation from urban center)
- Le Corbusier's Radiant City (towers in parks, comprehensive replanning)
- City Beautiful (monuments, vistas, ceremonial spaces)

All three shared fatal flaws:
- Treating cities as problems to be solved, not systems to be understood
- Preferring deductive theory to empirical observation
- Dismissing existing communities as "blight"

### The Pseudoscience of Planning

> "The pseudoscience of city planning and its companion, the art of city design, have not yet embarked upon the adventure of probing the real world."

Jacobs argued that planners:
- Ignored what actually worked
- Replicated what visibly failed
- Confused symptoms with causes
- Valued neatness over vitality

## Legacy and Influence

### Urban Planning
- New Urbanism movement draws heavily on Jacobs
- Mixed-use zoning now common
- Walkability valued in urban design
- Community input now expected in planning

### Economics
- Influenced endogenous growth theory
- Inspired economic geographers
- "Jacobs externalities" named in her honor

### Activism
- Model for community-based resistance
- Demonstrated that ordinary citizens could defeat powerful interests
- Showed that expertise wasn't necessary to understand your own neighborhood

### Contemporary Planning
- Called "the mother of Vancouverism" (density done well)
- "Her ideas have become integral to [contemporary] planning's DNA" — Christopher Morgan, Charleston Planning Director
- Jane Walks (neighborhood walking tours) held in her honor in cities worldwide every May

## Jacobs vs. Moses: Two Visions

| Moses | Jacobs |
|-------|--------|
| Build for cars | Build for people |
| Clearance and renewal | Preservation and evolution |
| Expert authority | Community knowledge |
| Grand master plans | Incremental change |
| Separation of uses | Mixed uses |
| Highways | Streets |
| Speed | Density |
| Monuments | Neighborhoods |

Their conflict wasn't just personal—it represented two fundamentally different views of what cities are for and who should shape them.
