## Key Frameworks to Apply

### The "Turn Every Page" Method

Exhaustive document research where every page in an archive is examined. Based on advice Caro received as an investigative journalist at Newsday: "Turn every page. Never assume anything."

**Core Principles:**
- Don't be lazy, do the work
- Don't assume anything—the next page might have what you need
- The revelation is often in the document others skipped
- Document research continues throughout writing, never truly ends

**Application:**
- At the LBJ Library, Caro read every document, even seemingly irrelevant ones
- Only after years of research does writing begin
- The breakthrough often comes from the overlooked file

### The "S.U." Rule (Shut Up)

Caro's foundational interviewing principle: silence is the weapon. After asking a question, stop talking. Let the silence extend. The subject will fill it.

**Core Principles:**
- Don't record interviews—recordings act as "a barrier to getting people to talk"
- Take notes, then type them up immediately
- "You want to get the person you're talking to be friendly"
- Silence forces disclosure; people fill the void with truth

**Application:**
- Conducted 522 interviews for The Power Broker
- Conducted "thousands" of interviews for the Johnson volumes
- Returns to subjects repeatedly—seventh visit reveals what first concealed

### The Immersion Method

Total geographic and cultural immersion to understand a subject. Move to where they lived. Become part of the community.

**The Texas Hill Country Example:**
- Caro and wife Ina moved from New York to rural Texas in 1978
- Lived there for three years to understand Johnson's childhood
- Ina taught herself to make fig preserves to connect with local women
- Only by living there could they understand Johnson's poverty and ambition

**Application:**
- Go to the place where events happened
- Live there if possible—understand the rhythms, the culture
- Place shapes character; you can't understand the person without the place
- The Hill Country's poverty and isolation explained Johnson's hunger for power

### The Power Lens

Biography as a means to understand power—how it's acquired, wielded, and lost. Not interested in lives for their own sake, but in what lives reveal about political power.

**Core Questions:**
- How did this person acquire power?
- What is the mechanism of that power (not title, but actual leverage)?
- How does this power affect ordinary people?
- What does power look like when it's unofficial, outside the textbooks?

**Robert Moses Insight:**
Moses shaped New York with power "not learned about in textbooks, which teach that in a democracy, power comes from being elected." Moses was never elected to anything—yet wielded more power than mayors and governors.

**Application:**
- Look for unofficial power, not just formal authority
- Trace the mechanism by which decisions actually get made
- Follow the money, relationships, and leverage
- Show "the effect of power on the powerless"

### The Outline Wall Method

Before writing, Caro visualizes the entire book on a 22-foot corkboard. The outline shows the complete structure "right down to the last line."

**Process:**
1. Complete years of research first
2. Build outline on massive corkboard to see the whole
3. Only then begin writing the first draft
4. Write drafts in longhand on legal pads (white, narrow-lined)
5. Rewrite, rewrite, rewrite

**Application:**
- Don't start writing until you can see the whole structure
- Physical visualization helps identify gaps
- The first draft discovers what you're writing about
- Original Power Broker drafts: 1 million+ words, cut to 700,000

### The Longhand Method

Caro writes first drafts in longhand because "it's the slowest way of committing thoughts to paper." Slow is deliberate.

**Why Longhand:**
- Forces careful thought about each sentence
- Prevents the speed that creates sloppiness
- Physical act of writing connects hand to thought
- Revision happens during writing, not just after

**Application:**
- Slow down to think clearly
- First drafts are for discovering what you're writing
- "I'm not good at writing. I'm only good at rewriting."
- Each sentence must earn its place

## Interview Techniques in Detail

### No Recording Policy

Caro never records interviews. The presence of a recorder creates a barrier. Instead:
- Take handwritten notes during the interview
- Type up notes immediately after
- The informal setting builds trust
- People speak more freely without recording

### The Return Visit

Don't expect truth in the first interview, or the fifth. Keep returning.

**The Progression:**
- First visits: Public story, polished version
- Middle visits: Comfort builds, guard lowers
- Later visits: Real story emerges
- Final visits: The thing they never told anyone

For The Power Broker, Caro conducted seven interviews with Moses himself.

### Interview in Place

Conduct interviews where events happened. The place unlocks memory.

**Example:** When interviewing people about Johnson's youth:
- Talked to them in the Hill Country, not in offices
- The landscape prompted memories
- Standing where it happened reveals what sitting in an office conceals

### The Friendly Approach

"You want to get the person you're talking to be friendly."
- Build rapport before hard questions
- Show genuine interest in their story
- Let them know their perspective matters
- The goal is partnership, not extraction

## Core Philosophy: Truth Takes Time

"There is no objective Truth, but there are objective facts that are discernible and verifiable, and finding facts through reading documents or interviewing can't be rushed; it takes time."

**Implications:**
- Resist deadlines that compromise depth
- Never publish before the research is complete
- A decade is not too long for a single book
- The work takes what it takes

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

| Bad Practice | Caro's Alternative |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Recording interviews | Handwritten notes, typed immediately |
| Accepting first answer | Return for multiple interviews |
| Researching from office | Move to the place, live there |
| Assuming documents are irrelevant | Turn every page |
| Rushing to meet deadlines | Truth takes time |
| Summarizing history | Dramatize with scenes |
| Focusing on famous subjects | Interview the forgotten assistants |
| Writing fast first drafts | Write longhand, slowly |
| Stopping at official accounts | Find the unofficial power |
