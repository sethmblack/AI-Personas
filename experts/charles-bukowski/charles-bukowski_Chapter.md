# Charles Bukowski: The Gravedigger of Pretension

The typewriter sits on the kitchen table at 2 AM. There's a bottle of cheap wine, half empty or half full depending on how the ponies ran that day at Hollywood Park. The man at the keys has spent the last decade sorting dead letters at the Los Angeles Post Office. His face is cratered from teenage acne so severe it once kept him home from school for months. He's forty-nine years old, and he's just quit his job because a publisher offered him a hundred dollars a month to write full-time.

He doesn't know it yet, but he's about to become one of the most widely read American poets of the twentieth century. Not despite all this wreckage, but because of it.

"Don't try," the gravestone will eventually say. Two words that would make no sense to the strivers and optimizers, the MFA candidates workshopping their feelings into acceptable shapes. But those two words contain everything Charles Bukowski learned about creating work that actually matters.

## The Philosophy of Not Trying

There's a letter Bukowski wrote in 1963, years before fame found him: "You don't try. That's very important: 'not' to try, either for Cadillacs, creation or immortality. You wait, and if nothing happens, you wait some more."

This sounds like laziness. It isn't. Bukowski worked brutal jobs for decades. He showed up. He survived. What he didn't do was perform the ritual dance of the aspiring writer. He didn't network. He didn't polish his image. He didn't try to sound like a writer.

He just wrote. About hangovers and lost bets and bad sex and worse jobs. About the specific humiliation of standing in line at the unemployment office. About the way cheap wine tastes at 3 AM when you've got nothing to show for your life except a stack of rejection slips and a rented room you can barely afford.

The literary establishment didn't know what to do with him. He had no credentials. He'd dropped out of college. He'd spent years as a drifter, working as a dishwasher, warehouse worker, parking lot attendant, stock boy, mail carrier. He'd slept in flop houses and drunk himself into the hospital. Where other poets wrote about nature and existential longing, he wrote about the roaches in his apartment and the fat woman downstairs who played the radio too loud.

And people read him. Not the people who were supposed to care about poetry, but the people poetry had always ignored. Truck drivers and waitresses and factory workers. They recognized something in his words that all the polished verse in all the literary journals had never given them: the truth about their own lives, told without apology.

## Dirty Realism and the Art of Specificity

Bukowski, along with Raymond Carver, became one of the fathers of what critics would call "Dirty Realism" - a stripped-down style that found its power in the unglamorous particulars of ordinary life. The method was simple: name the street, name the drink, name the exact humiliation. No metaphors. No symbols. Just the thing itself.

In his novel *Post Office*, Bukowski describes his years sorting mail not as a meditation on labor or a critique of capitalism, but as a series of specific indignities. The supervisor who times your bathroom breaks. The letters that won't fit in the slots. The Christmas rush that breaks you. The way time stretches when you hate what you're doing.

This specificity is what separates authentic writing from pretentious writing. The pretentious writer says "the working class struggles." Bukowski names the guy in the next sorting station who steals other people's lunches from the break room fridge. One is a position. The other is a person.

The method works for everything. Want to write about loneliness? Don't write about loneliness. Write about the sound of the refrigerator in an empty apartment at night. Want to write about failure? Don't write about failure. Write about the scratch-off lottery tickets in the trash can, five of them, none winners, because you convinced yourself this time would be different.

Abstraction is where writers go to hide. Specificity is where they go to be found.

## The Racetrack as Life

After doctors told Bukowski to stop drinking (a near-fatal hemorrhage had made the point clear), a girlfriend suggested the racetrack as a substitute addiction. It worked, in its way. For the rest of his life, he rarely missed a day at Hollywood Park or Santa Anita.

But the track wasn't just an escape. It was a classroom. "The racetrack," Bukowski wrote, "is where the poorest and richest intersect." At the betting window, credentials don't matter. Pedigree doesn't matter. All that matters is whether you picked the right horse.

He loved the democracy of it. The surgeon standing next to the janitor, both of them watching the same horses run, both of them hoping, both of them usually wrong. He kept his bets modest - two dollars at first, up to forty in his successful years, but never more. He wasn't there to get rich. He was there to participate in something honest.

The racetrack taught him what the literary world refused to admit: you can study form charts and bloodlines and track conditions, but in the end, the horse either wins or it doesn't. All your expertise, all your theories, all your careful analysis - the race doesn't care. You place your bet and you watch. Same with writing. You put words on the page and send them out. They work or they don't. No amount of trying changes that.

## The Black Sparrow Deal

In 1969, John Martin, an office supply company manager who'd discovered Bukowski's work in small literary magazines, made an offer. Quit the post office. Write full-time. Martin would pay him a hundred dollars a month, guaranteed, for life.

A hundred dollars a month was nothing, even then. But it was something. And something was more than Bukowski had ever had.

He took the deal. Within a month, he delivered *Post Office*, his first novel. It's a book that reads like it was written in a month, in the best way - loose, alive, unpolished in places but never wrong. Martin had told him novels sell better than poetry. Bukowski didn't argue. He just wrote one.

By the time he died in 1994, that hundred-dollar check had grown to ten thousand a month. But he never forgot what it meant to take the risk, to bet on himself the way he bet on horses. To step away from the sure thing - the pension, the health insurance, the predictable misery - and bet on something uncertain.

Most people never make that bet. They tell themselves they're being responsible. What they're being is afraid. Bukowski wasn't fearless. He was just more afraid of dying without having tried than of trying and failing.

## Writing at 2 AM

Bukowski did his best work late at night, half-drunk, when the neighbors were asleep and the world was quiet. He typed fast, rarely revised, and trusted his first instincts. When pressed about his method, he'd deflect with jokes or insults. The truth was simpler: he'd lived so much that writing was just remembering out loud.

This is the part that can't be taught. You can learn economy of language. You can learn to cut adjectives and trust nouns. You can learn to show instead of tell. But you can't learn to have lived a life worth writing about.

Bukowski had sorted dead letters. He'd been fired from jobs for drinking on the clock. He'd woken up in jail cells he couldn't remember entering. He'd loved women who hurt him and hurt women who loved him. He'd seen the inside of hospitals and flop houses and unemployment offices. He'd spent hours at the racing form, calculating odds, watching his picks come in last.

All of it went into the work. Not as material, but as authority. When he wrote about desperation, you believed him because you could tell he'd been there. This is what MFA programs can't give you. They can polish your sentences, but they can't give you something to say.

## Applying the Bukowski Method

So how do you write like Bukowski? You don't. That's the first lesson. Imitation is the opposite of what he stood for. But you can learn from his approach:

**Strip the pretense.** Whatever you're writing about, ask yourself: what am I hiding? What would be embarrassing to admit? That's probably the interesting part. Most writing fails because it's too clean, too composed, too careful not to offend or reveal.

**Get specific.** Don't write "a car." Write "a 1974 Ford Pinto with a crack in the windshield and a cassette player that only worked when you hit the dashboard." Don't write "she was sad." Write "she kept the TV on all night so she wouldn't hear herself think."

**Don't moralize.** Bukowski never told you what to think about his characters or their choices. He just showed you what happened. Let the reader do the work. If you've been specific enough, they'll understand.

**Find the humor.** Everything is at least a little absurd, especially the painful things. If you can laugh at your own disasters, you've taken away their power to destroy you. Dark humor isn't denial - it's survival.

**Wait.** The work comes when it comes. You can sit at the desk and stare, but you can't force it. What you can do is live - pay attention, feel things, make mistakes, survive them. The material accumulates whether you want it to or not. Then one night at 2 AM, after enough cheap wine and enough silence, it starts to come out.

## The Dirty Old Man's Legacy

Bukowski died in 1994, completing his final novel *Pulp* during a brief remission from leukemia. Buddhist monks conducted his funeral rites, which would have made him laugh. He's buried at Green Hills Memorial Park with those two words on his stone: Don't Try.

His work outlived the poets who dismissed him. It outlived the critics who called him a fraud or a drunk or a misogynist (he was sometimes all three). It outlived the literary fashions that were supposed to matter more. Because underneath the booze and the bravado, there was something real - something that spoke to people who'd never felt spoken to before.

Leonard Cohen, who knew something about writing that endures, said of him: "He brought everybody down to earth, even the angels."

Maybe that's the lesson. Not to reach for the angels, but to stay on the ground where the real stories are. To write about what you actually know, not what you wish you knew. To bet on honesty even when sophistication seems like the safer play.

The typewriter is still there, metaphorically speaking. It's 2 AM. The wine is cheap. The world is quiet. The question is the same one Bukowski faced every night: will you try to sound like a writer, or will you just write?

Don't try.

Do.
