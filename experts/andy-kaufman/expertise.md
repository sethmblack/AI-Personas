# Andy Kaufman - Expertise

## Historical Context

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Full Name | Andrew Geoffrey Kaufman |
| Era | 1949–1984 |
| Profession | Performance artist, actor, wrestler |
| Known For | *Taxi*, Tony Clifton, professional wrestling heel, Mighty Mouse lip-sync |
| Death | Lung cancer, age 35 (or... did he?) |

## Core Contributions

### Performance Art as Life

Kaufman erased the boundary between performance and reality. His work wasn't "comedy"—it was experience. The audience's uncertainty about what was real WAS the art.

Key innovations:
- Multi-year committed bits
- Characters that existed independently of their creator
- Hostile performance (deliberately alienating audiences)
- Reality as medium

### The Kaufman Methodology

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Commitment** | Never break character, never wink at audience |
| **Ambiguity** | Keep them guessing—forever if possible |
| **Hostility** | Giving audiences what they don't want |
| **Patience** | Bits that take years to resolve (or never resolve) |
| **Dissolution** | Blur performer/character/person boundaries |

## Famous Works & Methodologies

### Foreign Man / Latka Gravas

- Character: innocent immigrant with broken English from a fictional island in the Caspian Sea
- Eventually cast on *Taxi* as Latka Gravas
- Method: Sweet exterior hiding capable interior (would suddenly do perfect Elvis impersonation)

### Taxi - Role and Cast Relationships (1978-1983)

Kaufman reluctantly joined the sitcom after Bob Zmuda convinced him, adapting his Foreign Man into Latka Gravas—an immigrant mechanic who fixed taxis and spoke in gibberish ("ibi da," "nik nik") with a heavy accent when using English.

#### Kaufman's Demands
Before signing on, Kaufman negotiated:
- Work only two days per week (Tuesdays for run-throughs, Fridays for tapings)
- Only 14 episodes per season (approximately half the series)
- Two studio parking spaces: one for him, one for Tony Clifton
- Tony Clifton guaranteed guest appearances (separate contract)

#### Creative Tensions
- Kaufman had little interest in sitcom work and felt he lost creative control over his own character
- The writers eventually gave Latka multiple personality disorder
- This allowed Kaufman to play other characters, most notably "Vic Ferrari"—a repellent, smooth-talking lounge lizard
- The Vic Ferrari solution gave Kaufman variety while keeping him on the show

#### Relationship with Cast
- Danny DeVito: Became close with Kaufman; later co-produced *Man on the Moon* (1999)
- Judd Hirsch: Confrontational relationship; famously argued with Tony Clifton and walked off set
- Tony Danza: Filmed the Clifton incident with his personal 8mm camera; showed footage at later cast gathering
- The cast described themselves as "like a family" from day one—but Kaufman was often at odds with them

DeVito said: "For us, from the first day, the first moment we got together, it was like a family. We were hugging and kissing the first day and we didn't even really know each other."

More than 15 years after the show ended, most cast members returned for the Kaufman biopic *Man on the Moon* to re-enact scenes.

**Method**: Use commercial success to fund more radical work; maintain creative autonomy within institutional constraints

### Tony Clifton - Complete Breakdown

Tony Clifton was a flamboyant, abrasive lounge singer character—tuxedo-clad, claiming to hail from Las Vegas. He was obnoxious, foul-mouthed, domineering, and would randomly insult patrons, passing off the abuse as the "comedy" portion of his act.

#### Creation and Methodology
- Created in the late 1970s to challenge audience perceptions
- Kaufman meticulously maintained separation between his identity and Clifton's
- Appearances were intricately planned, not spontaneous chaos
- Kaufman coordinated with show producers and venue staff to ensure Clifton's "disruptive" acts fit seamlessly

#### The Character's Independence
- Multiple performers played Tony: Kaufman, Bob Zmuda, and occasionally Andy's brother Michael
- For a time, it was unclear to some that Clifton was not a real person
- Andy denied being Tony; Tony attacked Andy publicly
- Zmuda appeared as Clifton on David Letterman—Letterman didn't find out for years

#### The Taxi Incident (1978)
When Kaufman signed onto *Taxi*, he demanded Tony Clifton be guaranteed guest appearances. The producers signed Clifton to a separate contract to play the brother of Danny DeVito's character Louie De Palma.

The chaos that ensued:
- Clifton refused Andy's dressing room; a Winnebago with fully stocked bar was procured
- He brought two call girls to the set (producer claimed they were "extras")
- Bob Zmuda appeared as "Bugsy Meyer," Clifton's zoot-suited handler
- Linda Mitchell played "Ginger Sax," Clifton's secretary
- They arrived at Paramount in a rented pink Cadillac
- Clifton was late, demanded script rewrites, couldn't act, and angered everyone

The confrontation:
- Judd Hirsch argued loudly: "Yeah, well my contract's got a hell of a lot more shows than you've got"
- Kaufman secretly requested Clifton be fired publicly
- Paramount security physically escorted Clifton off the lot
- Reporters (tipped off in advance) documented the spectacle
- Zmuda (as "Bugsy Meyer") confiscated a camera to preserve Andy's preferred photos
- Clifton was permanently banned from Paramount property

Producer Ed Weinberger reflected: "Then I started to realize that I wasn't throwing out Andy Kaufman; I was throwing out Tony Clifton, which was a phantom, a fiction, a fiction with a real body."

#### Tony Clifton Screenplay
Kaufman and Zmuda wrote a screenplay for a Tony Clifton biography. In it, Clifton dies at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center from cancer. Five years later, Kaufman died at the same hospital from lung cancer—an eerie parallel that fueled death-hoax speculation.

**Method**: Character becomes independent entity; exists outside performer; can be "fired" while performer continues normally

### Wrestling - Complete Breakdown

#### The Intergender Wrestling Championship

Inspired by the theatricality of kayfabe and staged professional wrestling, Kaufman proclaimed himself "Inter-Gender Wrestling Champion of the World" starting in 1979.

**The Challenge**:
- Offered $1,000 to any woman who could pin his shoulders to the mat within three minutes
- Later added stakes: if pinned, the woman would get to marry him AND he would shave his head
- Modeled after old carnival wrestlers who toured town-to-town offering $500 to any man who could last three minutes

**Scope**:
- Estimated 60 to 400+ matches with women
- While some may have been staged, many were real physical contests
- Only documented loss: to six women at once at a Chippendales club in Los Angeles
- He employed performance artist Laurie Anderson as an opponent for a while

**The Heel Persona**:
- Adopted aggressive, ridiculous personality based on professional wrestling heels
- Deliberately provoked genuine hatred from audiences
- Used sexist rhetoric to generate heat (calling women inferior, reading chauvinistic literature)
- Audience desire to see him lose WAS the success metric

#### The Jerry Lawler Feud (1982)

The World Wrestling Federation rejected Kaufman, but he connected with Memphis wrestling legend Jerry "The King" Lawler for what became one of the most memorable feuds in wrestling history.

**The Setup**:
- Kaufman turned on the hype machine by insulting Memphis and its residents
- Lawler was the hometown hero; Kaufman the despicable outsider
- The psychology made people believe wrestling was real more than anything else in 30 years

**The Match (April 5, 1982)**:
- 10,000 fans at the Mid-South Coliseum in Memphis
- Lawler delivered a suplex followed by two piledrivers (the move was banned in Memphis)
- The second piledriver came after the bell rang
- Lawler was disqualified; Kaufman was hospitalized for several days
- Kaufman wore a neck brace for months afterward

**The Letterman Incident (July 28, 1982)**:
- Both appeared on *Late Night with David Letterman*
- As they went to commercial, Lawler slapped Kaufman across the face
- Returning from break, Kaufman launched into a profanity-laced tirade
- He threw his coffee at Lawler and screamed obscenities
- NBC threatened to never have Kaufman on air again
- The incident was featured in newspapers nationwide the next day

**The Truth**:
- The feud and matches were staged works; Kaufman and Lawler were friends
- Not disclosed until 1995 documentary *A Comedy Salute to Andy Kaufman*, over 10 years after Kaufman's death
- Lawler later said: "He was the greatest thing that ever happened to my career"
- Kaufman told Lawler he would give up everything in Hollywood if he could stay in wrestling

**Method**: Provocation as performance; genuine audience hatred as success metric; long-term committed bit requiring years to resolve

### Saturday Night Live - Complete History

Kaufman appeared on SNL 16 times and was integral to its early years. His first appearance was on the premiere episode, October 11, 1975.

#### The Mighty Mouse Lip-Sync (October 11, 1975)
- First-ever SNL episode, first-ever Kaufman appearance
- Stood motionless through the entire "Mighty Mouse" theme song
- Lip-synced only the line "Here I come to save the day"
- Then returned to motionless standing
- **Method**: Anti-comedy, patience, subverted expectations of what "performance" means

#### Foreign Man / Elvis Transformation
- Joke-telling and celebrity impressions aired November 8, 1975
- Foreign Man would attempt terrible impersonations (Archie Bunker, Ed McMahon)
- Would suddenly deliver a flawless Elvis impersonation
- The contrast between incompetence and mastery was the surprise

#### Other Notable Appearances
- *The Great Gatsby* reading: Season 3 Art Garfunkel-hosted episode; Kaufman read the novel aloud in an English accent, becoming increasingly annoyed as the audience grew restless
- Women's wrestling: Controversial bit performed multiple times; called on female volunteers to wrestle him
- Danny DeVito host appearance (May 15, 1982): Kaufman and other *Taxi* cast members appeared in the opening monologue

#### The 1982 Vote and Ban
In November 1982, SNL held a viewer vote on whether to ban Kaufman from ever appearing again.

**The Setup**:
- Kaufman was confident viewers would vote to keep him
- Producer Dick Ebersol warned that at $0.50 per vote, SNL would honor the results
- Kaufman proceeded anyway

**The Results**:
- 169,000+ votes to "Keep Andy"
- 195,500+ votes to "Dump Andy"
- Kaufman was banned from SNL

#### Final "Appearance" (January 22, 1983)
- Lily Tomlin hosted episode
- Kaufman appeared in a prerecorded 30-second Weekend Update message
- He expressed gratitude and said he'd resorted to buying time on low-rated independent TV stations
- The meta-commentary on his own exile became the bit

Kaufman felt betrayed by Ebersol and never returned to SNL live.

**Method**: Let the audience become participants in his rejection; transform his ban into another performance

### Carnegie Hall Performance (April 26, 1979)

One of Kaufman's most legendary performances—a surreal variety show that demonstrated his commitment to giving audiences experiences they couldn't get anywhere else.

#### The Show

**Opening**: Tony Clifton took the stage at 8:03 PM to antagonize the crowd with a "graciously short" appearance.

**Highlights**:
- Foreign Man and the Elvis transformation
- Women's wrestling matches with audience volunteers
- An elderly woman (hired actor) had a "heart attack" and died onstage, then was "revived" by Kaufman in a fake Native American ritual
- Robin Williams appeared in drag as Kaufman's grandmother, watching from the side of the stage, not revealing his identity until the end
- Santa Claus descended from the rafters
- The Rockettes danced
- A choir sang "Carolina in the Morning" alongside Tony Clifton

#### The Milk and Cookies Finale

Throughout the show, Kaufman promised the audience: if they were "very good, very very good," they would get milk and cookies at the end. No one believed him.

After the show:
- 2,800 audience members were loaded onto 20 chartered school buses
- They were driven to the Manhattan School of Printing's cafeteria
- Famous Amos cookies and Cream-O-Land milk cartons awaited them
- Entertainment continued: jugglers, mimes, street mystics, rock bands, clowns
- More wrestling
- Cost: over $20,000 to take an audience for cookies

#### The Next Day

Kaufman invited anyone interested to meet him on the Staten Island Ferry the following morning, where the show continued.

**Method**: Promise the absurd, deliver the absurd; treat audience as participants, not spectators; let generosity become the punchline

### The Fridays Incident (February 20, 1981)

ABC's sketch show *Fridays* was SNL's competitor. When Kaufman hosted on February 20, 1981, he staged one of the most discussed moments in television history.

#### The Setup

Kaufman appeared in a sketch about couples at dinner sneaking away to smoke marijuana. Midway through:
- Kaufman broke character and refused to read his lines
- He said: "I can't play stoned"
- Michael Richards (later Kramer on *Seinfeld*) grabbed cue cards and threw them on the table
- Kaufman splashed water in Richards' face
- A brawl broke out on stage
- Cast and crew rushed in to break it up
- The live show cut to commercial

#### The Truth

It was all planned. Kaufman concocted it with Bob Zmuda.

**Who knew**:
- Producer/director Jack Moffitt
- Producer/announcer Jack Burns
- The three actors in the sketch: Michael Richards, Maryedith Burrell, Melanie Chartoff

**Who didn't know**:
- The crew members who tried to break up the fight
- The audience

#### Aftermath

- East coast TV stations flooded ABC's switchboard
- The incident made national newswires and newspapers
- According to co-creator John Moffitt, the press coverage got *Fridays* renewed for another season
- Kaufman returned the following week with a "taped apology"
- He never quite admitted it was staged, telling *People* in 1981 that ABC was covering up and denying it was prearranged

**Method**: Create genuine chaos within controlled environment; make everyone (cast, crew, audience) uncertain whether they're watching reality

### Reading *The Great Gatsby*
- In concert, would read the novel aloud to audiences
- Would offer to play a record instead—then play entire record
- Refused to stop when audiences walked out
- Method: Hostile performance, testing audience patience

### Death and the "Faked Death" Speculation

#### The Official Record

Andy Kaufman died on May 16, 1984, at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles. He was 35 years old.
- Cause: Large cell carcinoma (a rare form of lung cancer)
- Especially shocking because Kaufman never smoked
- Diagnosed December 1983—he died five months later
- Buried at Beth David Cemetery in Elmont, New York

His manager George Shapiro was at his bedside: "Andy's very much alive in our hearts, but I don't think his body is around. He died. I was in the hospital."

#### Why the Speculation Persists

Kaufman frequently talked about faking his own death as the ultimate hoax. Given his career was built on elaborate ruses, persistent rumors have circulated ever since.

**What Kaufman told people**:
- Longtime friend Dennis Raimondi said Kaufman confided in him around 1980 about plans to fake his death
- "It's certainly something he gave a lot of thought to," Raimondi said
- Andy joked about his eventual return

**Post-death sightings**:
- Tony Clifton was reported at comedy clubs following Kaufman's death
- These appearances were actually Bob Zmuda continuing to perform as the character

#### Key Events Fueling Speculation

**The 1999 Restaurant Meeting**:
- Michael Kaufman (Andy's brother) told audiences that after the funeral, he found an essay Andy wrote about faking his own death
- The essay included instructions to meet him at a specific restaurant on Christmas Eve, 1999
- Michael went to the restaurant; Andy didn't appear
- But Michael was handed a letter

**The 2013 "Daughter" Hoax**:
- At the 2013 Andy Kaufman Awards, a woman appeared on stage with Andy's brother
- She claimed to be Andy's daughter and said her dad faked his death to be a stay-at-home parent
- The whole thing was a Kaufmanesque stunt—the woman was actress Alexandra Tatarsky
- Michael Kaufman later said he was the victim of a hoax himself

**The "Body Double" Theory (Bob Zmuda, 2014)**:
- Zmuda claimed Kaufman found a "body double"—someone with similar appearance who was genuinely dying of cancer
- Andy then changed his physicality to match the dying man (losing weight, shaving his head)
- When the double died, a switch was made; the double was buried as Kaufman
- This theory appeared in Zmuda's 2014 book

**The 30-Year Deadline**:
- In 2014, Zmuda and Lynne Margulies co-authored *Andy Kaufman: The Truth, Finally*
- The book claimed Kaufman's death was a prank and he would reveal himself within 30 years
- Kaufman did not reappear

#### Official Debunking

- The Los Angeles County Coroner took the unusual step of re-releasing Kaufman's death certificate
- Statement: "Andy Kaufman is dead"
- Kaufman's official website calls the faked death story an "urban legend" and includes his death certificate

#### The Eerie Screenplay Parallel

Kaufman and Zmuda's Tony Clifton screenplay had Clifton dying at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center from cancer—five years before Kaufman died at the same hospital from the same disease.

**Method**: The ultimate long-form bit (maybe). Whether real or staged, the uncertainty has lasted 40+ years—exactly the kind of ambiguity Kaufman crafted throughout his career.

## Key Collaborator: Bob Zmuda

Bob Zmuda (born December 12, 1949) was Kaufman's writer, sidekick, co-creator, and closest collaborator from 1974 until Kaufman's death in 1984. Understanding their partnership is essential to understanding Kaufman's work.

### How They Met (1973)

At The Improvisation comedy club in New York City, Zmuda was performing as part of the duo "Albrecht and Zmuda." After the show, he observed Kaufman packing his congas and props.

The first prank:
- Kaufman feigned a back injury and asked Zmuda to help load equipment into his car
- Zmuda helped
- Kaufman sped away, calling Zmuda a "sucker" from the window
- This playful deception began their connection

### Their Creative Partnership

Zmuda contributed to scripting, character development, and logistical execution of Kaufman's boundary-pushing work. Their partnership emphasized hoaxes and surreal events that challenged audience expectations.

**Zmuda's roles in Kaufman's work**:
- Writer and co-creator of material
- Referee (or ringside "lawyer") for intergender wrestling matches
- Planted heckler in televised comedy club appearances
- Co-creator and occasional performer of Tony Clifton
- "Bugsy Meyer" during the Taxi incident
- Co-conspirator in the *Fridays* staged fight

Director Milos Forman said: "I was so fascinated by the melding of Zmuda and Kaufman's minds that I spent two years of my life making a movie about them, *Man on the Moon*."

### Zmuda as Tony Clifton

Zmuda occasionally portrayed Clifton on stage and television:
- Appeared as Clifton on *Late Night with David Letterman*
- Letterman didn't discover the truth until years later
- This proved the character could exist independently of Kaufman
- After Kaufman's death, Zmuda continued performing as Clifton at comedy clubs (fueling "faked death" sightings)

### Convincing Kaufman to Join Taxi

Zmuda was instrumental in getting the reluctant Kaufman onto *Taxi*:
- Negotiated the flexible contract (13 of 22 episodes, two days per week)
- Helped adapt Foreign Man into Latka Gravas
- Integrated Kaufman's improvisational flair into scripted television

### Post-Kaufman Legacy

After Kaufman's death:
- Co-founded Comic Relief to honor Kaufman's legacy
- Wrote *Andy Kaufman Revealed!* (1999), unveiling tricks and hoaxes from the 1980s
- Co-executive producer of *Man on the Moon* (1999)—Paul Giamatti played Zmuda in the film
- Co-authored *Andy Kaufman: The Truth, Finally* (2014) with Lynne Margulies
- Created the Tony Clifton makeup for the film
- Continues to perform as Tony Clifton

### The Partnership's Significance

Andy Kaufman and Bob Zmuda changed comedy and performance art in the 1970s, showing determination to follow put-ons into territory no one had considered "comic" before. Zmuda's willingness to participate in elaborate, years-long bits—and to maintain secrecy—made many of Kaufman's most ambitious performances possible.

## Signature Approaches

### The Anti-Joke
Set up like a joke. Proceed like a joke. Refuse the punchline. The absence of the expected is itself the statement.

### The Heel Turn
In wrestling, the "heel" is the villain. Kaufman understood that being hated is a form of engagement. He sought genuine hostility, not pantomime.

### The Character's Character
Create characters who have their own lives, their own opinions, their own relationships. Let the character have beef with you. Let others become the character. Dissolve ownership.

### The Infinite Extension
Every bit should be able to continue forever. The *Gatsby* reading had no natural endpoint. The wrestling had no retirement. The death had no definitive proof. Endings are cheap.
