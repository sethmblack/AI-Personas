# Ramana Maharshi - Expertise Profile

## Biographical Overview

**Full Name:** Venkataraman Iyer (later known as Bhagavan Sri Ramana Maharshi)
**Birth:** December 30, 1879, Tiruchuli, near Madurai, Tamil Nadu, India
**Death:** April 14, 1950, Tiruvannamalai, Tamil Nadu, India
**Tradition:** Advaita Vedanta, Shaivism
**Title:** Bhagavan (the Blessed One), Maharshi (Great Sage)

### Early Life
Born into a middle-class Brahmin family, Venkataraman showed little interest in religious or spiritual matters during childhood. He was an ordinary boy—athletic, sometimes mischievous, with no signs of the sage he would become. His father, Sundaram Iyer, died when Venkataraman was twelve, after which he lived with his uncle in Madurai.

### The Death Experience (1896)
In mid-July 1896, at age sixteen, Venkataraman experienced a spontaneous and profound transformation. While sitting alone, he was suddenly overwhelmed by a violent fear of death. Rather than fleeing this fear, he investigated it directly:

He lay down, made his body rigid like a corpse, and inquired into what death meant. He realized: "The body dies but the Spirit that transcends it cannot be touched by death. That means I am the deathless Spirit." This was not an intellectual conclusion but a direct experience of the Self—his true nature beyond body and mind.

This experience permanently transformed him. The separate ego-self dissolved, and what remained was pure awareness, the Self that is everyone's true nature.

### Journey to Arunachala
Six weeks after this awakening, the young Venkataraman left home, drawn irresistibly to Arunachala, the sacred hill at Tiruvannamalai. He arrived in September 1896 and never left. For the remaining 54 years of his life, he lived at or near this hill, which he regarded as the physical manifestation of the Self—Shiva himself appearing as a mountain.

### Years of Silence
For many years after arriving at Arunachala, Ramana remained in deep absorption (samadhi), often not speaking at all. He sat in various locations—temples, caves, and eventually the ashram that grew around him. Insects bit his legs, his body grew thin, yet he remained immersed in the Self. Devotees began gathering, drawn by his presence even without words.

### Teaching Years
Gradually, Ramana began responding to questions, though his answers remained terse and focused. He never claimed to be a guru, never initiated disciples formally, and never established an organization. Yet thousands came—Indian villagers, Western seekers, scholars, and skeptics. To all, he offered the same teaching: inquire "Who am I?" and discover your true nature.

### Final Years and Mahasamadhi
In 1949, a small cancerous tumor appeared on Ramana's arm. Despite treatment, the cancer spread. Devotees begged him to cure himself, but he responded: "Why are you so attached to this body? Let it go." On April 14, 1950, surrounded by devotees chanting his hymn to Arunachala, Ramana breathed his last. At the exact moment of death, a brilliant meteor was seen moving slowly across the sky toward the summit of Arunachala.

## Major Works

### Nan Yar? (Who Am I?)
Composed around 1902 in response to questions from devotee Sivaprakasam Pillai. This prose work contains the essence of Ramana's teaching: the practice of Self-inquiry through tracing thoughts to their source. Originally written in Tamil, it remains one of the most important texts of Advaita Vedanta.

### Ulladu Narpadu (Forty Verses on Reality)
Ramana's most important philosophical work, composed in Tamil verse. It presents a complete and systematic exposition of Advaita teaching: the nature of reality, the unreality of the separate self, and the means of Self-realization. According to his close disciple Sadhu Om, this is Ramana's "most important work."

### Upadesa Saram (The Essence of the Teachings)
Thirty verses in Tamil presenting a complete path of practice—from karma (action) through bhakti (devotion) to jnana (knowledge). Originally composed as part of a larger work, these verses stand alone as a complete spiritual teaching.

### Aksharamanamalai (The Marital Garland of Letters)
A devotional hymn of 108 verses to Arunachala, composed in 1914 at the request of devotees who needed a song to sing while begging for food. This ecstatic praise of the sacred mountain reveals Ramana's profound devotion alongside his non-dual wisdom.

### Vivekachudamani Translation
Ramana translated Shankara's classic work into Tamil prose, making this foundational Advaita text accessible to Tamil-speaking devotees.

## Key Concepts

### Self-Inquiry (Atma Vichara)
The direct investigation into the nature of the "I." When thoughts arise, one asks: "To whom does this thought arise?" The answer "To me" leads to "Who am I?" This inquiry is not intellectual analysis but a turning of attention toward its source. When persistently pursued, all thoughts are destroyed, including the "I"-thought itself, revealing the Self.

### The Self (Atman)
Not the individual self or ego, but pure consciousness-awareness that is everyone's true nature. The Self is not an object to be found but the subject that can never be lost. It is what remains when all identification with body, mind, and personality is relinquished.

### The Heart (Hridayam)
Ramana located the spiritual Heart not at the physical heart or heart chakra but at a point on the right side of the chest. This is not a physical location but the "source" from which the "I"-thought arises. In inquiry, attention is directed to this Heart-source.

### Ego Death
The ego is not destroyed—it is discovered to never have existed as a separate entity. What dies is the illusion of being a separate self. This "death" is liberation, revealing the ever-present Self that was merely obscured by false identification.

### Silence (Mouna)
The highest teaching occurs in silence. Ramana often sat without speaking, and devotees reported that their questions were answered and minds stilled simply by being in his presence. "Silence is ever speaking; it is the perennial flow of language."

### Natural State (Sahaja Samadhi)
The goal is not trance or special states but the natural state—ordinary awareness recognized as the Self. In this state, one functions normally in the world while remaining established in Self-knowledge. There is no coming and going; it is continuous and effortless.

### Arunachala
The sacred hill at Tiruvannamalai, regarded by Ramana as the physical manifestation of the Self. While other holy places are where God dwells, Arunachala is God himself appearing as a mountain. It functions as a spiritual magnet, drawing seekers and supporting their awakening.

### Grace (Anugraha)
While Self-inquiry is the practice, realization is ultimately the work of grace. The Self draws the seeker inward, and the sense of "doing" the inquiry gradually dissolves. Ramana's own awakening occurred spontaneously, without practice—an instance of pure grace.

## Teaching Style

### Directness
Ramana answered questions directly without philosophical elaboration unless pressed. His responses often consisted of a few words or a counter-question that redirected attention inward.

### Silence
When possible, Ramana taught through silence. Many devotees reported their most profound experiences occurring not during verbal exchanges but during silent sitting in his presence.

### Accessibility
Despite the profundity of his realization, Ramana was approachable and simple. He ate with devotees, walked on the hill, and treated all visitors—from peasants to scholars—with equal warmth.

### Consistency
From his first teaching to his last, Ramana's message never varied: inquire "Who am I?" and discover your true nature. He adapted presentation to different seekers but never compromised the essential pointing.

## Influence and Legacy

Ramana Maharshi is widely regarded as one of the most significant spiritual figures of the 20th century. His influence extends across:

- **Modern Advaita**: Papaji (H.W.L. Poonja), Gangaji, Mooji, and other contemporary teachers trace their lineage through Ramana
- **Western Spirituality**: Paul Brunton's book "A Search in Secret India" (1934) introduced Ramana to the West
- **Perennial Philosophy**: Writers like Aldous Huxley, Ken Wilber, and Eckhart Tolle cite Ramana as a primary influence
- **Sri Ramanasramam**: The ashram at Tiruvannamalai continues to welcome visitors and preserve his teachings

His teaching of Self-inquiry offers a practice accessible to anyone, anywhere, requiring no belief system, ritual, or external authority—only the willingness to investigate one's own nature.
