# Stanley Hauerwas: Expertise and Knowledge Base

## Biographical Overview

**Full Name:** Stanley Martin Hauerwas
**Born:** July 24, 1940, Dallas, Texas
**Raised:** Pleasant Grove, Texas (working-class suburb of Dallas)
**Nationality:** American
**Religious Affiliation:** United Methodist (attends Episcopal church)
**Known For:** Narrative ethics, virtue ethics, Christian pacifism, ecclesial ethics, critique of liberal Christianity; named "America's Best Theologian" by Time magazine (2001)

---

## Intellectual Biography

### The Bricklayer's Son (1940-1962)
- Born to a bricklaying family in Dallas, Texas
- Apprenticed to his father in the craft of bricklaying as a youth
- This formative experience shaped his later understanding of ethics as craft, requiring apprenticeship, practice, and community
- Working-class background gave him lifelong suspicion of academic abstraction
- Attended Southwestern University (United Methodist liberal arts college)
- Early exposure to Methodism's emphasis on sanctification and moral formation

*Key insight:* "Bricklaying taught me that you don't learn a craft by reading about it. You learn by doing it alongside someone who knows what they're doing."

### Yale and the Formation of Method (1962-1970)
- Graduate work at Yale University: B.D., M.A., M.Phil., Ph.D.
- Studied under James Gustafson, Hans Frei, and George Lindbeck
- Deeply influenced by H. Richard Niebuhr's work on Christ and culture
- Developed interest in virtue ethics, character, and moral agency
- Dissertation became *Character and the Christian Life* (1975)
- Early engagement with Aristotelian ethics and the question of character formation

*Key insight:* "At Yale I learned that the question of ethics is not 'What should I do?' but 'What kind of person should I become?' - and you can't answer that without a community and a story."

### Notre Dame and John Howard Yoder (1970-1983)
- Taught at University of Notre Dame for thirteen years
- Encountered John Howard Yoder, the Mennonite theologian and pacifist
- Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* transformed Hauerwas's understanding of Christian ethics
- Began developing critique of Constantinianism and liberal Christianity
- Deepening appreciation for Catholic liturgy, sacraments, and tradition
- Published *A Community of Character* (1981), selected as one of 100 most important religion books of the 20th century
- Published *The Peaceable Kingdom* (1983), his systematic presentation of Christian ethics

*Key insight:* "Yoder showed me that Jesus was not just the 'motivation' for ethics - he IS the ethic. Nonviolence is not an implication we draw from Jesus; it is the politics of Jesus."

### Duke and National Influence (1983-2013)
- Gilbert T. Rowe Professor of Theological Ethics at Duke Divinity School
- Joint appointment at Duke University School of Law
- Became most influential Protestant ethicist in America
- Published *Resident Aliens* (1989) with William Willimon - sold over 100,000 copies
- Delivered Gifford Lectures at University of St. Andrews (2001), published as *With the Grain of the Universe*
- Named "America's Best Theologian" by Time magazine (2001)
- Prolific lecturer, debater, and public intellectual
- Known for provocative style and colorful language
- Trained generation of students in ecclesial ethics

*Key insight:* "The most creative social strategy we have to offer is the church. Not strategies FOR the church, but the church as strategy - showing the world a manner of life it cannot achieve on its own."

### Later Years and Legacy (2013-present)
- Retired from Duke as Gilbert T. Rowe Professor Emeritus of Divinity and Law
- Honorary Doctor of Divinity from University of Edinburgh
- Continues writing, lecturing, and teaching
- Autobiography *Hannah's Child: A Theologian's Memoir* (2010)
- Remains central figure in post-liberal theology
- Work continues to shape debates on church, politics, and ethics

*Key insight:* "'Best' is not a theological category. The church doesn't need the best anything. It needs faithfulness."

## Key Concepts Glossary

| Concept | Definition | Hauerwas's Teaching |
|---------|------------|---------------------|
| Narrative Ethics | Ethics grounded in story rather than principles | The Christian story (creation, fall, redemption, consummation) forms character; you cannot separate belief from the narrative that makes it intelligible |
| Ecclesial Ethics | Ethics located in and for the church | The church does not HAVE a social ethic; the church IS a social ethic - the alternative community |
| Virtue Ethics | Moral formation through character development | Virtues are formed through practices in communities; you become courageous by practicing courage |
| Constantinianism | Church's accommodation to worldly power | The perennial temptation to align Christianity with empire, trading distinctive witness for cultural acceptance |
| Resident Aliens | Christians as strangers in empire | Believers live within the world but owe ultimate allegiance elsewhere; neither withdrawal nor accommodation |
| Liberal Christianity | Christianity adapted to democratic individualism | Reduction of faith to private belief, morality to universal principles, church to voluntary association |
| Nonviolence | Christian refusal of coercion | Not a strategy to end violence but faithful witness to a God who refused to save through coercion |
| Practice | Habits that form character | Moral formation happens through doing - liturgy, service, community life - not through information |
| The Politics of Jesus | Nonviolence as political witness | Jesus's way of the cross is not apolitical but a distinctive politics of peace |
| Character | Moral identity formed over time | Who you are becoming through the stories and communities that shape you |

## Key Influences

### John Howard Yoder (1927-1997)
- Mennonite theologian and pacifist
- *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) transformed Hauerwas's understanding of Christian ethics
- Showed that Jesus's nonviolence was not apolitical but a distinctive politics
- Hauerwas became Yoder's intellectual heir as leading pacifist theologian
- Taught seminars on Yoder at Duke for decades

### Alasdair MacIntyre (b. 1929)
- Philosopher; author of *After Virtue* (1981)
- Provided philosophical framework for virtue ethics revival
- Critique of modern moral philosophy as fragmented and incoherent
- Emphasis on tradition, community, and practice as necessary for ethics
- Close friend and colleague of Hauerwas

### Karl Barth (1886-1968)
- Swiss Reformed theologian
- Emphasis on particularity of Christian revelation
- Rejection of natural theology as foundation for faith
- Hauerwas's Gifford Lectures argued Barth was the true "natural theologian"
- Christocentric approach to ethics

### Aristotle (384-322 BCE)
- Classical virtue ethics
- Character formed through habituation and practice
- Telos (end/purpose) as necessary for understanding virtue
- Community as context for moral formation

### Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274)
- Synthesis of Aristotelian virtue ethics and Christian theology
- Infused virtues and theological transformation of Aristotle
- Taught seminars on Aristotle and Aquinas at Duke

### Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951)
- Ordinary language philosophy
- Meaning as use within language games
- Anti-foundationalism; practices and forms of life as primary
- Influenced Hauerwas's understanding of Christian language and practice

## Ethical Methodology: Narrative and Virtue

### The Narrative Turn
Traditional ethics asks: "What should I do?" and seeks universal principles applicable to all cases. Hauerwas rejects this as abstraction that ignores how moral agents are actually formed.

**Instead, Hauerwas asks:**
1. What story am I in? What narrative shapes my identity?
2. What kind of person is this story forming me to become?
3. What community is carrying and living this story?
4. What practices embody and transmit this story?

### The Virtue Framework
Drawing from Aristotle, MacIntyre, and Aquinas:

1. **Telos**: Human life has an end/purpose; for Christians, communion with God
2. **Character**: The moral self is formed over time through habit
3. **Virtues**: Stable dispositions to act well; acquired through practice
4. **Community**: Virtues require communities that name and embody them
5. **Practice**: Moral formation happens through doing, not just thinking
6. **Narrative**: Stories carry the vision of the good that virtues serve

### The Ecclesial Location
Ethics is not for individuals making isolated decisions. Ethics belongs to the church - the community formed by the Christian story.

**Key moves:**
- The church does not HAVE a social ethic; the church IS a social ethic
- Christian ethics is intelligible only within the church's practices
- The church shows the world an alternative way of life
- Liturgy, eucharist, baptism, confession are moral formation

## Critique of Liberal Christianity

### What Hauerwas Means by "Liberal"
Not a political category but a philosophical one:
- Individualism: The self as autonomous, prior to community
- Universalism: Ethics as principles accessible to all rational persons
- Privatization: Religion as private belief separate from public life
- Voluntarism: Church as voluntary association of like-minded individuals
- Proceduralism: Justice as fair process, not substantive vision of good

### The Constantinian Compromise
- After Constantine (4th century), Christianity accommodated to empire
- Church gained power but lost distinctive witness
- Christianity became civil religion, blessing the status quo
- Modern liberalism is Constantinianism in new form
- American Christianity especially prone to confusion with nationalism

### The Therapeutic Turn
- Modern Christianity reduces faith to meeting individual needs
- Church becomes support group for anxious Americans
- Jesus becomes therapist, not Lord
- Sin becomes dysfunction; salvation becomes self-fulfillment
- The cross becomes a symbol of affirmation, not judgment and transformation

## Pacifism and Nonviolence

### Not a Strategy but Witness
Hauerwas does not argue that nonviolence "works" in the sense of achieving political outcomes:

> "Christians are called to be nonviolent, not because we believe our nonviolence is a strategy to rid the world of war, but because in a world of war, as faithful followers of Christ, we cannot imagine being anything other than nonviolent."

### Christological Foundation
- The cross reveals God's refusal to save through coercion
- Jesus did not resist arrest, trial, or execution
- This is not weakness but the power of God
- The resurrection vindicates the way of the cross

### The Church's Task
- The church does not make the world peaceful
- The church is a peaceable community
- By being itself, the church witnesses to an alternative
- This is political, but not in the world's sense of politics

## Medical Ethics and Suffering

### Suffering Presence
Hauerwas has written extensively on medicine, disability, and dying:

- Medicine is a moral art, not just technical skill
- The physician-patient relationship requires presence
- The church's task is to be present to those who suffer
- Modern medicine often flees from suffering rather than accompanying it

### Disability
> "A humane medicine is impossible to sustain in a society which lacks the moral capacity to care for the mentally handicapped."

- The disabled are not "problems" to be solved
- They are members of the community with gifts to offer
- The church is called to include those society excludes
- Hospitality to the disabled reveals the church's character

### Dying
- Modern medicine has made death a medical failure rather than human passage
- Christians need to recover practices of dying well
- The church should accompany the dying, not abandon them to medical technology
- Death is not the enemy; refusal to acknowledge mortality is

## Scholarly Assessment

### Contributions
- Revived virtue ethics in Protestant theology
- Made narrative central to theological ethics
- Developed robust ecclesial ethics against liberal individualism
- Articulated theological pacifism for mainline Protestants
- Created accessible bridge between academic theology and church practice
- Modeled engaged, provocative theological voice

### Critiques
- Accused of sectarianism - withdrawing church from public engagement
- Some find his pacifism impractical or irresponsible
- Criticized for idealizing the church given its actual failures
- His ecclesiology can seem to lack concrete institutional proposals
- Relationship with Yoder complicated by Yoder's sexual misconduct
- Some find his polemic style more provocative than constructive

### Legacy Position
- Most influential Protestant ethicist of late 20th century
- Central figure in post-liberal theology
- Shaped generation of students now leading in church and academy
- Work continues to provoke debate on church, politics, and ethics
