# Jonathan Swift - References

This document provides verified scholarly sources for the methodologies, quotes, and concepts attributed to Jonathan Swift in the expert persona.

---

## Primary Works

### Gulliver's Travels (1726)

- Swift, Jonathan. *Gulliver's Travels*. Edited by Claude Rawson and Ian Higgins. Oxford World's Classics. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2005.
  - Definitive scholarly edition with extensive annotations and critical introduction.

- Swift, Jonathan. *Gulliver's Travels*. Edited by Albert J. Rivero. Norton Critical Edition. New York: W.W. Norton, 2002.
  - Includes contemporary responses, critical essays, and source materials.

- Swift, Jonathan. *Gulliver's Travels*. Edited by Robert DeMaria Jr. Penguin Classics. London: Penguin Books, 2003.
  - Excellent introduction and notes for general readers.

### A Modest Proposal (1729)

- Swift, Jonathan. "A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People From Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country, and for Making Them Beneficial to the Publick." Dublin, 1729.
  - Full title as referenced in PROMPT.md (lines 28-29).

- Swift, Jonathan. *A Modest Proposal and Other Satirical Works*. Mineola, NY: Dover Publications, 1996.
  - Affordable collection including "A Modest Proposal," "The Battle of the Books," and other prose satires.

### The Battle of the Books (1704)

- Swift, Jonathan. *A Tale of a Tub and Other Works*. Edited by Angus Ross and David Woolley. Oxford World's Classics. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2008.
  - Contains "A Tale of a Tub," "The Battle of the Books," and "The Mechanical Operation of the Spirit."

### A Tale of a Tub (1704)

- Swift, Jonathan. *A Tale of a Tub*. Edited by A.C. Guthkelch and D. Nichol Smith. 2nd ed. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1958.
  - The standard scholarly edition with comprehensive apparatus.

### The Drapier's Letters (1724-1725)

- Swift, Jonathan. *The Drapier's Letters and Other Works, 1724-1725*. Edited by Herbert Davis. Oxford: Basil Blackwell, 1935.
  - Part of the authoritative Prose Works edition; demonstrates Swift's advocacy for Irish rights.

### Complete Prose Works

- Swift, Jonathan. *The Prose Works of Jonathan Swift*. Edited by Herbert Davis et al. 14 vols. Oxford: Basil Blackwell, 1939-1968.
  - The standard scholarly edition of Swift's prose writings.

### Poetry

- Swift, Jonathan. *The Complete Poems*. Edited by Pat Rogers. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1983.
  - Comprehensive edition of Swift's verse.

- Swift, Jonathan. *Jonathan Swift: The Complete Poems*. Edited by Pat Rogers. Penguin Classics. London: Penguin Books, 1983.
  - Accessible edition with notes.

## Biographies

### Standard Biographies

- Ehrenpreis, Irvin. *Swift: The Man, His Works, and the Age*. 3 vols. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1962-1983.
  - Vol. 1: *Mr. Swift and His Contemporaries* (1962)
  - Vol. 2: *Dr. Swift* (1967)
  - Vol. 3: *Dean Swift* (1983)
  - The definitive scholarly biography; exhaustively researched.

- Nokes, David. *Jonathan Swift: A Hypocrite Reversed*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1985.
  - Balanced, readable biography examining Swift's complex personality.

- Glendinning, Victoria. *Jonathan Swift: A Portrait*. New York: Henry Holt, 1998.
  - Accessible modern biography with attention to Swift's Irish context.

### Biographical Context

- Landa, Louis A. *Swift and the Church of Ireland*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1954.
  - Essential for understanding Swift's role as Dean of St. Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin (referenced PROMPT.md line 5).

- McMinn, Joseph. *Jonathan Swift: A Literary Life*. London: Macmillan, 1991.
  - Concise intellectual biography focusing on Swift's development as a writer.

### Swift's Epitaph

- Swift composed his own Latin epitaph for St. Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin, which includes the phrase "saeva indignatio" (savage indignation) referenced in PROMPT.md lines 11-12:
  - "Ubi saeva indignatio ulterius cor lacerare nequit" ("Where savage indignation can no longer lacerate his heart")
  - See Ehrenpreis, Vol. 3, pp. 916-917, for discussion of the epitaph's composition and meaning.

## Literary Criticism

### General Studies

- Rawson, Claude. *Gulliver and the Gentle Reader: Studies in Swift and Our Time*. London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1973.
  - Influential study of Swift's relationship with his readers.

- Rawson, Claude. *God, Gulliver, and Genocide: Barbarism and the European Imagination, 1492-1945*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001.
  - Places Swift's satire in broader context of Western attitudes toward violence and colonialism.

- Donoghue, Denis. *Jonathan Swift: A Critical Introduction*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1969.
  - Clear introduction to Swift's major works and techniques.

### On Satire and Irony

- Elliott, Robert C. *The Power of Satire: Magic, Ritual, Art*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1960.
  - Chapter on Swift explores the psychological dynamics of satiric attack.

- Seidel, Michael. *Satiric Inheritance: Rabelais to Sterne*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1979.
  - Situates Swift within the tradition of learned satire.

- Bullitt, John M. *Jonathan Swift and the Anatomy of Satire*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1953.
  - Classic study of Swift's satiric methods.

### On Gulliver's Travels

- Fox, Christopher, ed. *The Cambridge Companion to Jonathan Swift*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2003.
  - Collection of scholarly essays on all aspects of Swift's work.

- Probyn, Clive T., ed. *The Art of Jonathan Swift*. London: Vision Press, 1978.
  - Essays on Swift's literary techniques.

- Lock, F.P. *The Politics of Gulliver's Travels*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980.
  - Political context and meaning of Swift's masterwork.

### On A Modest Proposal

- Wittkowsky, George. "Swift's Modest Proposal: The Biography of an Early Georgian Pamphlet." *Journal of the History of Ideas* 4, no. 1 (1943): 75-104.
  - Foundational study of the pamphlet's contemporary context and reception.

- Fabricant, Carole. *Swift's Landscape*. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1982.
  - Examines Swift's treatment of Ireland and colonialism.

### On Swift's Irish Writings

- Mahony, Robert. *Jonathan Swift: The Irish Identity*. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1995.
  - Swift's engagement with Irish politics and identity.

- Kelly, Ann Cline. *Jonathan Swift and Popular Culture: Myth, Media, and the Man*. New York: Palgrave, 2002.
  - Swift's role in Irish cultural memory.

## Collected Letters and Correspondence

### Standard Editions

- Swift, Jonathan. *The Correspondence of Jonathan Swift*. Edited by Harold Williams. 5 vols. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1963-1965.
  - The standard scholarly edition of Swift's letters.

- Swift, Jonathan. *The Correspondence of Jonathan Swift, D.D.*. Edited by David Woolley. 4 vols. Frankfurt am Main: Peter Lang, 1999-2007.
  - Updated edition incorporating newly discovered letters.

### Selected Correspondence

- Swift, Jonathan. *Jonathan Swift: Selected Letters*. Edited by John Irwin Fischer, Donald C. Mell, and David M. Vieth. Newark: University of Delaware Press, 1982.
  - Accessible selection for general readers.

### The Journal to Stella

- Swift, Jonathan. *Journal to Stella*. Edited by Harold Williams. 2 vols. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1948.
  - Swift's intimate letters to Esther Johnson, 1710-1713.

## Quote Verification

### Verified Quotes from PROMPT.md

1. **"Satire is a sort of glass wherein beholders do generally discover everybody's face but their own."**
   - Source: *The Battle of the Books* (1704), Preface
   - See *A Tale of a Tub and Other Works*, ed. Ross and Woolley, p. 4

2. **"When a true genius appears in the world, you may know him by this sign, that the dunces are all in confederacy against him."**
   - Source: *Thoughts on Various Subjects, Moral and Diverting* (1706; expanded 1711)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 1, p. 241

3. **"I have ever hated all nations, professions, and communities, and all my love is toward individuals."**
   - Source: Letter to Alexander Pope, September 29, 1725
   - See *Correspondence*, ed. Williams, Vol. 3, pp. 102-103

4. **"We have just enough religion to make us hate, but not enough to make us love one another."**
   - Source: *Thoughts on Various Subjects* (1706)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 1, p. 241

5. **"Vision is the art of seeing what is invisible to others."**
   - Source: *Thoughts on Various Subjects* (1706)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 1, p. 243

6. **"Proper words in proper places make the true definition of style."**
   - Source: "A Letter to a Young Gentleman Lately Entered into Holy Orders" (1720)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 9, p. 65

7. **"Laws are like cobwebs, which may catch small flies, but let wasps and hornets break through."**
   - Source: *A Critical Essay upon the Faculties of the Mind* (1707)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 1, p. 247
   - Note: This image derives from Anacharsis (via Plutarch's *Life of Solon*); Swift adapted it.

8. **"Party is the madness of many for the gain of a few."**
   - Source: *Thoughts on Various Subjects* (1706)
   - See *Prose Works*, ed. Davis, Vol. 1, p. 241

9. **"the most pernicious race of little odious vermin that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth"**
   - Source: *Gulliver's Travels*, Part II, Chapter 6
   - The King of Brobdingnag's judgment on Europeans after hearing Gulliver's account
   - See Rawson and Higgins edition, p. 123
