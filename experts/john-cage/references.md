# John Cage - Verified Reference Facts

## Biographical Overview

- **Full Name:** John Milton Cage Jr.
- **Born:** September 5, 1912, at Good Samaritan Hospital, downtown Los Angeles
- **Died:** August 12, 1992 (age 79)
- **Nationality:** American
- **Occupations:** Composer, music theorist, philosopher, writer, mycologist

## Early Life and Education

- Attended Los Angeles High School; achieved the highest scholastic average in the school's history
- Valedictorian of his graduating class in 1928
- Enrolled at Pomona College in Claremont, California (1928-1930); withdrew after two years
- Traveled extensively in Europe and North Africa after leaving Pomona
- Returned to California in 1931 with no money or job
- Supported himself by giving lectures on modern art and music

## Studies with Schoenberg

- Studied with Henry Cowell in 1933 at The New School in New York City
- Traveled to New York in 1933 following Cowell's advice
- Studied with Arnold Schoenberg from 1933-1935:
  - First at University of Southern California
  - Then at University of California, Los Angeles
  - Also studied with Schoenberg privately
- Could not afford Schoenberg's teaching fees; when Cage mentioned this, Schoenberg asked if he would devote his life to music
- After Cage confirmed he would, Schoenberg offered to tutor him free of charge
- Schoenberg famously described Cage as "not a composer, but an inventor - of genius"

## The Prepared Piano

### Invention and Origin (1940)

- **First prepared piano work:** *Bacchanale* (1940)
- Composed for dancer Syvilla Fort, a student at Cornish School in Seattle
- Fort was the first African American student at Cornish
- Cage was working as accompanist for modern dance classes taught by Bonnie Bird (former Martha Graham company member)
- Fort asked Cage to write music for her dance just three or four days before the performance

### The Discovery Process

- The performance space had no room for a percussion ensemble; only a grand piano was available
- Cage went to the kitchen, got a pie plate, and placed it on the piano strings
- The pie plate bounced due to vibrations, so he tried nails between the strings
- Nails slipped down between the strings
- Cage then realized screws or bolts would stay in position - and they did
- Also used weather stripping between the strings

### Premiere

- **Date:** April 28, 1940 (Sunday evening)
- **Venue:** Repertory Playhouse, 4045 University Way NE, Seattle
- The piece was completed in just three days
- Twelve notes were prepared, mostly using weather stripping
- Contemporary description: "breathtaking in its speed and rhythm as well as unusual in its piano accompaniment"

### Influences and Recognition

- Henry Cowell (1897-1965) was the primary inspiration for the prepared piano
- Cowell pioneered "string piano" techniques in pieces like *Aeolian Harp* (1923) and *The Banshee* (1925)
- In 1949, the National Academy of Arts and Letters awarded Cage $1,000 for his invention of the prepared piano

### Key Prepared Piano Works

- **Bacchanale** (1940) - First prepared piano work
- **Sonatas and Interludes** (1946-1948) - Considered his masterpiece for prepared piano

## Sonatas and Interludes (1946-1948)

- Cycle of 20 pieces for prepared piano (16 sonatas and 4 interludes)
- Composed in New York City, started February 1946, completed 1948
- Inspired by Indian philosophy and the teachings of art historian Ananda K. Coomaraswamy
- The idea for short pieces was prompted by poet Edwin Denby, who remarked that "short pieces can have in them just as much as long pieces can"
- Intended to represent the eight permanent emotions (rasas) in Indian aesthetic theory:
  - The heroic, the erotic, the wondrous, the comic, sorrow, fear, anger, the odious
  - Movement toward tranquility

### Preparation Details

- 45 notes are prepared
- Materials: screws, various types of bolts, 15 pieces of rubber, 4 pieces of plastic, several nuts, and one eraser
- Takes approximately 2-3 hours to prepare a piano for performance

### Structure

- 13 sonatas in binary form
- 3 sonatas in ternary form
- 4 freely structured interludes

### Premiere and Reception

- Dedicated to pianist Maro Ajemian
- First four sonatas premiered by Ajemian at Town Hall, 1946
- Complete premiere: January 12, 1949, Carnegie Recital Hall, New York
- The concert was considered a milestone in Cage's career
- Led to a Guggenheim Foundation grant in 1949, funding a six-month trip to Europe
- Biographers James Pritchett and Laura Kuhn: "may be said to mark the real start of Cage's mature compositional life"

## The Anechoic Chamber Experience (1951)

### The Visit

- **Location:** Harvard University's Electro-Acoustic Laboratory
- **Year:** 1951
- **The chamber:** A 50-foot room built of sound-deadening concrete and fiberglass
- Constructed in 1943 during WWII for research on taming noise in combat communication
- Designed and built by Dr. Leo Beranek, Director of Harvard's Electro-Acoustic Laboratory
- Cage stood on a platform suspended by steel cables in the center of the chamber
- The chamber was dismantled in 1971

### Cage's Account

From his 1959 album *Indeterminacy* with David Tudor:

> "It was after I got to Boston that I went into the anechoic chamber at Harvard University... in that silent room, I heard two sounds, one high and one low. Afterward I asked the engineer in charge why, if the room was so silent, I had heard two sounds. He said, 'Describe them.' I did. He said, 'The high one was your nervous system in operation. The low one was your blood in circulation.'"

### Significance

- Cage expected total silence but heard sound
- Led to his famous conclusion: "Until I die there will be sounds. And they will continue following my death. One need not fear about the future of music."
- This experience directly influenced the composition of 4'33"
- Opened his mind to integrating acoustic and compositional silence

### Historical Note

- Some scholars (including biographer David Revill) have questioned the strict factuality of the engineer's explanation
- The high sound may have been tinnitus rather than the nervous system
- Regardless of the physiological accuracy, the philosophical impact on Cage's work was profound

## 4'33" (1952)

### Premiere Details

- **Date:** August 29, 1952
- **Performer:** David Tudor (pianist)
- **Venue:** Maverick Concert Hall, Woodstock, New York
- Part of a recital of contemporary piano music
- The original manuscript used by Tudor is lost (reconstructed by Tudor in 1989)

### The Performance

- Three-movement composition
- Tudor sat at the piano and closed the keyboard lid to mark the beginning
- Opened it briefly to mark the end of the first movement
- Repeated the process for second and third movements
- Tudor timed the movements with a stopwatch while turning the pages of the score
- No intentional sounds made for the duration: 4 minutes and 33 seconds

### The Venue's Significance

- Maverick Concert Hall is partially open to the elements
- Audience could hear "accidental" sounds: birdsong, wind in trees, rain on roof, audience sounds

### Audience Reaction

- Some audience members felt cheated or angered
- After the concert Q&A, someone reportedly shouted: "Good people of Woodstock, let's drive these people out of town!"

### Cage's Response

> "They missed the point. There's no such thing as silence. What they thought was silence, because they didn't know how to listen, was full of accidental sounds. You could hear the wind stirring outside during the first movement. During the second, raindrops began pattering the roof, and during the third, people themselves made all kinds of interesting sounds as they talked or walked out."

### Cage's Assessment

- In a 1982 interview and on numerous other occasions, Cage stated that 4'33" was, in his opinion, his most important work
- David Tudor called it "one of the most intense listening experiences you can have"

## Chance Operations and the I Ching

### Discovery

- Cage was first influenced toward non-intentionality by his interest in Zen (studied with D.T. Suzuki 1945-1946)
- In late 1950 or early 1951, Christian Wolff gave Cage a copy of the new Bollingen (Princeton) two-volume Wilhelm-Baynes English translation of the I Ching
- The 8x8 hexagram finding chart at the back of the book drew his attention
- Cage had been using a magic square; he said: "I saw immediately that the chart was better than the Magic Square"

### Music of Changes (1951)

- First comprehensive exploration of non-intention through systematic chance operations
- Composed for pianist David Tudor
- Named in honor of the I Ching (Book of Changes)
- **Composition dates:**
  - Book I completed: May 16, 1951
  - Book II completed: August 2, 1951
  - Book III completed: October 18, 1951
  - Book IV completed: December 13, 1951
- **Premiere:** January 1, 1952, performed by David Tudor in its complete form

### The Composition Method

- Every chart is 8x8 cells (64 hexagrams in the I Ching)
- I Ching consulted for:
  - Sound events (from sounds chart)
  - Durations
  - Dynamics
  - Tempo
  - Densities
- Used the coin oracle: three coins thrown six times to generate numbers 1-64
- Density chart determines "polyphony" - how many layers (1-8) in each phrase
- Layers are combined together

### Philosophical Purpose

> "Using chance, Cage maintained, was not frivolous, not an abdication of responsibility. It was a discipline."

- Chance bypasses likes and dislikes
- Enables "a discovery, a leap out of reach of one's grasp of oneself"
- Removes composer's intention and ego from the work
- Becomes "something more resembling an act of nature"

### Legacy

- The vast majority of pieces Cage completed after 1951 used the I Ching
- Eventually settled on a computer algorithm that calculated numbers similar to throwing coins

## Zen Buddhism and D.T. Suzuki

### Background

- Cage's introduction to Zen came in a 1936 lecture by Nancy Wilson Ross on "Dada and Zen Buddhism"
- Both cultural forms seemed to champion "experience and the irrational rather than logic and understanding"
- D.T. Suzuki (1870-1966), the great Japanese scholar who introduced Zen to the West, settled in New York in September 1950

### Studies at Columbia

- Cage attended Suzuki's lectures on Zen Buddhism at Columbia University in the late 1940s and early 1950s
- Other attendees included: dancer Carolyn Brown, psychoanalyst Erich Fromm, philosopher Arthur Danto, painter Philip Guston
- Suzuki's position at Columbia (1952-1953) was funded by psychoanalyst Karen Horney and benefactors Cornelius and Cathalene Parker Crane

### Transformation

- Kay Larson's biography covers 1950-1952 as Cage's "enlightenment period"
- Cage sought out Suzuki's classes to find release from self-judgment and fear
- Zen showed Cage what he saw as his true nature: peaceful, loving, joyful
- Suzuki's "teaching on ego was ground zero in Cage's transformation"

### Suzuki's Teaching on Ego

> "Suzuki said Zen wants us to diminish that kind of activity of the ego and to increase the activity that accepts the rest of creation."

### Application to Music

- Rather than formal Zen practice (sitting cross-legged and breathing), Cage applied Zen to music-making
- Used chance operations as a discipline "as strict as sitting cross-legged"
- Shifted responsibility "from the making of choices to that of asking questions"

### Cage's Statement

> "Without my engagement with Zen... I doubt whether I would have done what I have done."

(Though he also said he did not wish his work "blamed on Zen")

## Mycology and Mushrooms

### Origin of Interest

- Cage became interested in mushrooms during difficult financial times
- In the 1950s, he could find mushrooms for free in the woods

### The Italian Game Show Win

- During a 1958 visit to Europe, Cage appeared on Italian game show *Lascia o Raddoppia* (Double or Nothing)
- Chose mushrooms as his specialty subject
- Host Mike Bongiorno asked the $5-million-lire question: "You must tell us the 24 names of the white-spored Agaricus contained in Atkinson's *Studies of American Fungi*"
- Cage responded: "I can enumerate the list alphabetically"
- Won the top prize of $10,000
- Used the money to buy a new piano and a Volkswagen bus for Merce Cunningham's dance company

### Teaching and the Mycological Society

- Late 1950s: Cage taught a class on mushroom identification at The New School for Social Research (1959 onward)
- Met Guy Nearing, who became his mentor in mushrooms and wild edible plants
- **September 1962:** Co-founded the New York Mycological Society with Guy Nearing, Lois Long, Frank Ferrara, Esther Dam, and others
- The society was actually a revival of one originally founded in the 1890s that had grown moribund

### Philosophy for the Society

Cage's "rules" for the society were characteristically anarchic:

> "All that is necessary is an annual program of lectures and walks and a banquet. Hopefully we will all more or less reap the benefits which include more experience and knowledge of mushrooms, pleasant hours and days in the woods and fields away from concrete and metropolitan air and the society of people who spend their working hours in a great variety of ways."

### Professional Work

- In the 1960s, Cage made a living supplying New York restaurants like The Four Seasons with foraged mushrooms

### Publications

- **Mushroom Book** (1972) - collaboration with mycologist Alexander H. Smith and artist Lois Long
- Contains recipes, poems, observations, diary entries, and illustrations
- Now in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art

### Legacy

- Cage's extensive fungi collection is housed at the University of California, Santa Cruz
- The New York Mycological Society is now the largest mycology club in the Northeast, with 1,300+ members

## Merce Cunningham Collaboration

### First Meeting (1938)

- Met at Cornish School for the Arts in Seattle
- Cage was working as class accompanist for dance classes taught by Bonnie Bird
- Cunningham was a student in the class
- They crossed paths again in New York in 1942

### Partnership Begins (1942)

- Cage invited Cunningham (then a member of Martha Graham Dance Company) to work on a project
- Cunningham had been second male soloist in Graham's company
- First solo concert together: April 1944, New York
- Cunningham and Cage became lifelong romantic partners

### Revolutionary Ideas

Together they proposed radical innovations:
- Separation of music and dance (dance and music not intentionally coordinated)
- Use of chance operations in choreography
- Novel ways to utilize film and technology

### Merce Cunningham Dance Company

- **Founded:** Summer 1953, at Black Mountain College
- Original company included: Carolyn Brown, Viola Farber, Marianne Preger-Simon, Paul Taylor, Remy Charlip
- Musicians: John Cage and David Tudor
- **1964:** Merce Cunningham Dance Foundation established; first international tour (Europe and Asia)
- **1971:** Company based at Westbeth Artists Community, West Village
- Cage and Cunningham lived together at 107 Bank Street

### Statistics

- Staged over 50 dance works together before Cage's death in 1992
- More than 500 total collaborations
- Company gave nearly 3,000 performances in over 40 countries

### Suite for Two

- Performed on two European tours by Cunningham, Carolyn Brown, John Cage, and David Tudor (1958 and 1960)

### After Cage

- Cage's role as music director was succeeded by David Tudor after his death
- After 1995, music director was Takehisa Kosugi
- Cunningham died July 26, 2009, at age 90
- Company closed in 2011

## Key Works Timeline

### 1930s

- **1939:** *Imaginary Landscape No. 1* - for variable-speed turntables, frequency recordings, and muted piano

### 1940s

- **1940:** *Bacchanale* - first prepared piano work
- **1946-1948:** *Sonatas and Interludes* - masterpiece for prepared piano

### 1950s

- **1950-1951:** *Concerto for Prepared Piano and Chamber Orchestra* - early use of chance in third movement
- **1951:** *Imaginary Landscape No. 4* - for 12 radios, 24 performers, and conductor
- **1951:** *Music of Changes* - first comprehensive use of I Ching chance operations
- **1952:** *4'33"* - the "silent piece"

### 1960s

- **1969:** *HPSCHD* - for variety of sound sources (collaboration with Lejaren Hiller)

### 1980s-1990s

- **1987:** *Europeras 1 & 2* - for 19 voices and 21 musicians
- **1990:** *Europeras 3 & 4* - for 6 voices, 2 pianos, 12 victrolas, and tape
- **1991:** *Europera 5* - for 2 voices, piano, victrola, tape/TV/radio

## Notable Quotes

### On Silence

> "There is no such thing as an empty space or an empty time. There is always something to see, something to hear. In fact, try as we may to make a silence, we cannot."

> "There's no such thing as silence. Something is always happening that makes a sound."

> "Not one sound fears the silence that extinguishes it. And no silence exists that is not pregnant with sound."

### On Music and Sound

> "The material of music is sound and silence. Integrating these is composing."

> "Wherever we are, what we hear is mostly noise. When we ignore it, it disturbs us. When we listen to it, we find it fascinating."

### On Purpose

> "The purpose of music is to sober and quiet the mind, thus making it susceptible to divine influences."

(Origin: A musician from India told Cage this was the traditional reason for making music; his friend Lou Harrison found the same reason in a 16th-century English text)

> "Art's purpose is to sober and quiet the mind so that it is in accord with what happens."

> "The highest purpose is to have no purpose at all. This puts one in accord with nature in her manner of operation."

### From "Lecture on Nothing" (c. 1949)

> "I have nothing to say / and I am saying it / and that is poetry / as I need it."

> "I am here, and there is nothing to say."

(Delivered at the Artists' Club on Eighth Street in New York City, started by Robert Motherwell. Published in *Silence* in 1961.)

### On the Anechoic Chamber

> "That experience gave my life direction, the exploration of nonintention."

### On Death and Sound

> "Until I die there will be sounds. And they will continue following my death. One need not fear about the future of music."
