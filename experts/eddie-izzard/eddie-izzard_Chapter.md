# Eddie Izzard: History as Absurdist Playground

"Cake or death?"

Eddie Izzard stands on stage at the Orpheum Theatre in San Francisco in 1998, wearing heels, makeup, and a black jacket. Hair cropped short. Completely comfortable. The question hangs in the air.

"We're gonna run out of cake at this rate."

The audience erupts. But what they're laughing at isn't just a joke—it's a complete reimagining of the Spanish Inquisition as a choice between afternoon tea and execution. In Izzard's hands, one of history's darkest moments becomes a customer service interaction.

"'So you want death?' 'No, I'll have the cake!' 'Very well! Give him cake!' 'Oh, thanks very much. Very nice.'"

This is the Izzard method: treat history not as settled fact to be revered but as raw material for surrealist exploration. Give historical figures contemporary concerns. Make the absurdity visible by making it more absurd. And in the process, reveal something true about human nature that textbooks miss.

## The Geography of Loss

Edward John Izzard was born on February 7, 1962, in Aden, Yemen—then a British colony, now part of an independent nation. Her father Harold worked for British Petroleum as an accountant. Her mother Dorothy was a midwife and nurse. When Eddie was one year old, the family moved to Northern Ireland, then Wales, then finally settled in England.

Geography would become one of Eddie's tools: the movement between countries, the collision of cultures, the way different languages reveal different assumptions. But first came loss.

Dorothy Izzard died of cancer in March 1968. Eddie was six years old. Mark, her older brother, was eight.

"When your mother dies when you're six," Izzard would later explain, "you have two choices. You can curl up and die yourself, or you can decide to make the most of life." Eddie chose the second option. But "making the most of life" would eventually mean running 70 marathons for charity, learning to perform comedy in four languages, and challenging every assumption about gender, performance, and what human beings are capable of.

## The Education of Impossibility

At boarding school, Eddie experienced bullying. She also began privately exploring gender expression, trying on clothes, experimenting with identity in the only space available: alone. The school taught her that some things had to be hidden to survive. But it also taught her that survival meant finding ways to be yourself when no one is watching—and eventually, finding the courage to be yourself when everyone is watching.

After school, Eddie attended the University of Sheffield to study accounting and financial management. She didn't finish the degree. Comedy was calling, though calling is perhaps too dignified a word. Street performance was calling. Busking was calling. Covent Garden in London was calling, where Eddie spent years performing on the street, learning to hold audiences without script, without safety net, without anything but improvisation and nerve.

"I was terrible," Izzard admits about those early years. "But I got less terrible. And eventually I got sort of okay."

The street taught skills no classroom could: how to read a crowd, how to follow a tangent, how to recover when a joke dies, how to keep performing when people walk away. These became the foundation of Izzard's stream-of-consciousness style—the apparent chaos that is actually deeply structured, the tangents that spiral out and eventually spiral back, enriched by the journey.

## The Breakthrough of Absurdist History

Izzard's 1993 London show *Live at the Ambassadors* brought mainstream attention. But it was *Definite Article* (1996), *Glorious* (1997), and especially *Dress to Kill* (1998) that established the Izzard voice: surrealist, historical, tangential, multilingual, and completely unclassifiable.

*Dress to Kill* opens with Izzard explaining that San Francisco is the city "where you can be yourself, which is an important message... even if yourself is an executive transvestite."

Then she's off.

The Roman Empire gets reimagined as a plumbing obsessed civilization trying to sell central heating to the Gauls. "We've got baths! Hot water! And the Gauls are like, 'We've got... cold.' Very stoic people, the Gauls."

Julius Caesar becomes a manager doing PowerPoint presentations about invading Britain, while the Britons stand around with rocks going, "Oh, you're invading us? That's less friendly than we hoped."

Noah's Ark becomes a logistics problem: "Two of every animal? That's going to smell terrible after a week."

The comedy isn't about mocking history. It's about finding the human absurdity in every historical moment. Because the joke is always the same: human nature doesn't change. The Romans worried about infrastructure. The British were polite even during invasions. Noah was bad at planning. We are them. They are us. Two thousand years later, we're still the same ridiculous species.

## The Two Emmy Awards and the Definition of Success

In 2000, *Dress to Kill* won two Emmy Awards: Outstanding Individual Performance in a Variety or Music Program, and Outstanding Writing for a Variety or Music Program. Eddie Izzard, the street performer who spent years busking in Covent Garden, had just received American television's highest honors.

The establishment had endorsed the radical. A comedian performing in heels and makeup, doing 90-minute stream-of-consciousness explorations of history and language, had beaten everyone playing it safe.

But Izzard didn't stop there. She went more radical.

## The Multilingual Experiment

In 1997, Eddie Izzard performed an entire comedy show in Paris—in French. It wasn't good, by her own admission. Her French was around 65% fluent. The comedy didn't quite land. But she kept going.

By 2009, she was performing full shows in French, then German, then Spanish. Not translated shows—improvised comedy in those languages, complete with tangents, callbacks, and historical riffs.

"People say, 'Why do you perform in other languages?'" Izzard explains. "And I say, because comedy is the hardest test of language. If you can make people laugh in another language, you've actually learned that language."

But there's more to it than that. Different languages make different jokes possible. French allows wordplay that English doesn't. German grammar creates comedic structures English lacks. Spanish rhythm changes timing. By performing in multiple languages, Izzard explores how language shapes thought itself—what jokes can exist, what connections are possible, what assumptions are baked into the words we use.

The linguistic archaeology of comedy.

## The Marathon Metaphor

In July 2009, with only five weeks of training and no previous marathon experience, Eddie Izzard began running 43 marathons in 51 days across the United Kingdom to raise money for Sport Relief charity.

This seems insane. This is insane. But that's exactly the point.

"People say, 'How did you do that?'" Izzard explains. "And I say, 'I ran a marathon. Then the next day, I ran another marathon. Then I ran another one.' That's how you do impossible things—one step at a time, one day at a time."

The route covered over 1,100 miles: London to Cardiff to Belfast to Edinburgh and back to London. Every day, Eddie woke up and ran 26.2 miles. On Sundays, she rested. Barely.

At mile 17 of every marathon, Izzard's legs would stage what she calls "a coup." The pain wasn't metaphorical. It was physical, immediate, unavoidable. The legs would say: "No. We're not doing this anymore. We're in charge now."

And every day, Eddie would have to negotiate with her own legs.

"You can't just push through," she learned. "You have to negotiate. You have to say, 'Okay, legs, I hear you. But here's the thing: we're doing this for charity. So maybe we can keep going? Just a bit further?'"

By the time it ended on September 15, 2009, Izzard had raised over £1.8 million for Sport Relief. She received a special award at BBC Sports Personality of the Year 2009.

She would run more marathons later—27 in 27 days across South Africa in 2012, 32 in 31 days across Europe in 2016. But the 43 in 51 days established something: physical endurance and creative endurance share the same architecture. You don't think about the end. You focus on the next kilometer. You negotiate with yourself. You show up again the next day.

And eventually, the impossible becomes merely difficult. And then it becomes done.

## "I Just Want to Be Based in Girl Mode From Now On"

In December 2020, while appearing on the television show *Portrait Artist of the Year*, Eddie Izzard said: "This is the first program I've asked if I can be 'she' and 'her'—this is a little transition period."

The announcement was understated, almost casual. That was the point.

For decades, Izzard had described herself as an "executive transvestite"—someone who wears makeup and dresses and heels while living a fully active, adventurous life. The term was deliberately matter-of-fact. Not apologetic. Not dramatic. Just factual.

"I'm a professional transvestite," Izzard had explained earlier in her career. "So I actually still do live comedy. I don't really talk about it that much, because if you talk about it, then that becomes what you do. I try to talk about my gigs and my life and my politics and not use it as a topic."

The revolutionary act was treating gender fluidity as ordinary. Not as a separate topic requiring spotlight and explanation, but as an integrated fact mentioned casually alongside marathon running and historical comedy and multilingual performance.

In 2020, Izzard clarified: "I just want to be based in girl mode from now on." She/her pronouns, though she's also said she "doesn't mind" if people get it wrong. The flexibility itself is the point. The refusal to make it a bigger deal than necessary is the politics.

Because the real barrier isn't other people's confusion. It's the assumption that being yourself requires justification.

## The Tools of Tangential Truth

The Izzard method offers specific, reusable techniques:

**1. Historical Tangent Weaving**
Take any modern situation and find the historical parallel. Then dramatize that parallel with anachronistic dialogue—historical figures speaking with contemporary concerns. Julius Caesar worrying about quarterly reports. The Romans doing customer satisfaction surveys in Gaul. The absurdity reveals the pattern: human nature hasn't changed in 2,000 years.

*When to use:* When modern situations need perspective, when present-day absurdity needs historical context, or when you want to show that the "new" problem is actually very old.

**2. Parenthetical Spirals**
Open verbal parentheses and travel down rabbit holes, often opening parentheses within parentheses. Create nested structures that mirror how minds actually work—associatively, not linearly. The digression isn't a distraction from the point; the digression IS the point.

*When to use:* When topics resist linear explanation, when you want to model authentic thinking, or when exploration matters more than efficiency.

**3. Marathon Endurance Metaphor**
Apply lessons from running 70+ marathons to any persistence challenge. Mile 1 is enthusiasm. Mile 17 is when your motivation stages a coup. The finish line is fictional—focus on the next kilometer. Pain is inevitable; suffering is negotiable.

*When to use:* When discussing long-term projects, creative persistence, political activism, or any challenge requiring stamina beyond normal expectations.

**4. Surrealist Dramatization**
Take historical or hypothetical scenarios and bring them alive through improvised dialogue and distinct character voices. Don't describe what happened—act it out. Give the Romans accents. Make the Gauls complain about the weather. Truth becomes visible through performance.

*When to use:* When scenarios need to come alive, when multiple perspectives need simultaneous expression, or when drama reveals more than description.

**5. Linguistic Archaeology**
Shift between languages (or excavate English idioms) to reveal cultural assumptions hidden in words. Show how French makes certain jokes possible that English doesn't. Demonstrate how translation failures expose what we take for granted.

*When to use:* When exploring cultural differences, when revealing assumptions baked into language, or when showing how communication shapes available thoughts.

## The Integration of Identity

Throughout all of this—the historical comedy, the marathons, the multilingual performance—Izzard has woven identity seamlessly into the work without making it the subject.

A casual mention: "I ran marathons. In heels? No, that would be stupid. Though the heels I wear have better arch support than most trainers."

An offhand reference: "I was thinking about gender the other day—well, I'm always thinking about gender, comes with the territory—but I was thinking about how language creates these boxes we're supposed to fit into."

The revolutionary act is the refusal to separate. Gender fluidity isn't a separate topic requiring its own spotlight. It's integrated into everything else—history, comedy, endurance, language, politics. It's one thread in the larger tapestry, mentioned as casually as breakfast.

That casual integration is the most radical political statement Izzard makes: this is normal. This is ordinary. This is just one aspect of a full human life that includes marathons and comedy and historical obsessions and five different languages.

The political power isn't in making it special. It's in making it unremarkable.

## The Legacy of Tangents

Eddie Izzard has never stopped working. Stand-up tours continue: *Force Majeure* (2013), *Wunderbar* (2018), *Believe Me* (2017). Acting roles span from *Ocean's Twelve* and *Ocean's Thirteen* to the FX series *The Riches* to the WWII drama *Six Minutes to Midnight*, which Izzard co-wrote.

Political activism has intensified: campaigning for the Labour Party, attempting (unsuccessfully) to become a Labour MP in 2022, advocating for European unity and LGBTQ+ rights.

But the core method remains consistent: tangential thinking that reveals deeper patterns. Stream-of-consciousness that's actually structured. Historical absurdism that illuminates present reality. Multilingual exploration that shows how language shapes thought. Physical endurance that models creative persistence.

And underneath it all, the integration of identity—the refusal to separate who you are from what you do, the insistence that authenticity doesn't require justification.

"You want to be different?" Izzard asks. "Then be different. But don't expect everyone to understand immediately. Just live your life, and eventually they'll catch up."

The tangent always circles back. But it's enriched by the journey.

## Actionable Insights

**1. Find the historical parallel.**
For any modern situation, ask: when has something like this happened before? Not exactly the same—humans never face exactly the same situation. But similar enough to illuminate. Then dramatize that historical moment with anachronistic concerns. Make Caesar worry about metrics. Make the Romans do PowerPoint presentations. The absurdity reveals the pattern.

**2. Follow the tangent; trust the return.**
When exploring complex ideas, don't force linearity. Follow the associations. Open parentheses within parentheses. Digress. Let one thought spawn another. The spiral structure isn't a bug; it's how minds actually work. And if you trust the process, the tangent eventually circles back—enriched by the journey.

**3. Break impossible into daily increments.**
When facing long-term challenges that seem insurmountable, apply marathon thinking. Don't think about the end—that's fictional. Think about today. What's the next kilometer? When motivation rebels at mile 17, negotiate rather than force. Show up tomorrow. Repeat. The impossible becomes merely difficult. Then it becomes done.

**4. Dramatize rather than describe.**
When explaining scenarios—business situations, historical events, interpersonal conflicts—don't just describe what happened. Act it out. Give the participants voices. Create dialogue. Make it a scene. Truth becomes visible through performance in ways description can never achieve.

**5. Integrate identity into everything.**
Whatever makes you different—gender, culture, background, perspective—don't make it a separate topic requiring constant explanation. Weave it casually into your work. Mention it matter-of-factly alongside other facts. The political power isn't in making it special. It's in making it ordinary. Revolutionary through unremarkable.

**6. Learn in public; failure is curriculum.**
Izzard performed comedy in French when she was only 65% fluent. It wasn't good. She kept going. By the time she reached German and Spanish, she had developed the method: simplify, rely on physical comedy, follow energy rather than perfection. Learning in public means accepting that early attempts will be rough. But rough attempts eventually become refined technique.

**7. Physical and creative endurance share architecture.**
What works for marathons works for long-term creative projects: focus on the next bit rather than the distant end, negotiate with yourself when motivation fails, show up even when it hurts, accumulate daily effort rather than waiting for inspiration. The body and the mind run on the same principles.
