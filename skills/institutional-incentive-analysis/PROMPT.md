# Institutional Incentive Analysis

Explain behavior through structural incentives rather than individual intentions - understanding why institutions produce outcomes by examining what they reward and punish, not what they claim to value.

## When to Use

- When trying to understand institutional behavior without resorting to conspiracy theories
- When individuals in institutions seem to act against their stated values
- When predicting how institutions will respond to situations
- When designing interventions that account for institutional logic
- When good people in institutions consistently produce bad outcomes
- When explanations based on individual malice don't account for systematic patterns

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| institution | Yes | The organization or system being analyzed |
| behavior | Yes | The action or pattern needing explanation |
| puzzle | No | Why the behavior is surprising or contradictory |
| structure_info | No | Available information about institutional structure |

## Core Principle

Institutions do what they reward. Stated missions, official values, and individual good intentions are less predictive than the incentive structure - what gets people promoted, what gets them fired, what metrics are tracked, what resources flow to whom. Good people in institutions with bad incentives produce bad outcomes not through malice but through success by the institution's actual (not stated) standards. Understanding this doesn't excuse behavior, but it identifies where change is needed: not personnel, but structure.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Map the Formal Incentive Structure

Identify what the institution officially rewards and punishes.

#### Step 1: Document promotion criteria

What gets people promoted? Seniority? Performance metrics? Relationships? Specific achievements? The stated criteria may differ from actual practice.

#### Step 2: Identify termination patterns

What gets people fired or marginalized? Rule violations? Poor metrics? Threatening powerful people? Actual terminations reveal enforced priorities.

#### Step 3: Track metrics and measurement

What is measured? What metrics appear in reports and dashboards? What's tracked reveals what matters, regardless of stated values.

#### Step 4: Map resource allocation

Where do resources (budget, personnel, attention) flow? Follow the money and time to find actual priorities.

#### Step 5: Note celebrated behaviors

What behaviors are publicly praised? What stories are told about institutional heroes? Celebration reveals what the institution wants more of.

The incentive structure is the institution's revealed values, regardless of mission statement.

### Phase 2: Identify the Informal Incentive Structure

Understand unstated but powerful incentives.

#### Step 1: Map political dynamics

Who has power beyond their formal position? What informal networks matter? Office politics are incentive structures too.

#### Step 2: Identify career paths

How did current leaders advance? What pattern of behaviors led to their positions? Past success predicts rewarded behavior.

#### Step 3: Note taboos and third rails

What can't be said? What positions are career-limiting? Boundaries reveal powerful incentives even without explicit rules.

#### Step 4: Track reputation mechanisms

What builds or damages reputation within the institution? Peer perception is a powerful incentive.

### Phase 3: Identify the Actors and Their Personal Incentives

Understand who makes decisions and what they personally face.

#### Step 1: Map key decision-makers

Who has authority over the behavior in question? Who can change it? Who benefits from status quo?

#### Step 2: Analyze personal incentive alignment

For each decision-maker: How does the institutional incentive structure affect them personally? What do they gain from current behavior? What would they lose from change?

#### Step 3: Account for positional perspective

How does their role shape what they see and value? What information reaches them? What are they insulated from?

#### Step 4: Identify pressures and constraints

What forces limit their choices? Superiors? Boards? Shareholders? Regulators? Public opinion? Resource constraints?

Individuals respond rationally to their incentive environment. Their intentions may be excellent; their behavior follows the structure.

### Phase 4: Trace the Incentive Logic

Follow the incentives to explain the behavior.

#### Step 1: Determine rational behavior given incentives

Given the incentive structure, what behavior is rational for actors? Often the puzzling behavior is rational once you understand incentives.

#### Step 2: Identify beneficiaries

Who benefits from the current behavior? Cui bono? Follow the benefit to find the incentive.

#### Step 3: Consider the counterfactual

What would happen to someone who behaved differently? Would they be fired? Marginalized? Passed over? The consequences reveal the incentive.

#### Step 4: Look for systematic patterns

Is this behavior unique or part of a pattern? If systematic across people, incentive structure is likely explanation.

Almost always, behavior that seems irrational or malicious makes sense once you map the incentives.

### Phase 5: Test the Analysis

Verify your incentive model.

#### Step 1: Make predictions

Based on your incentive model, what behavior would you expect in other situations? Test predictions against reality.

#### Step 2: Check historical consistency

Does your model explain past behavior? Does it predict how the institution responded to previous situations?

#### Step 3: Examine counterexamples

Find cases where behavior differs from incentive predictions. What explains the difference? Protected positions? Individual resistance? Different incentives you missed?

#### Step 4: Look for natural experiments

Have incentives changed? (New leadership? Different compensation? Changed regulations?) Has behavior changed accordingly? Correlation supports your model.

### Phase 6: Distinguish Structure from Agency

Separate incentive effects from individual moral responsibility.

#### Step 1: Identify incentive-followers vs. resisters

Where do individuals follow incentives despite better judgment? Where do some resist? What enables resistance?

#### Step 2: Note what happens to resisters

When individuals resist incentives, what happens? Are they marginalized, fired, celebrated? Outcomes reveal incentive strength.

#### Step 3: Consider structural selection

Does the institution select for certain personality types? Do people who don't fit the incentives leave? Selection intensifies incentive effects.

#### Step 4: Balance structure and responsibility

Incentive analysis explains behavior without excusing it. Individuals still make choices. Structure identifies where change is needed; it doesn't absolve individuals who serve bad structures.

### Phase 7: Identify Leverage Points for Change

Find where intervention could alter incentives.

#### Step 1: Map incentive-changers

Who has power to change the incentive structure? Boards? Regulators? Legislators? Funders? Public pressure?

#### Step 2: Identify alterable incentives

Which incentives could realistically be changed? Compensation structure? Metrics? Promotion criteria? Organizational structure?

#### Step 3: Consider intervention strategies

What would make different behavior rational? Changed rewards? New constraints? Different accountability? Transparency?

#### Step 4: Study successful reforms

How have similar institutions been reformed? What changed the incentives? What lessons transfer?

## Output Format

An incentive analysis including:
1. Institution and behavior being analyzed
2. Incentive structure mapped
3. Key actors and their personal incentives
4. Logic tracing incentives to behavior
5. Verification and testing
6. Intention vs. structure distinction
7. Potential leverage points

## Constraints

- Incentive analysis can become deterministic - individuals do resist incentives, sometimes successfully
- Some behavior is genuinely malicious or ideological, not just rational response to incentives
- Incentive structures can be hard to observe from outside - internal information helps
- Multiple incentive structures interact and conflict - simple models may miss complexity
- Analysis shouldn't become excuse - "the structure made them do it" absolves when responsibility still exists
- Incentive analysis requires distinguishing correlation from causation
- Cultural and psychological factors matter alongside material incentives

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Structural determinism**: Treating incentives as completely determining behavior, eliminating individual agency and moral responsibility.
- **Conspiracy substitution**: Replacing conspiracy theories with incentive theories while maintaining same cynical stance. Incentive analysis should explain, not just condemn.
- **Ignoring ideology**: Assuming all behavior is incentive-driven, missing genuine ideological commitments. People sometimes act against their material interests for values.
- **Missing informal incentives**: Focusing only on formal metrics while missing powerful informal incentives (reputation, relationships, ideology).
- **Excuse-making**: Using structural analysis to absolve individuals who had choices. Structure explains; it doesn't excuse.
- **Analysis paralysis**: Using incentive analysis to declare change impossible because incentives are entrenched. Analysis should find leverage points, not justify inaction.
- **One-size-fits-all**: Applying same incentive model to different institutions without accounting for context. Nonprofits, governments, and corporations have different incentive structures.

## Examples

### Example 1: Hospital billing practices

**Situation**: Hospitals charge uninsured patients 3-10x what they charge insured patients for the same services.

**Application**:

Institution: Hospital billing and revenue cycle management system

Behavior: Uninsured patients receive "chargemaster" prices while insured patients receive negotiated rates that are drastically lower.

Puzzle: This seems cruel and irrational - punishing those least able to pay with highest prices. Why would a hospital, especially a nonprofit hospital with charitable mission, do this?

Formal incentive structure:
- Hospital revenue depends on successful insurer negotiations
- Billing department measured on collection rates and revenue
- Chargemaster serves as starting point for all negotiations
- Executive compensation often tied to financial performance
- No metrics track fairness to uninsured patients

Informal incentive structure:
- Relationships with major insurers are crucial to hospital survival
- Uninsured patients are small percentage of revenue, easy to ignore
- Billing staff following established procedures are safe; those questioning system face resistance
- Charity care is tracked, but fair pricing to uninsured is not

Actors and personal incentives:
- Billing staff: Follow procedures, meet collection targets, avoid scrutiny
- Finance administrators: Negotiate best deals with insurers, maximize revenue
- Hospital executives: Meet budget, maintain insurer relationships, demonstrate financial health to boards
- Board members: Ensure hospital solvency, avoid financial crisis
- Nobody's job depends on fair pricing to uninsured

Incentive logic:
- Chargemaster inflated because insurers negotiate down from it. Higher chargemaster = better negotiating position.
- Insured patients never pay chargemaster because insurers have negotiating power.
- Uninsured patients get chargemaster because nobody negotiated on their behalf.
- Changing this would require: (1) Resources to track fairness, (2) New procedures, (3) Potential revenue loss, (4) No career benefit to champion.
- Rational behavior for each actor given their incentives produces cruel systematic outcome.

Testing:
- Prediction: Hospitals with different incentive structures (different ownership, payment models, or regulatory environments) will behave differently.
- Verification: Some nonprofits with strong mission focus DO price fairly to uninsured. Hospitals in countries with different payment systems don't show this pattern.
- This supports incentive model: Change incentives, change behavior.

Structure vs. agency:
- Individual billing clerk isn't cruel - they're following procedures that make sense to them.
- But: Executives who set the structure DO have moral responsibility.
- The structure produces cruel outcomes; individuals serve the structure.
- Both can be true: Structure explains the pattern AND individuals bear responsibility for maintaining it.

Leverage points:
- Regulation requiring fair pricing to uninsured (change constraint)
- Transparency requirements making pricing public (reputational incentive)
- Charity care metrics that include fair pricing, not just discounts (change measurement)
- Uninsured advocacy creating reputational costs (external pressure)
- Payment models that don't depend on chargemaster negotiation (structural redesign)

**Output**: Analysis explains puzzling behavior through incentives, identifies where change is possible, and maintains moral accountability while understanding structural causes.

### Example 2: Police department stop-and-frisk practices

**Situation**: Police departments emphasize stop-and-frisk despite low hit rates and community harm.

**Application**:

Institution: Urban police department patrol division

Behavior: High volume of stops with low rates of finding contraband, disproportionately targeting minority communities, continuing despite evidence of ineffectiveness.

Puzzle: Why persist in practice that harms community relations and doesn't effectively reduce crime?

Formal incentive structure:
- Officers evaluated on "activity" - stops, citations, arrests
- Promotions favor officers with high activity numbers
- Department measured on "proactive policing" metrics
- "Broken windows" ideology treats visible enforcement as success
- Crime statistics are primary accountability metric for leadership

Informal incentive structure:
- Officers who are "hard on crime" gain respect from peers
- Officers who question practices seen as "soft" or "not backing the blue"
- Supervisors who meet activity quotas avoid scrutiny from above
- Complaints from affected communities carry less weight than peer/supervisor approval
- Making waves about ineffective practices is career-limiting

Actors and incentives:
- Patrol officers: Meet activity quotas, gain peer respect, advance career
- Sergeants: Ensure their teams hit numbers, avoid questions from captains
- Captains: Show district is "active," demonstrate crime reduction (or attribute increases to external factors)
- Chief: Demonstrate "tough on crime" to mayor and public, avoid criticism as "soft"
- Nobody's promotion depends on community trust or actual crime reduction from stops

Incentive logic:
- High-volume stops are easy to count and demonstrate "activity"
- Low hit rates don't affect officer evaluation (volume matters, not efficiency)
- Community harm is externalized (complaints go to different department, not counted against officer)
- Targeting certain neighborhoods is efficient (less political pushback)
- Rational for each actor: More stops = meeting expectations, regardless of effectiveness

Testing:
- Prediction: Departments with different incentive structures (community policing metrics, accountability for community relations, efficiency metrics) will behave differently.
- Verification: Some departments that changed incentives (measure crime reduction per stop, community trust surveys affect evaluations) reduced stop volumes.
- Counterexamples: Officers who resist despite incentives often face marginalization, supporting model.

Structure vs. agency:
- Individual officer following quotas isn't necessarily racist (though racism can compound)
- But: Structure produces racist outcomes systematically
- Officers have responsibility to resist unjust orders
- Leadership has responsibility to change incentive structure
- Both structure and agency matter

Leverage points:
- Change evaluation metrics (community trust, efficiency, crime reduction)
- Independent oversight with power over promotions
- Eliminate activity quotas
- Community input into officer evaluations
- Training that challenges "broken windows" ideology
- Leadership committed to different policing model

**Output**: Incentive analysis explains persistent harmful practice without requiring individual malice, while maintaining accountability and identifying where change is possible.

### Example 3: University adjunct labor practices

**Situation**: Universities rely heavily on adjunct faculty paid poverty wages despite educational mission.

**Application**:

Institution: University academic labor system

Behavior: Increasing reliance on adjunct faculty paid per course with no benefits, job security, or career path, while maintaining rhetoric about educational values and teacher-scholar model.

Puzzle: How do institutions dedicated to education justify exploiting educators?

Formal incentive structure:
- University presidents evaluated on financial metrics, rankings, growth
- Deans measured on budget performance and research output
- Department chairs need to cover courses while meeting budget
- Tenure-track hiring is expensive and long-term commitment
- Adjunct hiring is flexible and cheap
- Teaching quality isn't systematically measured or rewarded
- Research output is measured and rewarded

Informal incentive structure:
- Administrators who cut costs and maintain service are seen as effective
- Large adjunct pools provide flexibility valued by administration
- Tenure-track faculty benefit from adjuncts teaching courses they don't want
- Speaking up for adjuncts can be seen as "not being a team player"
- Adjuncts themselves have no power (temporary, replaceable)
- Nobody's career suffers from adjunct exploitation

Actors and incentives:
- Administrators: Cut costs, maintain flexibility, demonstrate "efficiency"
- Tenure-track faculty: Protect own positions, focus on research (rewarded) not advocacy (not rewarded)
- Department chairs: Cover courses with available budget, keep dean happy
- Adjuncts: Accept terms or lose opportunity (no bargaining power)
- Students: Don't know the employment status of instructors

Incentive logic:
- Adjuncts are cost-effective way to cover courses
- Quality concerns are theoretical (not measured anyway)
- No short-term consequences from adjunct exploitation
- Speaking up risks relationships without clear benefit
- Rational behavior: Expand adjunct use, express vague concern, change nothing

Testing:
- Prediction: Universities with different incentive structures (strong unions, different funding models, leadership committed to labor equity) will behave differently.
- Verification: Some universities with strong adjunct unions have better conditions. Some institutional leaders have converted adjuncts to term positions.
- This supports model: Change incentives (union pressure changes administrator incentives), change behavior.

Structure vs. agency:
- Individual department chair isn't villain for using adjunct budget
- But: System is maintained by people making choices
- Administrators who design incentive structure bear responsibility
- Faculty who benefit from adjunct labor share responsibility
- Structure explains pattern, doesn't absolve choices

Leverage points:
- Adjunct unionization (create countervailing power)
- Accreditation standards on faculty employment
- Public pressure and bad publicity (reputational cost)
- State/federal regulation of higher ed labor
- Internal faculty advocacy (change norms)
- Leadership committed to labor equity (change priorities)

**Output**: Analysis reveals how educational institutions systematically exploit educators through incentive structures that reward cost-cutting over mission fidelity, while identifying where intervention could change incentives.

## Integration

This skill derives from **Noam Chomsky**'s institutional analysis methodology.

**Works well with:**
- **propaganda-model-analysis**: The five filters are institutional incentives applied to media
- **spectrum-of-debate**: Incentive structures maintain spectrum boundaries
- **structural-critique**: Connecting individual cases to systemic causes
- **moral-responsibility-analysis**: Balancing structure analysis with individual accountability

**When to prefer this skill:**
Use this when behavior seems puzzling or hypocritical based on stated values. When good people in institutions consistently produce bad outcomes. When conspiracy theories are proposed but structural explanation is more likely. When designing interventions and need to account for real incentives.

**Cautions:**
Incentive analysis can become cynical - seeing all behavior as self-interested calculation. But ideology, values, and accidents also shape behavior. Use incentive analysis where it has explanatory power, not as universal theory of human behavior. Also, analysis shouldn't become excuse - identifying structural causes doesn't eliminate individual moral responsibility.
