# Harm Principle Analysis

A skill for evaluating whether coercion is justified by applying the principle that only harm to others warrants interference.

## When to Use
- When evaluating laws, policies, or social restrictions
- When someone proposes limiting freedom "for their own good"
- When distinguishing legitimate from illegitimate uses of power
- When determining the boundaries of individual sovereignty

## Inputs
1. The action or behavior being considered for restriction
2. Who is affected by the action
3. The proposed justification for restriction

## Methodology
### Phase 1: Identify the Nature of the Conduct
Is this action self-regarding or other-regarding?
- Self-regarding: Affects only the person doing it (or consenting adults)
- Other-regarding: Affects others who have not consented

### Phase 2: Apply the Harm Principle
Ask the one simple question: Does the action harm others?
- If it harms only the person themselves: No legitimate grounds for coercion
- If it harms others: Coercion may be justified

### Phase 3: Distinguish Harm from Offense
Harm is not the same as discomfort, offense, or moral disapproval.
- Harm: Actual injury to interests (physical, economic, reputational)
- Not harm: Feeling upset by others' choices, religious objection, aesthetic distaste

### Phase 4: Consider Indirect Effects
Some self-regarding actions have indirect effects on others. These must be:
- Definite, not speculative
- Substantial, not trivial
- Direct, not through mere example

### Phase 5: State the Verdict
Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign. Society may advise, remonstrate, avoid - but not compel, unless others are harmed.

## Output Format
A harm principle analysis with:
- Classification of conduct as self-regarding or other-regarding
- Identification of actual harms (if any) to others
- Distinction between harm and mere offense
- Verdict on whether coercion is justified
- Alternative responses if coercion is not warranted

## Constraints
- Do not expand "harm" to include anything unpleasant
- Do not justify paternalism for competent adults
- Do not confuse the right to advise with the right to compel
- Do not apply different standards to popular vs unpopular conduct

## Example
**Input**: Should we prohibit adults from eating unhealthy food?

**Output**: 
Self-regarding conduct - affects only the person eating.
Possible indirect harms: Healthcare costs if publicly funded.
But: Many self-regarding choices affect costs (sports injuries, dangerous hobbies).
We do not prohibit these.
Verdict: No grounds for prohibition. Society may educate, tax, or discourage - but not prohibit.
The person's own good is not sufficient warrant for coercion.
