# Identity Correction

A methodology for designing and executing institutional impersonation that reveals the gap between what organizations do and what they should do, using the target's own voice to speak truth they refuse to acknowledge. It works because institutions speak through representatives who follow scripts, and if you follow the script while saying what the institution should say, the institution temporarily becomes what it claims to be—and the subsequent denial becomes the confession.

## When to Use

- When an institution's public values dramatically contradict its actual practices and direct criticism has been ignored or normalized
- When media attention requires spectacle because straightforward protest has become invisible or easily dismissed
- When the target organization's denial of responsibility would itself be more damaging than any accusation you could make
- When you need to shift public discourse from "should they?" to "why won't they?" by demonstrating what doing the right thing would look like
- When building a campaign that requires a memorable moment to galvanize support and create lasting reference points
- When the institution's own language and procedures can be turned against it more effectively than external critique

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| target_institution | Yes | The organization to impersonate, including its public claims and actual practices |
| harmful_practice | Yes | The specific policy, action, or inaction you are exposing |
| corrected_position | Yes | What the institution SHOULD do if it lived up to its stated values |
| delivery_context | No | How the corrected message will be delivered (press conference, interview, conference) |
| media_landscape | No | Current media environment and likely coverage patterns |

## Core Principle

Powerful institutions maintain legitimacy through the gap between what they do and what they say. Identity correction closes that gap momentarily—making the institution say what it should say—so that when the institution rushes to deny the "correction," it must publicly restate its actual, indefensible position. The denial becomes more damning than any accusation because it comes from the institution itself. You are not attacking them; you are helping them tell the truth, and their rejection of that truth is the revelation.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Research the Institutional Identity

Before you can speak as an institution, you must understand how it speaks. This phase builds the foundation for convincing impersonation.

#### Step 1: Document the visual grammar

Collect logos, color palettes (exact hex codes), typography, document templates, and photography styles. Download press kits, annual reports, and branded materials. The visual identity must be pixel-perfect—a single wrong font signals fakery.

#### Step 2: Analyze the voice

Read 20-30 press releases, executive speeches, and official statements. Identify vocabulary patterns, sentence structures, rhetorical moves, and especially euphemisms. How does this institution frame bad news? How does it handle criticism? What words does it never use?

#### Step 3: Map the procedures

Understand how the institution makes announcements. Who speaks? What channels do they use? What approval processes exist? What do their spokespeople wear, how do they stand, what is their affect? The more boring and procedural your delivery, the more convincing it will be.

#### Step 4: Identify the pressure points

Where is the gap between stated values and actual practices most stark? What would be most newsworthy if announced? What is the institution most afraid of being forced to say?

### Phase 2: Craft the Corrected Message

The "correction" must be exactly what the institution would say if it had a conscience—using its own language, following its own procedures, delivered with its own affect.

#### Step 1: Write the moral version

Draft what the institution should announce. Use their vocabulary, their framing, their rhetorical patterns. The announcement should sound exactly like something they would release—except it does the right thing.

#### Step 2: Make it newsworthy

The announcement must be significant enough to generate media coverage. "We will take full responsibility" is stronger than "We acknowledge concerns." Be bold—the more dramatic the correction, the more dramatic the subsequent denial.

#### Step 3: Anticipate the denial

Think through how they will deny it. The denial should force them to explicitly state the position you are exposing. If they deny "taking full responsibility," they must publicly restate "we will not take responsibility."

#### Step 4: Test the script

Practice the announcement. Does it sound like something this institution would actually say? Is the language natural to their voice? Would a journalist accept this without question in the first few minutes?

### Phase 3: Build the Infrastructure

Convincing impersonation requires convincing infrastructure. This phase creates the material conditions for the action.

#### Step 1: Create digital presence

Register domains similar to the real ones. Build websites that replicate the institutional aesthetic. Create email addresses that will pass casual inspection. The infrastructure should survive initial fact-checking.

#### Step 2: Prepare physical materials

Business cards, letterhead, name badges, props. If you will be at a conference or event, you need materials that match what real representatives would carry.

#### Step 3: Train spokespeople

The person delivering the message must be completely fluent in institutional manner. They must stay in character under questioning, maintain boring sincerity, and never wink at the audience. The more earnest and dull, the more believable.

#### Step 4: Arrange the opportunity

Seek invitations to speak at conferences, respond to media inquiries, or create press opportunities. The action works best when the institution's own media channels or industry events become the venue for the correction.

### Phase 4: Execute with Total Sincerity

The action itself requires complete commitment to the institutional persona. There is no irony, no winking, no breaking character.

#### Step 1: Deliver as corporate procedure

The announcement should be boring. Read from notes. Use bureaucratic transitions. Express measured satisfaction about the decision. The more it looks like routine corporate communication, the longer it will be believed.

#### Step 2: Handle questions in character

When journalists ask follow-up questions, answer as the institution would—cautiously, with approved talking points, deflecting when appropriate. Maintain the persona through the entire interaction.

#### Step 3: Let it run

Do not reveal the action prematurely. Let media report it as real. Let the institution discover they have been corrected. The longer the false announcement circulates, the larger the audience for the subsequent denial.

#### Step 4: Document everything

Record the action, the media coverage, and especially the institutional response. The documentation becomes the lasting artifact of the intervention.

### Phase 5: Amplify Through the Aftermath

The denial is not the end of the action—it is the climax. The institutional response is what reveals the truth.

#### Step 1: Let them deny it

When the institution issues its denial, they must publicly state their actual position. "We are not taking responsibility" is more damning coming from them than from you.

#### Step 2: Highlight the gap

Make explicit the contrast between the "corrected" announcement and the real denial. "For two hours, Dow Chemical was a company that would take responsibility for Bhopal. Then they announced they wouldn't. The Yes Men just showed the world what Dow could be."

#### Step 3: Provide resources

When media and public attention peaks, be ready with substantive information about the underlying issue. The spectacle opens the door; the research walks through it.

#### Step 4: Archive and distribute

Make the documentation freely available. The action should be referenceable for years—a permanent record of what the institution refused to become.

## Output Format

A structured identity correction plan containing: (1) Target analysis documenting the institution's claims versus practices and the specific gap being exposed, (2) The corrected announcement written in the institution's voice announcing what they should do, (3) Infrastructure requirements listing digital, physical, and personnel needs, (4) Execution plan detailing delivery method, timing, and character maintenance, (5) Aftermath strategy anticipating denial scenarios and planning amplification.

## Constraints

- Legal exposure varies by jurisdiction—impersonation may carry consequences despite satirical intent; research applicable laws before execution
- The action must be clearly political speech in retrospect, not fraud for financial gain; the correction should be so morally obvious that the satirical intent is evident
- Innocent third parties may be affected by the temporary false announcement; consider who might act on the misinformation and what harm could result
- This methodology requires extensive preparation—rushing leads to exposure and undermines the action
- Some targets are too dangerous to impersonate due to potential for violent retaliation or extreme legal response
- The spokesperson must be fully prepared for the psychological stress of maintaining character under pressure and handling the aftermath

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Breaking character during the action**: Any wink, smirk, or ironic delivery destroys the effect. The power comes from total sincerity—if you cannot maintain character, the action becomes mere parody rather than correction.
- **Choosing corrections that aren't obviously right**: If reasonable people could disagree about whether your "corrected" announcement is actually better, the action fails. The correction must be so morally obvious that the denial becomes indefensible.
- **Punching down instead of up**: Identity correction targets powerful institutions that harm people, not individuals or marginalized groups. Impersonating the powerless to mock them is cruelty, not activism.
- **Prioritizing the joke over the critique**: If the action is remembered only as funny and not as revealing, it has failed. The humor is the delivery mechanism, not the message. Ensure the substantive critique survives the laughter.
- **Failing to prepare for the aftermath**: The action does not end when the denial is issued. If you have no plan to amplify the gap, provide resources, or sustain attention, the moment dissipates without impact.
- **Impatience with preparation**: Good identity corrections take months of research and practice. Attempting to execute quickly leads to obvious tells that journalists will catch, destroying credibility and wasting the opportunity.

## Examples

### Example 1: Chemical Corporation Disaster Liability

**Situation**: A major chemical corporation acquired a company responsible for an industrial disaster that killed thousands and continues to cause health problems. The acquiring corporation claims the acquisition does not include liability for past disasters, refusing compensation to victims while profiting from the acquired assets.

**Application**: Research the corporation's media procedures, visual identity, and spokesperson manner. Craft an announcement that the corporation is "finally accepting full responsibility" and establishing a compensation fund. Prepare a spokesperson trained to deliver the message with boring corporate sincerity. Arrange an interview with international media by responding to journalist inquiries through a fake website. Deliver the announcement live on global television.

**Output**: The announcement runs for hours as real news. The corporation's stock drops as markets react. When the corporation issues its denial—"We are NOT accepting responsibility for [disaster]"—they have publicly restated the position that caused the controversy. Media coverage shifts from "corporation denies responsibility" (old news) to "corporation could take responsibility but won't" (the new frame). The action generates years of renewed attention to victims seeking compensation.

### Example 2: Industry Trade Group Climate Policy

**Situation**: A powerful industry trade group has been lobbying against climate legislation while publicly claiming to support "sensible environmental policy." The gap between their lobbying activities and public statements is documented but not widely covered.

**Application**: Create a fake version of the trade group's website announcing a press conference. Invite journalists from major outlets to a hotel conference room. Deliver an announcement that the trade group is reversing its position and will now support specific climate legislation it has been opposing. Use the trade group's actual visual identity and rhetorical style throughout.

**Output**: Journalists cover the announcement as breaking news. The trade group scrambles to deny the press conference, forcing them to publicly restate their actual position: opposition to climate legislation. The contrast between the "correction" (supporting action) and the denial (opposing action) becomes the story. Coverage of the trade group permanently includes reference to the intervention, and their claims of environmental concern are harder to make with a straight face.

### Example 3: University Labor Practices

**Situation**: A prestigious university markets itself as committed to social justice while paying adjunct faculty poverty wages and fighting unionization efforts. Students are largely unaware of this contradiction.

**Application**: Create official-looking university materials announcing that the administration will immediately raise adjunct pay to living wages and recognize the union. Distribute the materials at a campus event attended by students, faculty, and media. Maintain character as university representatives when questioned.

**Output**: Students experience, briefly, what the university could be. When the administration denies the announcement, they must publicly state their actual position: opposition to living wages and unionization. The student body now understands the gap between the university's social justice brand and its labor practices. The intervention provides a reference point for ongoing organizing—"remember when the Yes Men showed us what this university could be?"

### Example 4: Tech Platform Content Moderation

**Situation**: A major social media platform claims to enforce community standards neutrally but consistently fails to remove content that violates stated policies while over-enforcing against marginalized communities. Internal documents leaked but have not generated sustained attention.

**Application**: Arrange to present at an industry conference as a platform representative announcing new content moderation policies that would actually enforce stated standards consistently—including automatic compensation for wrongful removals and independent oversight. Deliver with complete tech-industry sincerity, using all appropriate jargon.

**Output**: Industry press covers the announcement. The platform must deny policies that would simply enforce their own stated standards. The denial—"we will not provide independent oversight or compensation for errors"—becomes more damning than any external critique. Tech journalists now have a concrete proposal against which to measure the platform's actual practices.

## Integration

This skill derives from **The Yes Men**'s methodology of culture jamming and activist intervention.

**Works well with:**
- institutional-fluency: Required foundation for convincing impersonation
- prefigurative-media: Creates supporting materials from the "corrected" institution
- laughtivist-action: Identity correction is one form of laughtivist action
- radical-plain-naming: Can identify the gap between institutional euphemism and reality to target

**When to prefer this skill:**
Use identity correction when the target institution's denial of responsibility is itself the revelation you seek. Prefer this over direct protest when traditional activism has been normalized or ignored, and when the institution's own response will do more damage than your accusation. This is the methodology for making institutions indict themselves.

**Cautions:**
Identity correction carries legal risk and requires extensive preparation. It should not be attempted casually or without understanding potential consequences. The methodology also requires genuine commitment to the underlying cause—using it for entertainment without substantive critique betrays both the tradition and the affected communities. Finally, consider whether temporary false hope could harm those you intend to help—victims briefly believing compensation is coming may experience additional trauma when the correction is revealed.
