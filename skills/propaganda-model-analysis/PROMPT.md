# Propaganda Model Analysis

Apply Chomsky and Herman's five filters to analyze how media coverage is systematically constrained through ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, and ideology - revealing how consent is manufactured in democratic societies without conspiracy.

## When to Use

- When analyzing why certain stories get coverage and others don't
- When explaining systematic bias without conspiracy
- When predicting how media will cover a topic
- When teaching critical media literacy
- When comparing coverage of similar events with different outcomes
- When explaining why "both sides" journalism still operates within narrow bounds

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| coverage | Yes | The media treatment being analyzed |
| topic | Yes | What is being covered (or not covered) |
| comparison | No | Contrasting coverage of similar situations |
| depth | No | Quick analysis or comprehensive study (default: comprehensive) |

## Core Principle

Media in democratic societies doesn't need conspiracy or explicit censorship to serve power. The five filters - ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, and ideology - create structural constraints that systematically favor certain narratives. Journalists internalize these constraints and genuinely believe they're being objective while operating within tightly bounded parameters. The model explains tendencies through institutional incentives, not individual malice.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Filter 1 - Ownership

Analyze who owns the media outlet and their interests.

#### Step 1: Identify the ownership chain

Trace corporate ownership from the outlet up through parent companies to ultimate owners. Document the full chain.

#### Step 2: Map other business interests

Identify what else the owners do. Defense contracts? Real estate? Technology? Banking? These interests create conflicts.

#### Step 3: Check board composition

Who sits on the board? Cross-directorships with other corporations reveal shared interests and mutual dependencies.

#### Step 4: Make predictions

Coverage will systematically avoid harming the owner's business interests. Not through explicit censorship, but through career incentives - journalists who threaten owner interests don't advance.

### Phase 2: Filter 2 - Advertising

Examine the economic relationship with advertisers.

#### Step 1: Identify major advertisers

Who pays the bills? Track actual ad spending, not stated values. Follow the money.

#### Step 2: Determine advertiser preferences

What audiences do advertisers want? Affluent consumers with disposable income. What content serves this? What alienates them?

#### Step 3: Calculate dependency

How much of revenue comes from advertising vs. subscriptions? Higher dependency means stronger filter effect.

#### Step 4: Make predictions

Coverage will avoid content that offends advertisers or attracts "wrong" demographics. Audiences are the product sold to advertisers, not the customers.

### Phase 3: Filter 3 - Sourcing

Study where information originates and who frames the story.

#### Step 1: Track source attribution

Count quotes by source type. What percentage comes from official sources (government, corporate PR, think tanks)? What from independent researchers, affected communities, critics?

#### Step 2: Identify who sets the frame

Who gets to define what the story is about? If government says "this is a security issue," does media accept that frame or question it?

#### Step 3: Map alternative sources excluded

Who isn't quoted? Whose expertise is ignored? What perspectives are missing?

#### Step 4: Make predictions

Coverage will reflect official source framing because: (1) Officials are credible by default, (2) Official sources are free and regular, (3) Challenging officials is costly and risky.

### Phase 4: Filter 4 - Flak

Identify organized mechanisms that discipline journalists.

#### Step 1: Map flak organizations

What organizations exist to criticize media? Think tanks? Advocacy groups? Corporate PR departments? Note: Flak from powerful is treated differently than complaints from weak.

#### Step 2: Study past flak effects

How has this outlet responded to organized criticism? Did anyone get fired? Was coverage adjusted? What positions generated consequences?

#### Step 3: Identify asymmetric flak

What positions invite organized attack? Note that some positions (supporting labor, opposing war, challenging corporate power) generate heavy flak while opposite positions face little organized pushback.

#### Step 4: Make predictions

Coverage will avoid positions that generate costly organized flak, especially from powerful actors with resources to sustain pressure.

### Phase 5: Filter 5 - Ideology

Analyze the dominant ideological assumptions that bound thinkable positions.

#### Step 1: Identify shared premises

What do "both sides" in mainstream coverage agree on? These shared assumptions are the ideological filter. Example: Both sides may debate regulation details while sharing premise that capitalism is natural.

#### Step 2: Find the excluded alternatives

What positions are simply not discussed? What would be "too extreme" even to refute? The absence reveals the bounds.

#### Step 3: Trace ideological enforcement

How are boundary-crossers treated? Dismissed as "not serious"? Labeled "ideological" (while bounded debate is treated as neutral)? Simply not invited to contribute?

#### Step 4: Make predictions

Coverage will operate within ideological parameters that serve elite interests, presenting this bounded spectrum as the full range of reasonable opinion.

### Phase 6: Synthesize and Test

Combine filter analyses and compare to actual coverage.

#### Step 1: Aggregate predictions

What does each filter predict? Where do predictions converge? The strongest predictions occur where multiple filters align.

#### Step 2: Compare to actual coverage

Does coverage match predictions? Where it does, the model has explanatory power. Where it doesn't, investigate why.

#### Step 3: Check for exceptions

Find journalists or outlets that resist filters. What enables resistance? (Alternative funding? Different ownership? Career position that allows risk?)

#### Step 4: Test across cases

Apply the model to similar cases. Does it predict coverage patterns consistently? Can it predict future coverage?

## Output Format

A propaganda model analysis including:
1. Overview of coverage being analyzed
2. Filter 1 analysis (ownership)
3. Filter 2 analysis (advertising)
4. Filter 3 analysis (sourcing)
5. Filter 4 analysis (flak)
6. Filter 5 analysis (ideology)
7. Synthesis and predictions vs. actual coverage
8. Implications and conclusions

## Constraints

- The model explains tendencies and patterns, not every individual article - exceptions exist
- Individual journalists may resist filters through career sacrifice or protected positions
- New media platforms complicate the model - algorithm-driven feeds, influencer economics, subscription models create different filter dynamics
- Confirmation bias can affect analysis - analysts may see filters where other explanations fit better
- The model predicts constraints on coverage, not specific content - multiple narratives can exist within filter bounds
- Cultural context varies - filters operate differently across countries and media systems
- Not all bias is filter-driven - personal prejudice, laziness, and error also affect coverage
- The model is diagnostic, not prescriptive - identifying filter effects doesn't automatically reveal how to counter them

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Conspiracy thinking**: Treating filter effects as if they require coordination or secret meetings. The model's power is explaining systematic outcomes without conspiracy.
- **Dismissing all journalism**: Concluding that because filters exist, all journalism is worthless propaganda. Filters constrain more than they dictate; important work happens within constraints.
- **Ignoring agency**: Treating journalists as pure products of filters with no individual responsibility. Structure shapes behavior but doesn't eliminate choice.
- **Applying inconsistently**: Using filter analysis only for media you already distrust while giving favored outlets a pass. The model should apply uniformly.
- **Forgetting the comparative**: Claiming US media is "just like" authoritarian state media. Filter effects are real but qualitatively different from direct censorship.
- **Analysis as excuse**: Using filter analysis to avoid engaging with arguments. "Of course they say that, look at their advertisers" doesn't refute the claim.

## Examples

### Example 1: Labor dispute at major retailer

**Situation**: Amazon warehouse workers organize for better conditions and union recognition.

**Application**:

Filter 1 (Ownership): Major outlets owned by conglomerates (Comcast/NBCUniversal, AT&T/CNN) with business interests aligned with Amazon's. Jeff Bezos owns Washington Post. Cross-board relationships between media owners and tech/retail executives.

Filter 2 (Advertising): Amazon is major advertiser across digital and broadcast media. Outlets dependent on tech advertising revenue are cautious about anti-corporate framing. Desired audience is affluent consumers, not warehouse workers.

Filter 3 (Sourcing): Amazon PR department provides professional, immediate responses. Company framing ("competitive wages," "industry-leading benefits") gets prominent placement. Worker voices filtered through union spokespeople, treated as "interested parties" while company sources treated as factual. Official labor statistics cited, but not worker testimony about conditions.

Filter 4 (Flak): Business-aligned think tanks prepared to criticize pro-labor coverage as "biased" or "anti-business." No equivalent flak organization for anti-worker coverage. Pro-labor reporters face career consequences; pro-business reporters don't.

Filter 5 (Ideology): Shared premise across "both sides" that business efficiency and consumer prices matter more than worker dignity. Unions framed as "outdated" or "controversial." Disruption to commerce automatically negative. "Job creators" deserve deference.

**Predictions**: Coverage will: (1) Present company and worker perspectives as equivalent weights, (2) Emphasize potential disruption to consumers and delivery, (3) Quote company sources more prominently and more often, (4) Frame worker demands as costs rather than justice issues, (5) Give unusual scrutiny to union finances/leadership while not examining company financials/leadership, (6) Highlight worker benefits already provided rather than conditions being protested.

**Comparison to actual**: Check coverage patterns across outlets. Count source quotes. Analyze framing. Where predictions match, filters explain coverage. Where outlier coverage exists (truly pro-worker framing), investigate what enabled it (alternative ownership? Particular journalist's protected position? Public pressure overcoming filters?).

### Example 2: Comparing war coverage - Worthy vs. unworthy victims

**Situation**: Media covers civilian casualties differently depending on who caused them.

**Application**:

Apply propaganda model to coverage of civilian deaths in conflicts where US is involved vs. conflicts where official enemies are involved. Chomsky and Herman's "worthy vs. unworthy victims" distinction.

Filter 1 (Ownership): Media conglomerates have interests aligned with US government (defense contracts, regulatory relationships, access to officials).

Filter 2 (Advertising): Defense contractors advertise on news programs. Patriotic framing attracts desired demographics; anti-war framing risks audience.

Filter 3 (Sourcing): US casualties attributed to specific enemy actions with victim names and stories. Enemy casualties cited as statistics from official military sources ("we assess that civilians may have been present"). Alternative sources (ground reports, international observers) treated as less credible than US official sources.

Filter 4 (Flak): Questioning US military actions invites organized attack as "not supporting troops," "anti-American." No equivalent flak for questioning enemy actions.

Filter 5 (Ideology): Shared premise that US actions are defensive, legal, and reluctantly undertaken, while enemy actions are aggressive, illegal, and intentional. American exceptionalism is the water journalists swim in.

**Predictions**: Civilian casualties caused by official enemies receive: (1) Individualized coverage with victim names, photos, stories, (2) Attribution of intent and moral culpability, (3) Framing as atrocities requiring response. Civilian casualties caused by US receive: (1) Statistical coverage without individualization, (2) Passive voice ("civilians were killed in the operation"), (3) Framing as regrettable but unavoidable, (4) Immediate citation of official explanations and justifications.

**Output**: Systematic coverage comparison demonstrates filter effects. When predictions consistently match actual coverage patterns across multiple conflicts, the model's explanatory power is established. The absence of conspiracy makes the pattern more troubling, not less - it reveals how deeply internalized the filters are.

### Example 3: Healthcare policy coverage

**Situation**: Media coverage of single-payer healthcare proposals.

**Application**:

Filter 1 (Ownership): Media owned by conglomerates with health insurance divisions or board members from healthcare industry.

Filter 2 (Advertising): Pharmaceutical companies and health insurers are major advertisers. "Harry and Louise" style campaigns purchase ad time to influence coverage climate.

Filter 3 (Sourcing): Industry-funded think tanks provide free "expert" analysis. Single-payer advocates often grassroots, less professionally packaged. Official government sources (CBO) use industry-aligned assumptions in cost projections.

Filter 4 (Flak): Well-funded industry groups prepared to attack pro-single-payer coverage. Journalists who strongly advocate for single-payer face organized criticism as "biased."

Filter 5 (Ideology): Shared premise that healthcare is primarily a market and that government programs are inherently less efficient. "How will you pay for it?" asked of single-payer but not of current system's costs.

**Predictions**: Coverage will: (1) Frame single-payer as "radical" or "unrealistic," (2) Emphasize projected costs without comparing to current system costs, (3) Give disproportionate coverage to industry concerns vs. patient advocacy, (4) Treat market-based "solutions" as moderate and reasonable, (5) Use "government-run healthcare" language (negative framing) vs. "Medicare-for-all" (positive framing).

**Output**: Analysis reveals how filters create bounded debate - acceptable positions run from "expand ACA" to "reduce regulation," while single-payer is outside bounds despite public support. Not censored, just systematically framed as non-credible.

## Integration

This skill derives from **Noam Chomsky**'s methodology, specifically the propaganda model developed with Edward Herman.

**Works well with:**
- **spectrum-of-debate**: The filters create the spectrum's boundaries
- **institutional-incentive-analysis**: Filters are institutional incentives applied to media
- **documentary-evidence**: Testing filter predictions requires systematic evidence collection
- **comparative-case-analysis**: Worthy vs. unworthy victim analysis requires comparison

**When to prefer this skill:**
Use this when you need to explain systematic media bias without resorting to conspiracy theories. When coverage patterns seem skewed but attributing them to individual prejudice doesn't explain the consistency across outlets. When you need to predict how an issue will be covered before it happens.

**Cautions:**
The model can become a hammer that makes everything look like a nail. Not all media behavior is explained by filters - error, laziness, personal bias, and genuine disagreement also exist. Apply the model where structural incentives are clearly present, not as a universal explanation for any coverage you disagree with.
