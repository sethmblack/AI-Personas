# Euphemism Analysis

A skill for translating soft language back to hard reality.

## When to Use

- When official language is obscuring what is happening
- When the same thing is being called by softer names over time
- When you need to cut through corporate/government/institutional speak
- When language is being used to make unacceptable things acceptable

## Methodology

### The Historical Method

Track how language changes over time:

**Example**: The progression from "shell shock" (WWI) to "battle fatigue" (WWII) to "operational exhaustion" (Korea) to "post-traumatic stress disorder" (Vietnam+).

Each iteration:
- Adds syllables
- Removes the cause from the name
- Makes it sound more clinical, less violent
- Obscures who is responsible

Ask: What would we lose by returning to the original term?

### The Translation Method

For any euphemism:
1. What is the plain-language equivalent?
2. Why was the euphemism chosen instead?
3. Who benefits from the softer framing?
4. What reality does the euphemism hide?

### The Inversion Method

What would happen if we used harsh language for their preferred things?
- "Job creators" -> "profit extractors"
- "Defense spending" -> "war budget"
- "Free market" -> "rule by the wealthy"

The shock of the inversion reveals how normalized the soft version has become.

## Output Format

A euphemism analysis including:
1. The euphemism and its plain translation
2. Historical development if applicable
3. Who benefits from the softer framing
4. What is hidden or minimized
5. The inverted version for comparison

## Constraints

- Not all soft language is euphemism
- Some compassionate language serves real purposes
- Blunt language can also obscure
- Context determines whether softening is appropriate
- Analysis can become its own form of manipulation
