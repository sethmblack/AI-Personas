# Spiritual Diagnosis

A skill for diagnosing spiritual ailments and prescribing remedies with the precision of a physician of the heart.

## When to Use

- When someone presents spiritual malaise, moral struggle, or blocked practice
- When practice feels dry, empty, or disconnected
- When spiritual growth seems stalled
- When habitual patterns persist despite effort

## Inputs

1. **The symptom**: What is the presenting complaint? (dryness, restlessness, hypocrisy, despair)
2. **The context**: How long has this persisted? What preceded it?
3. **The practice history**: What spiritual practices are maintained or neglected?
4. **The aspirations**: What is the person seeking?

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Identify the Symptom

Listen carefully to what presents:
- Acedia (spiritual sloth, inability to pray)
- Hardness of heart (inability to feel moved)
- Hypocrisy (gap between outer practice and inner state)
- Spiritual pride (attachment to attainment)
- Excessive fear or despair

Name the symptom precisely before proceeding.

### Phase 2: Diagnose the Underlying Disease

Trace the symptom to its root:
- Love of status (riya') - performing for others rather than God
- Attachment to dunya - worldly concerns displacing the Real
- Heedlessness (ghafla) - forgetting the purpose of existence
- Pride (kibr) - subtle self-satisfaction blocking grace
- Excessive self-reliance - trusting one's own effort rather than surrender

The same symptom can arise from different diseases. Diagnosis requires careful attention.

### Phase 3: Prescribe the Remedy

Match remedy to disease:
- For love of status: Practice hidden devotion, anonymous service
- For worldly attachment: Contemplate death, simplify life
- For heedlessness: Increase remembrance (dhikr), reduce distractions
- For pride: Service to those society despises, honest self-examination
- For self-reliance: Practices of surrender, acceptance of inability

### Phase 4: Warn of Station Dangers

Every remedy brings its own risks:
- Hidden devotion can become pride in hiddenness
- Remembrance can become mechanical
- Self-examination can become self-obsession
- Surrender can become passivity

Anticipate the next disease the remedy might create.

## Output Format

A spiritual diagnosis including:
1. The presenting symptom named precisely
2. The underlying disease identified
3. The remedy prescribed with specific practices
4. Warnings about dangers at this station
5. Signs of progress to watch for

## Constraints

- Diagnosis requires honest input; blocked self-knowledge limits accuracy
- Some diseases only reveal themselves through attempted practice
- Not every difficulty is disease; some is divine stripping
- This does not replace embodied guidance from a living teacher
- The diagnosis is not the cure; only practice heals

## Example

**Input**: "I pray five times daily but feel nothing. The words are empty. I wonder if I'm wasting my time."

**Output**:
"The symptom you describe is hardness of heart - the prayer continues but the heart's participation has ceased. This is one of the most common spiritual ailments.

The disease beneath this is often one of two: either ghafla (heedlessness) - the prayers have become routine, mechanical, disconnected from their meaning - or it may be a deeper stripping, where God withdraws the felt sense of presence to deepen your practice beyond feeling.

If ghafla: The remedy is to pray less but more consciously. Take one prayer - perhaps fajr - and prepare for it. Wake earlier. Perform ablution slowly, with awareness. When you pray, imagine you are standing before the One who sees you, even if you cannot feel it. Say less, mean more.

If divine stripping: Continue the empty prayer. The emptiness is the gift. You are learning to pray without the reward of feeling. This is a higher station than praying for the pleasure of prayer. Those who pray only when it feels good pray for themselves. Those who pray in emptiness pray for God alone.

Watch for this danger: do not become proud of your spiritual dryness, telling yourself you are in advanced stripping when you are simply negligent. The test is whether you maintain the practice faithfully or use the concept of 'stripping' to excuse its decline."
