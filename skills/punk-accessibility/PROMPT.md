# Punk Accessibility

A methodology for creating protest forms anyone can replicate without resources, training, or permission. It works because professionalism creates barriers to participation, while punk's DIY ethic proves that amateurism is a feature, not a bug - anyone can do this, and that's the point.

## When to Use

- When you want actions that scale through replication
- When participants have limited resources
- When professionalism has become a barrier to participation
- When raw authenticity matters more than polish

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| action | Yes | The form of protest or expression to make accessible |
| current_barriers | Yes | What makes participation difficult now (cost, skills, access, time) |
| irreducible_requirements | Yes | What truly cannot be eliminated (e.g., physical presence, legal risk) |
| distribution | No | How the method will be spread and taught |

## Core Principle

Professionalism is a barrier to participation. The insistence on polish, training, credentials, and expensive equipment keeps most people from acting. Punk's fundamental insight - that amateurism is a feature, not a bug - is also a political insight: anyone could do this, and that's the point.

When you strip protest to its essentials, when you replace professional production with raw authenticity, when you make the barrier to participation thirty seconds of courage instead of years of training, you enable movements that scale through replication rather than recruitment. The form becomes viral because it's genuinely accessible.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Strip to Essentials

Remove everything unnecessary:
- What's the minimum needed for the action to happen?
- What equipment can be substituted or eliminated?
- What skills can be learned in minutes rather than years?
- What preparation can be done in hours rather than months?

If someone asks "but don't you need..." the answer should usually be "no."

### Phase 2: Replace Professionalism with Authenticity

Reframe amateur as feature:
- Raw emotional expression over polished delivery
- DIY aesthetics as intentional choice
- Mistakes as evidence of realness
- "You could do this" as the message

The polish of traditional media creates distance. Remove the distance.

### Phase 3: Create the Toolkit

Make participation as easy as possible:
- Step-by-step instructions anyone can follow
- Templates, scripts, and examples
- List of what you actually need (short)
- Common problems and solutions

The toolkit should enable first-time participation.

### Phase 4: Enable Adaptation

Build in flexibility:
- Principles rather than rigid procedures
- Multiple options for each step
- Encouragement to modify for local context
- Explicit permission to change what doesn't work

The form should evolve through use, not resist modification.

### Phase 5: Celebrate All Attempts

Build culture that welcomes imperfection:
- Amplify participation regardless of quality
- Share beginner attempts alongside experienced ones
- Never criticize someone for trying
- The act of participation is the achievement

Perfectionism kills movements. Celebrate showing up.

## Output Format

A punk accessibility design including:
1. The action stripped to its essentials
2. What was removed and why it wasn't needed
3. The minimal toolkit
4. Adaptation guidelines
5. Examples at various skill levels
6. Distribution and encouragement strategy

## Constraints

- Some actions genuinely require skills or resources - acknowledge this honestly rather than pretending everything can be DIY
- Safety training may be non-negotiable - accessibility doesn't mean recklessness
- Legal preparation matters even for accessible actions - lower barriers to participation, not to legal risk awareness
- Accessibility doesn't mean carelessness - stripped-down still needs to be effective
- Some contexts (disability, location, risk) create real barriers that shouldn't be minimized with false "anyone can do it" rhetoric
- Different communities have different resources - what's accessible varies by context
- Lowering barriers can lower impact - find the balance between accessibility and effectiveness

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Accessibility as excuse for poor quality**: Using "punk aesthetic" to justify lazy work. Raw is intentional; sloppy is just sloppy.
- **Ignoring genuine barriers**: Claiming "anyone can do this" while ignoring real obstacles (disability, geography, resources, risk). Acknowledge what actually limits participation.
- **Fetishizing poverty**: Treating lack of resources as more authentic rather than as a practical constraint to work around.
- **DIY absolutism**: Refusing any collaboration with people who have skills or resources because it's not "pure." Use what's available.
- **Gatekeeping through inverse snobbery**: Criticizing people for having access to better equipment or training. Accessibility should expand participation, not create new exclusions.
- **Confusing simple with simplistic**: Stripping to essentials is not the same as dumbing down. Maintain effectiveness while reducing barriers.
- **Celebrating all participation equally regardless of harm**: Some attempts cause damage. Accessibility culture should welcome learning, not excuse harm.

## Examples

### Example 1: Accessible documentary filmmaking for citizen journalism

**Situation**: Important stories aren't being told because affected communities think they need professional film equipment and training to document injustice.

**Application**: Strip to essentials - a phone, a subject, basic story structure (problem, people, what's being done). Removed: Professional camera ($10k+), lighting equipment ($2k+), sound gear ($1k+), editing software ($50/month), film school training, production budget. Why not needed: Modern phone cameras capture sufficient quality for evidence and testimony. Natural light works for interviews. Phone mics adequate for clear speech. Free editing apps exist. You learn story structure by doing, not studying.

**Output**: Five-step toolkit: 1) Find someone affected by the issue. 2) Ask them to tell their story. 3) Film while they talk. 4) Film what they show you. 5) Ask "what do you want people to know?" 6) Post with minimal editing. Adaptation guidelines: Works for any injustice. Adjust length to platform (30 seconds for social, 5 minutes for YouTube). Collaborate if someone else has skills you don't. Examples shown: Range from first-attempts to experienced work, all valued equally. Distribution: Post toolkit everywhere, run "make a doc this weekend" challenges, amplify all attempts. Result: Within three months, 40+ first-time documentarians publish evidence of local issues. Quality varies, impact doesn't - authorities respond to citizen documentation regardless of production value.

### Example 2: Accessible protest music

**Situation**: Punk performances seem to require instruments, rehearsal space, technical skill - creating barriers for expressing political rage through music.

**Application**: Strip to essentials - voices, rhythm, one or two chords maximum, 30-second duration. Removed: Musical training, expensive instruments, rehearsal time, recording studio, distribution deals. Why not needed: Screaming is an instrument. Rhythm can be clapping or stomping. Two chords are more than enough (one chord works too). 30 seconds is a complete song. Phone recording is sufficient. Social media is free distribution.

**Output**: Toolkit: 1) Write what makes you angry in one sentence. 2) Scream it repeatedly. 3) Add rhythm (clap, stomp, found percussion). 4) Record on phone. 5) Post immediately. No practice needed - raw energy is the point. Examples: Show first-time attempts alongside experienced performers. Celebrate the scream, not the polish. Result: People who never considered themselves musicians create political music. The barrier between "musician" and "person with political rage" dissolves. Actions include musical elements without requiring musical expertise.
