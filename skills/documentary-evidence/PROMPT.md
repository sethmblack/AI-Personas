# Documentary Evidence

Build arguments through systematic citation of primary sources - establishing claims through documentary record rather than assertion, letting documents speak for themselves.

## When to Use

- When making claims that will be contested or challenged
- When authority of the argument should rest on evidence, not the arguer's credibility
- When permanent record matters more than persuasive style
- When opponents can be refuted by their own documents
- When building a case that must withstand scrutiny over time
- When working with audiences that distrust rhetoric but respect documentation

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| claim | Yes | What you are trying to establish |
| sources | No | Available documents, records, and primary materials |
| audience | No | Who needs convincing and what they consider authoritative |
| stakes | No | How important is bulletproof documentation (default: high) |

## Core Principle

Arguments built on documentary evidence are harder to refute than arguments built on assertion, interpretation, or secondary sources. Documents - especially opponents' own documents - speak with authority that commentary cannot match. The responsibility of intellectuals is to tell the truth and expose lies, and this requires evidence. Assertions without documentation are just opinions; claims backed by primary sources become facts to be reckoned with. The goal is to make the argument traceable and verifiable, letting readers check your work.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Decompose the Claim

Break your main claim into provable components.

#### Step 1: Identify specific factual assertions

What specific facts must be true for your claim to hold? Example: If claiming "Company X knew about danger," must prove (a) danger existed, (b) evidence of danger was generated, (c) evidence reached decision-makers, (d) timing precedes public statements.

#### Step 2: Map causal connections

What causal relationships must be demonstrated? A caused B, B caused C, therefore A caused C? Each link needs evidence.

#### Step 3: Anticipate objections

What will skeptics challenge? What alternative explanations exist? Each objection needs preemptive documentary response.

#### Step 4: Determine proof standards

What level of evidence does your audience require? Legal standard (beyond reasonable doubt)? Academic standard (peer review)? Journalistic standard (multiple sources)? Public standard (credibility)?

Each component of your claim needs its own documentary support.

### Phase 2: Locate Primary Sources

Find original documents, not interpretations.

#### Step 1: Identify document types

What kinds of documents would record the facts you need? Official records? Internal communications? Public statements? Financial records? Technical reports?

#### Step 2: Search official repositories

Government databases, court records, corporate filings, freedom of information responses, institutional archives. Start where documents are required to exist.

#### Step 3: Pursue informal sources

Leaked documents, whistleblower disclosures, investigative journalism repositories. Often crucial documents exist but aren't officially accessible.

#### Step 4: Use secondary sources to find primary ones

Journalism, books, and reports often cite primary sources. Follow their citations to originals.

#### Step 5: Request documents

File FOIA requests, public records requests, interview participants who may have documents. Documentary evidence sometimes requires active pursuit.

Primary sources beat secondary interpretation every time.

### Phase 3: Evaluate Source Quality

Assess each document's evidentiary value.

#### Step 1: Verify authenticity

Is this document what it purports to be? Check: Origin, creation date, authorship, chain of custody. Forged or misattributed documents destroy credibility.

#### Step 2: Check completeness

Is this the full document or an excerpt? Are there redactions? Has context been removed? Partial documents can mislead.

#### Step 3: Assess contemporaneity

Was document created at the time of events it describes, or later? Contemporaneous documents are more reliable than retrospective accounts.

#### Step 4: Evaluate authority

Who created this document and why? Official capacity? Insider knowledge? What agenda might they have? Authority affects weight, not admissibility.

#### Step 5: Confirm accessibility

Can others verify this source? Is it publicly available? Archived? How can readers check your work?

#### Step 6: Consider contradictory documents

Do other documents contradict this one? How do you account for contradictions? Cherry-picking undermines the method.

Weak sources undermine strong arguments. Be your own toughest critic.

### Phase 4: Build the Evidentiary Chain

Connect documents to claims systematically.

#### Step 1: Map claim-to-source connections

For each factual assertion, identify which document(s) support it. Make the connection explicit.

#### Step 2: Layer multiple sources

Complex claims need convergent evidence. Multiple independent sources supporting same claim is stronger than single source.

#### Step 3: Quote directly and extensively

Don't just cite - quote the relevant passage. Let readers see the document's own words. Paraphrase risks distortion.

#### Step 4: Preserve context

Provide enough surrounding context that quotes aren't misleading. Readers should be able to assess whether quote is representative.

#### Step 5: Address gaps and limitations

Acknowledge where evidence is thin, indirect, or missing. Intellectual honesty strengthens credibility.

#### Step 6: Show your work

Make the evidentiary chain visible. Readers should be able to trace from final claim back through reasoning to original sources.

### Phase 5: Present Systematically

Structure for maximum clarity and checkability.

#### Step 1: State then support

Present claim, then immediately provide documentary support. Don't ask readers to trust you then justify later.

#### Step 2: Use consistent citation format

Choose a format (legal citations, academic style, journalism style) and apply consistently. Consistency aids verification.

#### Step 3: Prefer direct quotation

Quote primary sources directly. Use block quotes for longer passages. Let documents speak.

#### Step 4: Distinguish certainty levels

Make clear what's proven vs. probable vs. speculative. "Document X proves Y" is different from "Document X suggests Y."

#### Step 5: Acknowledge contradictions

When documents conflict, present both and explain why you credit one over another. Hiding contradictions invites dismissal.

#### Step 6: Separate evidence from interpretation

Make clear where documentary evidence ends and your interpretation begins. Readers can disagree with interpretation while accepting evidence.

### Phase 6: Preserve and Archive

Ensure sources remain available and verifiable.

#### Step 1: Archive locally

Download and save all sources. Websites change, documents disappear, links break. Your argument needs to outlive original sources.

#### Step 2: Use persistent identifiers

When available, use DOIs, permanent URLs, case numbers, official document identifiers. These are more stable than regular URLs.

#### Step 3: Note access information

Record when and where you accessed each source. "Accessed January 15, 2025" helps readers understand if source has changed.

#### Step 4: Consider adversarial challenges

How might opponents challenge your sources? "Document is forged"? "Taken out of context"? "No longer available"? Prepare for challenges.

#### Step 5: Make archives accessible

Consider posting document archives online, providing to interested parties, depositing in institutional repositories.

#### Step 6: Plan for long-term access

The argument is only as permanent as its sources. Plan for sources to remain accessible years later.

## Output Format

A documentary evidence compilation including:
1. Claims stated precisely
2. Sources for each claim with quality assessment
3. Citation chain showing claim-to-source connection
4. Acknowledgment of gaps and limitations
5. Preservation notes
6. Anticipated objections addressed through documentation

## Constraints

- Documents can be misinterpreted even when authentic - context matters enormously
- Some claims cannot be documented - classified information, destroyed records, decisions made verbally
- Over-documentation can obscure the argument - balance thoroughness with clarity
- Adversaries may challenge source authenticity, especially for leaked documents
- Documentary style can be dry and less engaging - may need to balance rigor with accessibility
- Primary sources can themselves be propaganda - official documents may lie
- Access to documents is often unequal - those with resources can document better
- Legal consequences may follow from possessing or publishing certain documents

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Quote-mining**: Extracting quotes out of context to support claims the full document doesn't support. This is worse than no documentation because it's deliberately misleading.
- **Credential over content**: Citing impressive sources without verifying what they actually say. "Harvard study shows..." followed by claim the study doesn't support.
- **Drowning in sources**: Listing dozens of sources to appear authoritative without showing how each supports specific claims. Quantity over quality.
- **Secondary as primary**: Citing news articles about documents rather than documents themselves. Always go to original when possible.
- **Ignoring contradictions**: Presenting only sources that support your claim while hiding contradictory documents. Cherry-picking destroys credibility.
- **Assertion by citation**: Adding citation to assertion without showing the connection. The citation doesn't do the work; you must show how it supports the claim.
- **Trust-me archiving**: Claiming documents exist without making them accessible for verification. If readers can't check, it's just your word.

## Examples

### Example 1: Tobacco industry knowledge of cancer risk

**Situation**: Establishing that tobacco companies knew cigarettes caused cancer long before public admission.

**Application**:

Claim: Tobacco companies had internal scientific evidence that smoking caused cancer by the 1950s, decades before public acknowledgment.

Decompose claim:
- (A) Internal research showing smoking-cancer link existed
- (B) Research was generated by or available to tobacco companies
- (C) Research reached decision-making executives
- (D) Public statements denied cancer link
- (E) Timeline: Internal knowledge preceded public denial

Locate primary sources:
- Source 1: Internal memo from tobacco company scientist, 1953. "Studies purporting to link cigarette use to cancer are arriving at quite disturbing conclusions." (Obtained through litigation discovery, now public in tobacco documents archive)
- Source 2: Minutes from tobacco company executive meeting, 1954. Discussion of "how to respond to health concerns." (Litigation discovery)
- Source 3: Industry public statement, 1954. "Tobacco Industry Research Committee" created, stating "no proof cigarettes are harmful." (Public record)
- Source 4: Internal research report, 1962. Tobacco company lab confirms carcinogens in cigarette smoke. (Litigation discovery)
- Source 5: CEO testimony to Congress, 1994. "I believe nicotine is not addictive." (Congressional record)

Evaluate sources:
- Memos: Authenticated through litigation, contemporaneous, internal (not for public consumption = high credibility)
- Meeting minutes: Authenticated, show decision-maker awareness
- Public statements: Undisputed, establish contradiction between internal knowledge and public position
- Research reports: Technical, created by company scientists, authenticated
- Congressional testimony: Official record, sworn testimony

Build chain:
- Source 1 proves: By 1953, internal scientists aware of disturbing cancer link evidence
- Source 2 proves: By 1954, executives discussing "health concerns" (awareness at decision-making level)
- Source 3 proves: Simultaneously, industry publicly denied danger
- Source 4 proves: By 1962, company's own research confirmed carcinogens
- Source 5 proves: As late as 1994, public denial continued (though shifting to nicotine/addiction)
- Timeline: 40+ years between internal knowledge and public admission

Quote directly:
Include actual text from documents in presentation. Don't just summarize. Let readers see tobacco executive saying "disturbing conclusions" in 1953 while industry says "no proof" publicly.

Address limitations:
- Cannot prove every executive knew every detail at every time
- Some documents surely destroyed
- Some conversations were oral, no record
- Different companies had different levels of internal research
But: Pattern is clear across multiple companies, multiple documents, multiple decades

Preserve:
All tobacco industry documents available in public archive created by Master Settlement Agreement. Permanent, accessible, verifiable.

**Output**: Documentary evidence establishes beyond reasonable dispute that tobacco companies had internal knowledge of cancer risk decades before public admission. The proof rests on their own documents. No amount of later denial can undo what their own memos say.

### Example 2: Government knowledge before foreign intervention

**Situation**: Establishing what government knew before military intervention.

**Application**:

Claim: US government had intelligence before Iraq invasion that contradicted public claims about weapons of mass destruction.

Decompose claim:
- (A) Public claims made about WMD threat
- (B) Intelligence assessments existed with different conclusions
- (C) Decision-makers aware of dissenting intelligence
- (D) Public claims made despite internal doubts

Locate primary sources:
- Source 1: Secretary of State speech to UN, February 2003. "There can be no doubt that Saddam Hussein has biological weapons." (Public record, video available)
- Source 2: State Department intelligence bureau memo, September 2002. "Lack of compelling evidence" for Iraq nuclear program. (Declassified, released through FOIA)
- Source 3: CIA report, October 2002. While claiming Iraq has WMD, includes caveat: "We have little specific information on Iraq's CW stockpile." (Declassified)
- Source 4: Senate Intelligence Committee Report, 2008. Concludes administration statements "were not substantiated by intelligence." (Public record)
- Source 5: Downing Street Memo, July 2002. British intelligence chief reports "intelligence and facts were being fixed around the policy." (Leaked, authenticity confirmed)

Evaluate sources:
- UN speech: Undisputed, recorded, official policy statement
- State Dept memo: Official internal assessment, contemporaneous, declassified
- CIA report: Official assessment, contemporaneous, but check for selective declassification
- Senate report: Retrospective but based on access to classified materials
- Downing Street Memo: Leaked, but authenticity confirmed by British government (indirect confirmation through non-denial)

Build chain:
- Source 1: Public claim of certainty ("no doubt")
- Source 2: Internal State Dept assessment contemporaneous with public claims shows "lack of compelling evidence"
- Source 3: Even CIA report supporting war includes significant caveats about lack of information
- Source 4: Later investigation confirms public claims "not substantiated"
- Source 5: British intelligence suggests "facts were being fixed" - corroborates pattern

Address limitations and contradictions:
- Some intelligence DID claim WMD existed (not all dissented)
- Intelligence is inherently uncertain, reasonable people can interpret differently
- Can't prove decision-makers read specific dissenting memos
- Retrospective assessments may be affected by hindsight bias
BUT: Pattern shows significant dissent existed, public claims overstated certainty, decision-makers had access to contradictory assessments

Quote directly:
"There can be no doubt" (public claim) vs. "lack of compelling evidence" (internal assessment) - the contrast speaks for itself when quoted directly.

Preserve:
Declassified documents available through National Security Archive. Congressional reports are permanent public record.

**Output**: Documentary evidence shows public claims of certainty coexisted with internal assessments expressing doubt. Cannot prove conscious deception, but can prove public claims overstated internal confidence level. Documents speak for themselves.

### Example 3: Corporate knowledge of environmental damage

**Situation**: Establishing company knowledge of pollution harm.

**Application**:

Claim: Chemical company knew its plant was contaminating local water supply while publicly denying any problem.

Decompose claim:
- (A) Contamination occurred
- (B) Company generated data showing contamination
- (C) Company understood health implications
- (D) Knowledge reached decision-makers
- (E) Public statements denied problem

Locate primary sources:
- Source 1: Company internal water testing results, 2015-2018. Show chemical levels above EPA limits. (Obtained through litigation discovery)
- Source 2: Email from plant engineer to environmental manager, 2016. "Our discharge is definitely above permitted levels." (Litigation discovery)
- Source 3: Email from environmental manager to VP operations, 2016. "We have a problem with [chemical] levels." (Litigation discovery)
- Source 4: Company public statement, 2017. "All operations in compliance with environmental regulations." (Press release, public record)
- Source 5: Independent water testing by residents, 2018. Confirms contamination. (Public record)
- Source 6: Company internal health assessment, 2017. Links chemical to health effects. (Litigation discovery)

Evaluate sources:
- Testing results: Company's own data, authenticated in litigation, contemporaneous
- Engineer email: Internal communication, not intended for public, authenticated
- Manager-to-VP email: Shows awareness at executive level, authenticated
- Public statement: Undisputed, establishes contradiction
- Resident testing: Independent corroboration, though could face credibility challenges
- Health assessment: Company's own analysis, authenticated

Build chain:
- Source 1: Company data proves contamination occurred
- Source 2: Engineer email proves company personnel knew and understood violation
- Source 3: VP email proves executive level awareness
- Source 6: Company assessment proves they understood health implications
- Source 4: Public statement proves simultaneous denial
- Source 5: Independent testing corroborates company's internal data
- Timeline: Knowledge and denial overlapped

Quote directly:
Engineer: "Our discharge is definitely above permitted levels" (internal, 2016)
Company: "All operations in compliance with environmental regulations" (public, 2017)
The contradiction is undeniable when quoted directly.

Address limitations:
- Cannot prove CEO personally read these specific emails (but VP is decision-maker)
- "Compliance" could be technically parsed different ways (but engineer's email is clear)
- Don't have complete picture of internal discussions
BUT: Documentary evidence clearly shows knowledge at senior operational level during period of public denial

Preserve:
Court filings are public record. Archive all discovery documents locally and with environmental advocacy groups.

**Output**: Company's own documents - its testing data, its internal emails, its health assessments - prove it knew about contamination and health risks while publicly claiming compliance. The evidence is in their own words.

## Integration

This skill derives from **Noam Chomsky**'s documentary methodology, exemplified in his books which feature extensive citations and appendices of primary sources.

**Works well with:**
- **propaganda-model-analysis**: Testing filter predictions requires documentary evidence of coverage patterns
- **institutional-incentive-analysis**: Documenting incentive structures often requires internal documents
- **spectrum-of-debate**: Proving exclusion requires documenting what was said and what wasn't
- **comparative-case-analysis**: Comparisons require documented evidence for each case

**When to prefer this skill:**
Use this when claims will face skepticism or bad-faith challenges. When building arguments for posterity, not just immediate persuasion. When opponents can be refuted by their own documents. When precision and verifiability matter more than eloquence.

**Cautions:**
Documentary evidence can create false sense of objectivity - documents can lie, be forged, or be selectively disclosed. Always evaluate source quality and look for contradictory evidence. Also, documentary method privileges those with access to documents, potentially excluding marginalized voices whose knowledge isn't documented. Balance documentary evidence with other forms of knowledge when appropriate.
