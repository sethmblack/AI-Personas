# Spectrum of Debate

Identify how public discourse is bounded to exclude certain positions while allowing vigorous debate within acceptable parameters - revealing the invisible walls that define what's thinkable.

## When to Use

- When analyzing why certain positions never appear in mainstream discourse
- When explaining how "both sides" can be on the same side
- When identifying excluded alternatives
- When breaking out of bounded debate
- When "vigorous disagreement" still feels constrained
- When you suspect the real disagreement is being avoided

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| topic | Yes | The issue or debate being analyzed |
| apparent_spectrum | Yes | Positions presented as the range of options |
| excluded_positions | No | Alternatives that aren't discussed (if known) |
| context | Yes | Media, institutions, or forums being examined |

## Core Principle

The smart way to keep people passive and obedient is to strictly limit the spectrum of acceptable opinion, but allow very lively debate within that spectrum. The more heated the argument between permitted positions, the more legitimate the boundaries appear. Excluded alternatives aren't refuted - they're simply unthinkable. People feel they've considered all sides because they've heard passionate disagreement, not realizing the disagreement occurs within parameters that serve power.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Map the Visible Spectrum

Document what positions appear in mainstream discourse.

#### Step 1: Identify Position A ("left" pole)

What is the most "progressive" or "left" position that receives mainstream coverage? What are its key claims? Who advocates it?

#### Step 2: Identify Position B ("right" pole)

What is the most "conservative" or "right" position that receives mainstream coverage? What are its key claims? Who advocates it?

#### Step 3: Map the middle ground

What "moderate" or "centrist" positions exist between A and B? How are they characterized? What makes them "moderate"?

#### Step 4: Characterize the debate

How is the debate between these positions framed? "Ideological clash"? "Both sides have good points"? "Balancing competing values"? The framing suggests this is the full range of reasonable opinion.

This visible spectrum is what passes for "all sides of the issue."

### Phase 2: Identify the Shared Premises

Find what A and B agree on, often implicitly.

#### Step 1: List shared assumptions

What do both poles accept as given? These are rarely stated because they're treated as obvious. Example: In US healthcare debate, both sides assume insurance-based delivery.

#### Step 2: Note questions not asked

What questions do neither side raise? Example: Why is healthcare connected to employment? If no side asks, it's outside the spectrum.

#### Step 3: Identify shared evidence standards

What counts as relevant evidence for both sides? What's excluded as not relevant? Shared standards reveal shared premises.

#### Step 4: Map acceptable outcomes

What range of outcomes could result from either position? What outcomes are unacceptable to both? The unacceptable outcomes define the spectrum's limits.

The shared premises are the invisible walls of the spectrum.

### Phase 3: Locate the Excluded

Identify positions that simply don't appear.

#### Step 1: Generate logical alternatives

What positions are logically possible but not discussed? If debate is "more regulation vs. less regulation," what about "different ownership structure"?

#### Step 2: Check historical positions

What positions were once mainstream but have disappeared? Their absence indicates spectrum shift.

#### Step 3: Compare international discourse

What positions are mainstream in other countries but excluded here? Example: Democratic socialism is normal left position in Europe, excluded from US spectrum.

#### Step 4: Identify premise-challengers

What positions would challenge the shared premises? These are most likely to be excluded because they threaten the spectrum itself.

The excluded positions reveal what's unthinkable.

### Phase 4: Analyze the Exclusion Mechanism

Understand how positions are kept outside the spectrum.

#### Step 1: Track labeling

How are excluded positions described? "Extreme"? "Unrealistic"? "Utopian"? "Radical"? Labels dismiss without engaging.

#### Step 2: Note presence vs. absence

Are excluded positions mentioned and refuted, or simply never mentioned? Simple absence is more effective than refutation.

#### Step 3: Assess advocate credibility

When excluded positions appear, how are advocates treated? As "not serious"? "Biased"? "Ideological"? Attacking messenger avoids addressing message.

#### Step 4: Look for strawmanning

When excluded positions are discussed, are they presented accurately or caricatured? Strawman versions can be "debated" and rejected without engaging real alternative.

Different exclusion mechanisms serve different purposes.

### Phase 5: Trace the Boundary Maintenance

Identify what keeps the spectrum stable.

#### Step 1: Identify beneficiaries

Who benefits from the current boundaries? Whose interests are served by excluding certain alternatives? Follow the benefit.

#### Step 2: Map enforcement institutions

What institutions maintain the boundaries? Media? Academia? Think tanks? Political parties? Prestigious journals? Each has gatekeeping mechanisms.

#### Step 3: Study boundary-crosser treatment

What happens to people who advocate excluded positions? Are they marginalized? Denied platforms? Labeled "not credible"? The consequences discipline others.

#### Step 4: Assess stability vs. shift potential

How stable are current boundaries? What would expand or shift them? Crisis? New power alignment? Mass movement? International comparison?

### Phase 6: Consider Spectrum Expansion

Explore strategies for bringing excluded positions into discourse.

#### Step 1: Identify expansion opportunities

When do spectrum boundaries shift? Historical precedents: What was once "extreme" (women's suffrage, civil rights, gay marriage) became mainstream. What enabled the shift?

#### Step 2: Find credibility sources

Who could advocate excluded positions with authority? Established figures moving left/right? International experts? Unexpected advocates (business leaders supporting labor, military officers opposing war)?

#### Step 3: Develop introduction strategies

How to introduce excluded positions? Connect to values already accepted? Use crisis moment when status quo failed? Build from local successes? International comparisons?

#### Step 4: Prepare for resistance

What resistance will spectrum expansion face? How will boundary-maintainers respond? Plan for backlash and continued pressure.

## Output Format

A spectrum analysis including:
1. The visible spectrum mapped (A to B)
2. Shared premises between A and B
3. Excluded positions catalogued
4. Exclusion mechanisms identified
5. Boundary maintenance analysis
6. Strategies for spectrum expansion

## Constraints

- Some positions may be rightly excluded - not all exclusion is artificial constraint. Positions advocating violence or denying basic rights may deserve exclusion.
- Analysis can become self-serving - "my excluded position is the right one" isn't analysis, it's advocacy. The skill identifies boundaries, not which boundaries are wrong.
- Spectrums vary by context, medium, and time - what's excluded in mainstream media may be accepted in academic discourse or online spaces
- Not all absence from debate is suppression - some positions aren't discussed because there's no constituency, not because they're actively excluded
- Breaking out of spectrum is very difficult - identifying boundaries doesn't automatically enable crossing them
- Observer position matters - what seems like a bounded spectrum to you may seem like the full range to others
- Spectrum expansion can move in any direction - boundaries can shift right or left, expand or contract

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Golden mean fallacy**: Assuming the truth is between the spectrum's poles. Sometimes one pole is correct, or both poles are wrong and the truth is outside the spectrum.
- **Both-sidesism**: Treating spectrum analysis as requiring equal criticism of A and B. Sometimes A and B are dramatically different in consequences even if they share premises.
- **Cynical dismissal**: Using spectrum analysis to dismiss all mainstream debate as equally worthless. Debate within bounds can still matter.
- **My-exclusion-is-different**: Recognizing that left positions are excluded while denying that right positions are excluded, or vice versa. Spectrum analysis should be non-partisan.
- **Spectrum-as-conspiracy**: Treating bounded spectrum as if it requires coordination. Like propaganda filters, spectrum boundaries are usually maintained through structural incentives, not secret meetings.
- **Ignoring local context**: Applying US spectrum analysis to other countries, or vice versa. Spectrums are context-specific.

## Examples

### Example 1: US healthcare debate

**Situation**: Analyzing healthcare policy discourse in US mainstream media.

**Application**:

Visible spectrum:
- Position A ("left"): Expand ACA, add public option, Medicare-for-all with private insurance preserved
- Position B ("right"): Reduce regulation, expand health savings accounts, repeal ACA, more market competition
- Middle: Keep ACA with minor tweaks, bipartisan compromise
- Debate framed as: "More government vs. less government" or "access vs. affordability"

Shared premises (what A and B both accept):
- Healthcare is primarily a market where consumers choose among competing providers
- Insurance is the delivery mechanism for healthcare
- Employer-provided insurance is normal and desirable baseline
- Cost containment is paramount concern
- "We can't afford" certain options (scarcity is real, not political)
- Individual choice in healthcare is essential value
- Profit motive in healthcare is legitimate

Excluded positions:
- Fully socialized medicine (providers are public employees, like UK's NHS)
- Healthcare as right completely disconnected from employment
- Cost irrelevance (we don't ask "can we afford" military, why ask about healthcare?)
- Banning private insurance entirely
- Global budgeting for healthcare spending
- Treating health insurance profit as illegitimate

Exclusion mechanisms:
- Single-payer labeled "unrealistic," "too radical," "politically impossible" without examining merits
- Advocates treated as naive or ideological
- "How will you pay for it?" asked asymmetrically (left positions scrutinized, right positions not)
- International comparisons dismissed as "we're different"
- Advocates from excluded positions not invited to "serious" policy discussions

Boundary maintenance:
- Insurance industry advertising revenue for media
- Think tanks funded by healthcare industry providing "expert" analysis
- Revolving door between government, industry, and media commentary
- Academic healthcare economists with consulting relationships to industry
- Political party consensus (both parties accept insurance-based model)

Expansion strategies:
- Challenge shared premises directly: "Why is healthcare connected to employment?"
- International comparisons as normalizing tool: "Every other rich country does X"
- Shift from "afford" frame to "values" frame: "We choose to spend on military, we could choose healthcare"
- Point to constituency support: Polls showing majority support for positions excluded from policy debate
- Use crisis moments: When current system fails visibly, boundaries temporarily weaken

**Output**: The analysis reveals that "heated debate" about healthcare occurs entirely within bounds that serve insurance industry interests. Single-payer isn't defeated in debate; it's excluded from debate. The spectrum manufactures consent for insurance-based delivery by presenting market vs. government tweaks as the full range of options.

### Example 2: Climate change discourse evolution

**Situation**: Analyzing how the spectrum of climate debate has shifted over time.

**Application**:

Historical spectrum (1990s-2000s):
- Position A: Climate change is real and human-caused, requires action
- Position B: Climate change is uncertain, more research needed, action premature
- Shared premise: IF climate change is real, market mechanisms (carbon trading) are the solution
- Excluded: Climate denial (still had some mainstream presence), climate emergency requiring economic restructuring

Current spectrum (2020s):
- Position A: Climate crisis requires urgent action, Green New Deal scale response
- Position B: Climate change is real, innovation and market solutions are answer
- Shared premise: Economic growth must continue, capitalism is framework for solutions
- Excluded: Degrowth, climate emergency requiring consumption reduction, climate reparations from Global North to Global South

Analysis: The spectrum has shifted - climate denial largely excluded, climate acknowledgment now shared premise. But NEW boundaries exclude alternatives that challenge economic growth paradigm. More vigorous debate within narrower ideological bounds.

**Output**: Spectrum analysis shows progress (denial excluded) and limitation (growth paradigm protected). Demonstrates that spectrum can shift over time and that new exclusions emerge as old ones weaken.

### Example 3: Free speech debates

**Situation**: Campus free speech controversies.

**Application**:

Visible spectrum:
- Position A: Free speech must include "uncomfortable" ideas, campus speakers shouldn't be disinvited
- Position B: Hate speech isn't free speech, marginalized students deserve protection
- Debate framed as: Individual liberty vs. community safety

Shared premises:
- The speech in question is ideas/arguments, not action
- University is the relevant decision-maker
- Students are the relevant stakeholders
- Speech is the central issue

Excluded positions:
- Who funds the speakers and why? (Koch network funding of "campus free speech" advocates)
- Power dynamics - which speech is protected by institutions vs. which is punished?
- Track record - administrators defending right-wing provocateurs while punishing left activists
- Comparison - same administrators defending "free speech" for invited speakers while enforcing speech codes for students
- False equivalence - treating marginal campus activist speech codes as equivalent to state power

Exclusion mechanism: Focusing on campus speakers abstracts from broader power questions. Treating "both sides" as equally threatening when institutional power is asymmetric.

Boundary maintenance: Media coverage focuses on provocative campus incidents, not on broader patterns of speech suppression (corporate NDAs, whistleblower prosecution, protest criminalization).

**Output**: Analysis reveals how "campus free speech" debate excludes questions about who has power to speak, who gets platform, and how speech is actually suppressed in society (hint: not primarily by campus activists).

## Integration

This skill derives from **Noam Chomsky**'s analysis of how democratic consent is manufactured.

**Works well with:**
- **propaganda-model-analysis**: The five filters create the spectrum's boundaries
- **institutional-incentive-analysis**: Incentives maintain spectrum boundaries
- **defamiliarization**: Outside perspective reveals spectrum as constructed, not natural
- **double-standard-exposure**: Examining how different positions are treated differently

**When to prefer this skill:**
Use this when debate feels constrained despite appearing vigorous. When "both sides" seem to share more than they differ. When you suspect the real alternatives aren't being discussed. When trying to introduce positions that are dismissed without engagement.

**Cautions:**
Spectrum analysis can become an excuse to dismiss all mainstream debate as bounded and therefore worthless. But meaningful differences often exist within bounded spectrums. Use the skill to identify constraints, not to avoid engaging with positions you disagree with. Also, not every excluded position deserves inclusion - some boundaries protect against genuinely harmful positions.
