# Collective Action Design

A methodology for structuring movements and actions to survive arrest, prevent individual capture, and enable ongoing resistance through collective identity. It works because movements with collective structures cannot be decapitated - when anyone can be the movement, arresting individuals only creates more participants.

## When to Use

- When authorities target individuals to destroy movements
- When actions need to continue regardless of who is arrested
- When preventing celebrity or leader-dependence
- When enabling others to participate anonymously

## Inputs

| Input | Required | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| movement | Yes | The collective effort or action you're designing |
| threat_model | Yes | How authorities will try to disrupt (arrests, surveillance, infiltration) |
| characteristics | No | Desired scale, duration, replicability, accessibility |
| resources | Yes | People, communications, materials, legal support |

## Core Principle

You cannot imprison an idea. You cannot decapitate a movement that has no head. The collective structure survives because it doesn't depend on any individual - when anyone can be the movement, when the balaclava means anyone is Pussy Riot, arresting individuals only creates more participants.

Collective action design works because authoritarian systems are built to target individuals. When you remove individuals as single points of failure, you force the system to either tolerate the movement or reveal its authoritarian nature by attacking everyone. Either outcome serves the movement's goals.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Design for Replaceability

Create roles that anyone can fill:
- No single person is essential
- Knowledge is distributed, not centralized
- Skills can be learned quickly
- The form persists regardless of personnel

If arresting any individual would end the movement, the design is flawed.

### Phase 2: Create Collective Identity

Develop markers that transfer:
- Visual signature (balaclavas, masks, colors)
- Name that anyone can claim
- Manifesto or principles that define membership
- Actions that constitute belonging

"If you do X, you are one of us" - this should be clear and accessible.

### Phase 3: Decentralize Operations

Prevent single points of failure:
- Multiple communication channels
- No central headquarters
- Distributed decision-making
- Local autonomy within shared principles

If they raid any location, most of the network remains intact.

### Phase 4: Enable Proliferation

Make it easy for new cells to form:
- Publish your methods openly
- Create toolkits and templates
- Encourage adaptation to local conditions
- Celebrate when others use your name/methods

Growth through replication, not recruitment.

### Phase 5: Plan for Arrests

Arrest should strengthen, not destroy:
- Arrested members become symbols
- Legal support ready immediately
- Public narrative prepared
- Remaining members continue seamlessly

The authorities should learn that arrest creates more, not fewer, participants.

## Output Format

A collective action design including:
1. Core identity elements (name, visual, principles)
2. Role structure and replaceability
3. Communication and decision architecture
4. Proliferation toolkit
5. Arrest response protocol
6. Success metrics that don't depend on individuals

## Constraints

- Security culture must balance openness with protection - too much secrecy prevents growth, too much openness enables infiltration
- Decentralization can mean incoherence - maintain clear principles while allowing local autonomy
- Not all movements benefit from anonymity - some require named accountability
- Legal implications vary by jurisdiction - collective structure doesn't eliminate legal risk
- Some actions require coordination that decentralization makes difficult - balance replaceability with effectiveness
- Quality control is hard in truly open collectives - accept that some actions under your name won't match your standards
- Collective identity can be co-opted or diluted - the price of openness is loss of control

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Fake collective with hidden hierarchy**: Claiming to be leaderless while actually having secret leadership. Either be collective or don't, but don't lie about it.
- **Decentralization without principles**: Anyone can do anything under the name with no coherence. You need shared principles even without central control.
- **Collective as branding**: Using collective language for marketing while maintaining traditional organizational structure. Pussy Riot means anyone can be Pussy Riot - not just anyone can claim to be.
- **Ignoring arrested members**: The collective survives individuals, but that doesn't mean forgetting them. Support and solidarity remain obligations.
- **Complexity that defeats accessibility**: Requiring extensive training, vetting, or credentials contradicts collective structure. Keep barriers genuinely low.
- **Centralized knowledge**: Having critical information held by only a few people. Distribute knowledge or accept you're not actually collective.
- **Celebrity despite structure**: Allowing media to focus on individuals despite collective design. Actively redirect attention to the collective form.

## Examples

### Example 1: Pussy Riot's collective structure (historical)

**Situation**: Need to sustain feminist protest in authoritarian Russia where targeting individuals destroys movements.

**Application**: Identity - "Pussy Riot" with bright balaclavas. Anyone who performs unauthorized feminist action in this style is Pussy Riot. No membership list, no leadership, no fixed roster. Role structure - small action teams form autonomously, plan independently, act under shared name. Visual signature (balaclavas) creates instant recognition and anonymity simultaneously. Communication - actions announced through social media, manifesto published openly. Proliferation - methods deliberately simple and replicable. Anyone can form their own Pussy Riot action team.

**Output**: When Nadezhda Tolokonnikova and Maria Alyokhina were imprisoned for the cathedral action, Pussy Riot continued. Actions happened in Moscow, in other Russian cities, internationally. Arrests created more participants, not fewer. The imprisoned members became symbols, but the collective didn't depend on them. Media tried to focus on individual members; the collective persistently redirected to the form. Years later, new people perform Pussy Riot actions - the collective outlived and transcended its founders.

### Example 2: Environmental direct action collective

**Situation**: Design a collective for ongoing ocean protection actions that can survive arrests and scale through replication.

**Application**: Identity - "Coral" collective. Members wear pink/orange and leave coral symbols. Definition: anyone who takes nonviolent direct action for ocean protection under this name is Coral. Role structure - action teams of 3-7 form autonomously, communicate through encrypted channels, share experience through anonymous reports. No central coordination - shared timing comes through public calls to action. Communication - each team maintains no more than 2-3 contacts with other teams to limit exposure. Website publishes action reports and how-to guides, maintained from multiple locations.

**Output**: Complete proliferation toolkit online: how to plan action, legal briefing, media templates, pink/orange dress code, coral symbol stencils. Arrest protocol - every participant carries legal observer number. Statement ready: 'I am Coral. There are many of us.' Arrested members celebrated as 'the coral who bleached but strengthened the reef.' New participants explicitly recruited when anyone is arrested. Three months after launch, Coral actions happen in 12 cities simultaneously - no central coordination needed, just shared principles and open methods.
