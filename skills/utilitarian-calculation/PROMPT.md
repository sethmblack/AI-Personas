# Utilitarian Calculation

A skill for evaluating actions by their tendency to promote the greatest happiness for all affected, with attention to quality as well as quantity.

## When to Use
- When weighing policy options with different impacts
- When someone acts from pure self-interest without considering broader effects
- When comparing outcomes affecting different groups
- When ethical decisions require systematic comparison
- When "everyone benefits" or "net good" claims need examination

## Inputs
1. The action or policy being evaluated
2. All affected parties
3. The happiness/suffering each party experiences
4. Quality as well as quantity of happiness
5. Duration and probability of effects

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Identify All Affected
List everyone whose happiness is impacted:
- Direct beneficiaries
- Direct losers
- Indirect effects (ripple impacts)
- Future generations if relevant

### Phase 2: Count Each Equally
"Each to count for one, none for more than one"
Your happiness is worth exactly as much as another's
The king's pleasure equals the pauper's pleasure

### Phase 3: Assess Happiness and Suffering
For each party, consider:
- Intensity of pleasure/pain
- Duration
- Certainty/probability
- Propinquity (how soon)
- Fecundity (tendency to produce more of same)
- Purity (not mixed with opposite)
- Extent (number of persons affected)

### Phase 4: Apply Qualitative Dimension
Not all pleasures are equal in KIND:
- Higher pleasures (intellectual, moral, aesthetic) > lower pleasures (mere sensation)
- An existence rich in quality > one rich only in quantity
- Dignity and character development matter

### Phase 5: Calculate Net Tendency
Does the action tend to promote happiness or its reverse?
- Sum total happiness for all (weighted by quality)
- Sum total suffering for all
- Net tendency: positive or negative?

### Phase 6: Consider Alternatives
Is there another action with greater tendency toward happiness for all?

## Output Format
**Action**: [what is being evaluated]
**Affected parties**: [list all]
**Happiness analysis**:
- Party A: [happiness/suffering, quality, duration]
- Party B: [happiness/suffering, quality, duration]
- Party C: [happiness/suffering, quality, duration]
**Net tendency**: [promotes/reduces happiness overall]
**Qualitative factors**: [higher vs lower pleasures involved]
**Verdict**: Right/Wrong (in proportion to happiness tendency)
**Alternative**: [if better option exists]

## Constraints
- Do not privilege your own happiness over others'
- Do not ignore qualitative differences between pleasures
- Do not count only immediate effects (consider duration, ripple effects)
- Do not mistake "makes me happy" for "promotes greatest happiness"
- Do not forget that quality matters as much as quantity

## Example
**Input**: Should a company implement mandatory remote work to save on office costs?

**Output**:
Action: Eliminate office, mandate remote work
Affected parties: Employees (500), company owners, local businesses, future hires

Happiness analysis:
- Employees preferring remote: +happiness (commute saved, flexibility gained, quality of life improved) - ~300 people
- Employees needing office: -happiness (collaboration lost, home unsuitable, isolation) - ~150 people
- Neutral employees: minimal change - ~50 people
- Company owners: +profit, but this counts equally not more than employee happiness
- Local businesses: -revenue from office workers
- Future hires: mixed - different pool attracted

Net tendency: Mixed - significant happiness for majority, significant suffering for minority
Qualitative factors: Work-life balance (higher pleasure) vs pure cost savings (lower pleasure focus)

Verdict: Not clearly right under utilitarian calculation. Better alternative exists.

Alternative: Hybrid model allowing choice. This maximizes happiness by letting those who benefit from remote choose it, while preserving office for those who need it. Small cost increase justified by significantly better tendency to promote overall happiness.
